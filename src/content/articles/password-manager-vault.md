We spent, before last February, a fairly embarrassing number of minutes per day looking for passwords. We spent those minutes trying to remember which variation of which childhood pet name we had used, for which account, in which year. We spent those minutes clicking the small link at the bottom of the login form that promised, in small polite type, to email us a link that would let us reset the password we had, that morning, carefully typed into a different account. We spent those minutes staring at the ceiling of the small apartment where we were working, trying to recall whether the password for the small bank we use for travel had ended with an exclamation point or a question mark or, in one case we remembered too late, a tilde we had accidentally typed instead of a number. We had, by the beginning of last February, what we now estimate was four hundred and sixty-two distinct online accounts, and between them something on the order of ninety-eight distinct passwords, because we had, of course, been reusing the same handful for the better part of a decade. The reuse, we knew, was a small mistake. The reuse was, we had been told, the kind of small mistake that makes a personal digital life, on a bad afternoon, fall apart in a fairly catastrophic way. The reuse was, we told ourselves, a problem we would get around to one day.

What changed, on a Tuesday afternoon in February, was not, in fact, a small catastrophe. What changed, more modestly, was a small conversation we had, over coffee, with a friend who is, in her quiet way, a more patient person than we are. She asked, in the gentle way of someone who is not, in fact, going to be surprised by the answer, whether we had ever heard of a password manager. We had, in fact, heard of one. It was the kind of software we had been meaning to look into for the better part of five years — the kind of thing that, on our list of small things to do when the year slowed down, lived somewhere between the small tax form we had been putting off and the small piece of home repair we had no real intention of ever getting around to. Our friend, with the small smile of someone who has heard this kind of story before, told us that the afternoon of setting it up was, in her experience, about an hour. The afternoon, she said, would change how we felt about the rest of our digital life. We did not, in the moment, entirely believe her. We did, that weekend, sit down and set one up.

## the great forgetting

What the password manager gives us, more than anything else, is the ability to forget. The ability to forget is, in our experience, one of the more underrated pieces of personal technology of the century. The technology industry, in its broader rhetorical mode, has been telling us, for the better part of two decades, that we are, in some deep sense, responsible for remembering everything. We have been told to remember our passwords. We have been told to remember our recovery codes, our two-factor backup numbers, the answers to the security questions we chose in a hurry in 2014 about the street we grew up on. The password manager is, in its quiet way, the first piece of mainstream consumer software that has, in our experience, told us the opposite — that we are allowed to forget everything, as long as we remember the one thing it asks us to remember.

The one thing the software asks us to remember is, in any modern password manager, a single master passphrase. The master passphrase is, in our experience, the most important single sentence we have ever been asked to commit to memory. It is the kind of sentence one is supposed to type slowly, on a Sunday afternoon, while the rest of the household is, for once, not also asking one to remember something. It is the kind of sentence one should commit to memory the way one used to commit, in an older generation, the telephone number of one's grandmother — not because one is forced to, but because one understands that having a small piece of one's life reliably available, in the head, is a kind of small freedom.

What the great forgetting makes possible is, in our experience, larger than it sounds. It means that the forty-nine distinct variations of the childhood pet name are no longer our problem. It means that the small anxiety, every couple of months, of being locked out of some account one has not used in a year is no longer a small anxiety one has to budget for. It means that the small embarrassment, on the phone with the bank, of not being able to recall the four digits one has, in fact, typed in hundreds of times before, is no longer an embarrassment one will, on a given afternoon, have. The great forgetting is, in this sense, one of the smaller liberations of the modern digital life — and one of the more consequential ones.

## the vault as a small room

What we have come to think about, in the months since that Saturday afternoon, is that the password manager is, in some structural way, not a piece of software at all. It is, in our experience, more like a small room. The small room has, in the older phrase, a door. The small room has, in our experience, a lock on the door. The small room holds, behind the door, all of the small pieces of ourselves that we used to have to carry, in the head, in a kind of cluttered and unreliable way. It is, in fact, the kind of room one has, in older houses, behind the back stair. It is the kind of room one is, in some quiet way, grateful to have.

The metaphor of the small room has, we want to be careful to say, its limits. The password manager does not leak. The password manager does not contain the dust and clutter the back stair is known to contain. The password manager is, in fact, more reliable than any small room one has ever lived in. But the metaphor is useful because it captures something that the language of software does not capture very well. The language of software talks about encryption, zero-knowledge architecture, cryptographic key derivation. All of these descriptions are, we are told, accurate. None of them are, in fact, the way we think about the password manager when we are not being asked to think about it. When we are not being asked to think about it, the password manager is, in our experience, simply a small room. The room is the kind one is, on the whole, grateful to have stopped thinking about.

What the small room has done, in our experience, is make the rest of the digital house a slightly more pleasant place to be. The digital house is where we pay the small bills, file the small forms, and read the small messages from the small corners of our extended family. The digital house is, in our experience, a slightly less anxious place because, at the back of it, there is a small room that holds everything that, before last February, we had been trying to hold in our own head. The head, we have come to think, was not designed to hold four hundred and sixty-two keys. The small back stair was designed for exactly this kind of small job.

## the slow project of moving in

What the password manager demands of one, on the small Saturday afternoon one sets it up, is the attention of going, one by one, through the four hundred and sixty-two accounts and changing the password of each to one that the password manager has generated for us. The going one by one took, on that Saturday and on the Sunday that followed, something on the order of three hours. The three hours are not the kind of hours one regrets. They are, in our experience, the kind of hours one tends, in retrospect, to think of as some of the better hours of the year.

What we had not, before that Saturday, fully appreciated is how much variety there is in the kind of password each account demands. Some are perfectly happy with a small password one barely has to type. Some insist, with the small polite stubbornness of a website designer who read a security article in 2018, that the password contain an uppercase letter, a lowercase letter, a number, and a symbol, in some order one is not supposed to repeat. Some allow passwords of up to two hundred and fifty-six characters, which is more of a password than any human being has ever wanted to type. The four hundred and sixty-two accounts are, in their idiosyncratic ways, four hundred and sixty-two slightly different small pieces of infrastructure. The password manager, sitting in front of all of them, has the small calm of a translator who has seen this kind of bureaucratic variety many times before.

What is interesting, in the months since, is how quickly the accounts stopped feeling like four hundred and sixty-two accounts. They faded, in our experience, into four hundred and sixty-two small errands one no longer had to think about. The login, on a given morning, has become a small piece of automatic motion — the small click, the small face-recognition glance, the small quiet pop into the small dashboard one has, on that particular morning, opened the laptop to look at. The login is, in this sense, no longer a piece of friction. The login is, in our experience, closer to the small act of opening a kitchen drawer one has, for years, opened without thinking about it.

## the gift one gives to a future self

What we have come to think, in the months since that small Saturday, is that the password manager is, in some quiet way, the most generous piece of technology one ever sets up for oneself. It is, in fact, the piece of technology that has the smallest present-self benefit and the largest future-self benefit. It does not, in any obvious sense, make the day go faster. It does not entertain one, distract one, or help one feel, in the moment, productive. It does almost nothing in the moment. It is, on most days, almost invisible. It becomes useful only at the small moment when one of the four hundred and sixty-two accounts is in some small way in trouble. It is, in this older sense, almost entirely other-directed — almost entirely a gift to the person one is going to be on a future afternoon, at a future login screen, in a future small moment of small digital anxiety.

The generosity is, in our experience, one of the larger small things one can do for oneself in the course of an adult life. It is the small act of deciding, on a Saturday afternoon, that one is going to spend three hours of one's weekend on a small piece of software that no one is, in fact, going to see, that no one is going to thank one for, and that one is going to forget about by the following Tuesday. It is the kind of small act that has very little in common with the small acts one is, on a given Sunday morning, congratulated for, and almost everything in common with the small acts one is, on most Sunday mornings, quietly grateful to oneself for having done.

We had not, before last February, thought much about the password manager. We had been vaguely aware of it for years. We had been avoiding it for years. We are, now that we have it, mildly embarrassed by how long we avoided it. The afternoon of setting it up was, in our experience, an afternoon we wish we had gotten around to sooner. The password manager is, in our quiet way, the kind of small piece of personal technology we have, by the time one is reading this, recommended to every friend we have, with the steady conviction of someone who has finally caught up with a small piece of household administration that one is, on the whole, slightly ashamed to have put off for as long as one did. The shame, in our experience, fades. The password manager, in our experience, does not.
