There is a particular pleasure in packing a car for a camping trip and discovering, halfway up the mountain road, that you have brought almost nothing. You have a small bag. You have a tent you have owned since you were twenty-six. You have a stove the size of a coffee mug. You have one good knife. You have the layers you are wearing and a book you probably will not read. The rest of what you need is, you realise, already out there, waiting for you, in the form of cold air, dry wood, dark water, a horizon you cannot see yet but will, by morning.

We have made camping into something it was never meant to be. We have made it into a kind of dressed-up apartment, with air mattresses and camp kitchens and folding chairs and portable espresso machines and solar panels and bluetooth speakers, all of it hauled, with great logistical effort, into a clearing in the woods so that we can sit, in the woods, almost exactly as we sat in our living rooms the night before. There is nothing wrong with any of this. Some of it is genuinely useful. Some of it is a small kindness to the people you camp with. But the original pleasure of camping, the one that drew us all out there in the first place, is not in any of these things. The original pleasure is in their absence.

What I want to talk about, then, are the small luxuries. Not the large ones, not the inflatable mattress or the camp shower, but the small ones. The good knife. The warm layer. The way to make coffee at altitude. The book that is small enough to fit in a back pocket. The little things that, together, are the entire difference between a miserable night in the woods and a night you will think about for the rest of the year.

## the knife you trust

Buy one good knife, before you buy anything else for camping. I mean this literally. Before the tent, before the stove, before the sleeping pad. Buy a knife. Spend more on it than you think you should. Carry it with you, in a leather sheath, on every trip for the rest of your life.

A good knife is the most useful object in the world, after a sharp pencil and a clean pot. With a good knife you can do almost anything: open a package, slice an apple, sharpen a stick, cut cord, repair a piece of gear, dress a small fire, prepare a meal, defend yourself from something that has wandered too close to your tent in the dark. The list is, when you sit down to write it, almost embarrassing. We have outsourced most of these small tasks to gadgets. A good knife undoes the outsourcing in a single afternoon.

The knife does not need to be large. It does not need to be expensive. It needs to be a knife you trust, which is a different thing. It needs to be a knife that fits your hand, that opens easily when you are cold and tired, that holds its edge for a few days at a time, that you would be sad to lose. There is no recipe for this. You try knives, and one of them will feel like it has always been yours. That is the one. Bring it camping. Bring it everywhere.

The reason I put the knife first is that it is the smallest possible luxury, and the most reliable. A good knife will outlive every other piece of gear you own. It will be there, in your hand, at the end of a long day, when the wood is too wet for the fire and the propane is running low. It will save you, in small ways, a hundred times, without ever asking to be thanked.

## the warm layer

There is a particular moment, around four in the morning, when the temperature drops and the body, which has been pretending it is fine all night, admits it is cold. This is the moment the warm layer is for.

The warm layer is not the parka. The parka is for the walk. The warm layer is for sitting, for standing still, for being in the dark with a friend and looking at the fire and feeling, suddenly, that the night is colder than you had banked on. The warm layer is the fleece, or the wool sweater, or the puffy you keep rolled at the bottom of your bag, the one you only put on when the weather demands it, which is most nights, even in August.

The principle here is layers. One thick layer is useless. Three thin layers are a system. The body can vent heat through three thin layers in a way it cannot vent heat through one thick layer. The system has to be tunable. You have to be able to add a layer when you stop moving. You have to be able to subtract a layer when you start moving. You have to be able to do both without thinking about it, which is why you bring the same layers every time, the ones you have worn for years, the ones that have become, without your noticing, a second skin.

Wool is, I think, the most underrated material we have. It is warm when wet. It does not hold odour. It can be washed in cold water and dried in the morning sun. It does not pill in the way synthetics do. A good wool sweater is, in its own quiet way, more luxurious than almost any high-performance jacket on the market. Buy one. Bring it on every trip. Wear it until it has more holes than sweater, and then keep wearing it.

## coffee, slowly

There is a particular pleasure in making coffee outside, in the morning, with cold hands and a small pot, while the rest of the campsite is still asleep. The pleasure is not really in the coffee. The pleasure is in the making. It is in the small ritual of boiling water, of grinding beans, of pouring slowly, of holding the warm cup in both hands and standing at the edge of the clearing, looking at the trees, which are still mostly black, with a thin line of gold along the horizon that is going to be, in twenty minutes, the morning.

You do not need much to make coffee outside. You need a small stove, or a fire, or a thermos of water you boiled the night before. You need a way to pour the water slowly. You need a cup you like the feel of. You need a few minutes of patience. The patience is the part that matters most, because the patience is what the morning is for. We have, on most mornings, almost no patience. We want the coffee, and we want it now, and we want it at the temperature we asked for, and we want it from a machine that does not require us to be involved. Coffee at altitude, in the cold, made slowly, by your own hand, returns the patience to you. It is a small luxury. It is the entire point.

I have a friend who makes his coffee with a small brass pot over a small gas flame, and he brings the same pot on every trip, and he has been doing this for twenty years. The pot is dented. The handle is wrapped in electrical tape. He refuses to replace it. He says, and I think he is right, that the pot is part of the morning, and a new pot would not know the morning. There is something to this. Objects that have been places with us, that have been in our hands in the cold and the wet and the early morning, take on a kind of loyalty. They become part of the practice. They are the small luxuries.

## the book you almost do not read

Bring a book on every camping trip. You will almost certainly not read it. This is part of the point.

The book, on a camping trip, is not really for reading. It is for holding. It is for the moment when you are sitting on a rock in the afternoon and the day is so quiet you can hear your own breathing, and you reach into your pack and pull out a book, and you open it to a random page, and you read two paragraphs, and you close it, and you look at the river again. The book is, in those moments, a small anchor. It is a way of marking the day. It is a way of saying, later, when you are back in the city, that this paragraph was read in this place, and the place is now inside the paragraph.

The book should be a real book. Not a phone. Not a tablet. A real book, with pages, that smells of paper. The smell of paper is, I think, one of the small luxuries we have lost. We have replaced books with screens, and screens with thinner screens, and we have lost the smell. The smell is small. The smell is also, somehow, everything. A real book in a backpack, on a camping trip, is a small refusal of the century. It says, gently, that there are still some things we do not need to swipe.

The book should be one you have read before. Not a new book you are planning to read. A book you already know. A book you have, somewhere in your memory, a place for. The pleasure of reading a known book in an unknown place is different from the pleasure of reading an unknown book in a known place. The known book becomes, briefly, a different book, in the new place. It has a new weather. It has a new light. It has, if you are lucky, the sound of a river nearby.

## what the small luxuries are for

The small luxuries are not, in the end, about the objects. The objects are small. The luxuries are smaller. The luxuries are, I think, the small ways we tell ourselves that we are still living, even when we have given up almost every other comfort. The knife that we trust. The wool sweater that we have worn for years. The brass coffee pot that is dented and refuses to be replaced. The book that we read for two paragraphs and then put down. These are the things that make a campsite a home, briefly, and a trip a memory, briefly, and a night in the woods a night we will think about, on a bad day in the city, for the rest of our lives.

You do not need many small luxuries. You need the right ones. You need the ones that will fit, in their small way, into your hand and your memory and your next trip, and the trip after that. The most successful camping kit is not the biggest one. It is the one that has been pared down, slowly, over years, to the things you actually use, the things you actually trust, the things that have become, without your noticing, part of you.

This is, I think, the real argument for simplicity. Not the moral argument, although there is one. Not the environmental argument, although there is one too. The real argument is that simplicity, when it works, is the most luxurious thing in the world. The fewer things you have, the more each thing matters. The fewer luxuries you have, the more each luxury means. A morning coffee made with cold hands, in a brass pot, on a hillside, by a person who packed nothing, is more luxurious, in the older sense of the word, than almost any meal at almost any restaurant in almost any city in the world.

Bring less. Bring better. Bring the knife, and the layer, and the pot, and the book. The rest will come.
