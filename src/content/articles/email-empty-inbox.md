We had, in our working life, before the spring of two years ago, an inbox we did not, in fact, look at before lunch. We had an inbox we did not look at before the second cup of coffee. We had an inbox we had made a small private agreement with ourselves not to look at on weekends, and that agreement had, in the years we had maintained it, been one of the small better decisions we had made in the running of our working life. We had an inbox that contained, on most mornings, something on the order of four hundred and twenty unread messages, and another two thousand or so that we had read at some point in the previous decade and marked as read without doing anything further with them. We had, before that spring, an inbox we did not, in fact, want to open, and which we had, in the kind of small quiet way one does not want to admit to, been avoiding for the better part of three years.

What changed, on a Wednesday afternoon in that spring, was a fairly small conversation with a friend who had recently become a person who kept her inbox at zero. The friend told us, with the small polite insistence of someone who has recently been through a small conversion, that the empty inbox was, in her experience, not a piece of productivity theater. It was the small piece of digital housekeeping that had changed the texture of her week. The empty inbox was, she said, the kind of small piece of digital architecture one does not fully appreciate until one has it. We were skeptical, in the polite way one is skeptical of a friend's small enthusiasm. We were also mildly curious. We were, by the end of the following week, in the kind of small committed way one commits to a small household project, in the middle of our own attempt to get to zero.

We did not, in fact, get to zero that week. We did, however, get to zero, for the first time in nearly a decade, by the end of the following month. We did, in the months since, keep the inbox at zero for an entire week, several entire weeks in a row, and eventually, in the kind of small loose way one keeps the small pieces of digital housekeeping, for an entire month at a time. The empty inbox has, in our experience, changed the texture of our week. We had not, before that small Wednesday, fully believed our friend. We had, in the years since that small Wednesday, become, on this particular point, the slightly embarrassed kind of small evangelist she had, in fact, been that afternoon.

## the inbox as a small room

The inbox is, in our experience, the kind of small room one does not, in fact, realize one is living in until one cleans it. The inbox is the small room into which, on most working days, something like twenty or forty messages arrive, and from which, on most working days, something like two or three messages leave. The inbox is the small room in which the small undifferentiated mass of one's working correspondence accumulates, in the kind of small quiet way one does not notice, until it has, in fact, become two thousand messages one has been meaning, for several years, to do something about. The inbox is, in this older sense, the small room of the working life — and, in our experience, the most underrated.

The metaphor of the small room is, in our experience, useful for a particular reason. The metaphor captures, in a way the language of email software does not, the small fact of how one actually, on most mornings, feels about one's inbox. The small room has a door. The small room has, on most mornings, a small stack of papers one has, in fact, been avoiding. The small room has, on most mornings, a small piece of mail one has been meaning to answer for several weeks. The small room is, in this older sense, the small room one does not like to walk into in the morning — and which one has, on most mornings, been walking into anyway.

What we learned, in the small weeks of getting to zero, is that the small weight is not, in our experience, a feature of the correspondence one has been receiving. The small weight is, in fact, a feature of the accumulation. The small weight is what the two thousand small pieces of unread paper do, in the small quiet of the inbox, to the small room. The small weight is, in our experience, a small piece of small digital archaeology — the small residue of every small week one has been meaning, for several years, to come back to. The small weight disappears, in our experience, the moment one does the small work of cleaning the small room. The small room, once cleaned, is a different small room — the kind one is, on most mornings, quite glad to walk into.

## the small discipline of triage

What the empty inbox requires of one, on most mornings, is a small piece of small discipline. The small discipline is, in our experience, the discipline of triage. The triage is the small piece of small work one does, on most mornings, in the small quiet of the first half hour of the working day, in which one opens every small message, and one decides what one is going to do with it. The triage is a small piece of small work that, once one has gotten into the small habit of it, takes, on most mornings, the small amount of time one might, in an older generation, have spent reading the morning paper. The triage is the kind of small piece of small work that has, in fact, made the rest of the small working day a slightly less anxious place to be.

The triage has, in our experience, three small moves. The first small move is replying, on the spot, to the small messages one can reply to in less than two minutes. The first small move is the small move that the rest of the small discipline is built on top of. The first small move clears, on most mornings, something like a third of the small unread inbox. The second small move is forwarding, on the spot, to someone else, the small messages that are not for one. The second small move clears, on most mornings, another quarter. The third small move is deciding, on the spot, to do something with the remaining messages — to file them, or to put them on the small list of small things one is going to do later in the week, or to delete them, on the small quiet basis that one is never going to do anything with them. The third small move completes the triage.

What is interesting about the small discipline is, in our experience, that it does not, in fact, take very long. The small discipline takes, on most mornings, somewhere between fifteen and forty minutes. The small discipline is, in this older sense, the kind of small piece of small work one is, in fact, more than willing to do. The small discipline is the kind of small piece of small work that pays, in our experience, for itself, in the kind of small relief one is, in fact, able to feel, on most mornings, for the rest of the small working day.

## the small reward of nothing

What we did not, in fact, expect, in the months of having an empty inbox, is the small piece of small pleasure one gets, on most mornings, from the empty inbox itself. The empty inbox is, in our experience, a small piece of small visual pleasure. The empty inbox is the kind of small satisfaction one used to feel, in an older generation, in walking into a small kitchen one has cleaned the night before. The empty inbox is the kind of small satisfaction one used to feel in the small quiet of a small drawer one has, in fact, finally organized. The empty inbox is the kind of small pleasure one did not, in fact, realize one was missing, until one had it. The empty inbox is, in this older sense, the small reward of the small discipline — and a small reward that is, in our experience, more than the small discipline is worth.

What the empty inbox has, in our experience, is the small particular quality of being, in fact, the small visual reminder of a small piece of small work one has done. The empty inbox is the kind of small artifact that one does not, in fact, look at every five minutes. The empty inbox is the kind of small artifact one looks at once, on most mornings, and then, in the small quiet of the rest of the small working day, does not look at again. The empty inbox is the kind of small background reassurance one used, in an older generation, to get from the small calendar on the small wall of the small office one was working in. The small wall calendar told one, on most mornings, what one was, in fact, supposed to be doing. The empty inbox tells one, on most mornings, what one has, in fact, already done.

The empty inbox has, in our experience, another small piece of small pleasure that we had not expected. The empty inbox is the small piece of small digital infrastructure one has cleared that does not require, in the small quiet of a small afternoon several months from now, the small piece of small digital archaeology one would, in fact, otherwise have to do. The empty inbox is the small piece of small digital housekeeping that pays, in our experience, for itself not just in the small relief of the small morning, but in the small relief of every small morning of the small working year. The empty inbox is, in this older sense, one of the better small pieces of small infrastructure one can, in fact, build, on a Wednesday afternoon, in the small quiet of one's working life.

## the inbox as a small piece of furniture

What we have come to think, in the months of keeping the inbox at zero, is that the inbox has, in some structural way, become a small piece of small furniture of the modern working life. The small piece of small furniture has, in our experience, the small quality of being, in fact, more or less invisible. The small piece of small furniture has, in our experience, the small quality of being taken for granted, in the kind of small quiet way one takes, in fact, the small hot water heater of a small apartment for granted. The small piece of small furniture is, in this older sense, the kind of small piece of small architecture one does not, in fact, notice, on most mornings, precisely because one does not have to notice it. The small piece of small furniture has, in the small quiet of the small working week, become a small part of one's small working life.

We had not, before that small Wednesday afternoon two years ago, thought of the inbox as a small piece of small furniture. We had not thought of the inbox as anything more than the small unpleasant pile of small paper that one has been meaning to deal with for several years. We had not thought of the inbox as a small place one is, in some quiet way, supposed to keep tidy. We had not thought of the inbox as a small piece of small architecture one is, in fact, supposed to maintain, in the kind of small steady small way one maintains, in fact, the small kitchen counter, the small shelf in the small hallway. The inbox is, in this older sense, a small piece of small furniture. The small piece of small furniture is, in our experience, the kind one is, on most mornings, quite glad one has.

The empty inbox, in the end, is not the kind of small piece of small personal technology one is going to talk about at a dinner party. The empty inbox is the kind of small piece of small personal technology one does not, in fact, talk about at all. The empty inbox is the kind of small piece of small personal architecture one is, in this older sense, going to quietly keep, for oneself, in the small quiet of the small working morning, for the small rest of the small working life. The empty inbox is, in our experience, the kind of small achievement that is smaller than it sounds — and which, for that very reason, has been one of the better small things we have gotten around to.
