There is a particular moment, just before you step into cold water, when the body knows what is about to happen and the mind has not yet caught up. The water is dark, and a little opaque, and a little colder than you were expecting, although you knew, of course, what the temperature would be. You knew because you checked the lake report on your phone, in the car, twenty minutes ago. You knew because you have been here before. You knew, and you came anyway, because this is, in some way you cannot quite justify, the reason you came.

The cold water movement is, by now, a small industry. There are ice baths in the basements of a certain kind of apartment in Brooklyn. There are cold plunges in the back gardens of a certain kind of house in Los Angeles. There are podcasts, books, gurus, retreats in Finland, retreats in Iceland, retreats in the desert, all of them built around the same basic proposition, which is that cold water, applied to the body, does something to the body that nothing else quite does. The proposition is not new. The packaging is new. The discipline is older than any of us.

What I want to talk about, here, is not the packaging. I want to talk about the practice, and about what the practice does, and about why we have, in the last few years, returned to a thing humans have been doing for ten thousand years, and which we are now pretending to have invented.

## the gasp and what follows

The first thing that happens, when you step into cold water, is a gasp. The gasp is involuntary. It is a small, sharp intake of air, the chest contracting, the shoulders rising. The gasp is the body's oldest response to a sudden change in temperature. The gasp is, in evolutionary terms, it is the body's way of preparing for the worst.

The second thing that happens, after the gasp, is a kind of settling. The body, having prepared for the worst, discovers that the worst is not, in fact, arriving. The water is cold, but it is not killing the body. The body is, in fact, managing. The body has been managing, throughout the long history of our species, in water colder than this, for longer than this, without the wetsuit and the watch and the post-plunge infrared sauna. The body is more capable than the mind, almost always, in moments like this.

The third thing that happens, after the settling, is a strange clarity. The clarity is the part that is hard to describe, and it is the part that the cold water people are mostly trying to describe, badly, when they say things like it changed my life. The clarity is not, exactly, euphoria. It is more like the absence of the usual static. The mind, which had been busy with the morning's first worries, suddenly has nothing to do, because the body is using all of the available bandwidth, and the mind has been temporarily suspended. There is, in that suspension, a kind of quiet. The quiet is the part the cold water people are talking about.

I am not going to argue that the clarity is medically significant. I am not a doctor. The literature, as far as I have read it, suggests that cold water exposure does a number of measurable things to the body — activates the sympathetic nervous system, increases norepinephrine, briefly spikes dopamine, trains the vascular system. Some of this is probably true. Some of it is probably overstated. The medical argument is not, in any case, the argument I am interested in. The argument I am interested in is the older argument, which is the one our ancestors were making, in their own way, ten thousand years ago, when they stepped into the cold river in the morning because the river was there and the morning was there and the body wanted, for reasons it could not name, to be reminded that it was alive.

## the body, on purpose

We have, in the modern world, almost entirely removed the element of deliberate discomfort from our lives. We have central heating, and central cooling, and cars, and grocery delivery, and standing desks, and ergonomic chairs, and remote work, and a thousand other small arrangements, all of them built around the same premise, which is that comfort is the goal and discomfort is the failure. We have, in pursuit of this premise, made our lives smaller in ways we do not entirely understand.

Cold water is a small, deliberate return to discomfort. It is not, in most cases, a large discomfort. The water is not, in most cases, freezing. It is, in most cases, twelve or fourteen or sixteen degrees, which is cold, but not dangerous. The discomfort is not heroic. The discomfort is small. The smallness is the point. The smallness is what makes the practice possible. You can step, almost every morning, into a cold water, without much preparation, without much equipment, without much time. The cold water does not ask for your weekend. The cold water asks for ten minutes, in the morning, before the day has started.

What the cold water gives you, in exchange, is a kind of remembering. You remember, when you step into cold water, that you are a body. You remember that the body has opinions. You remember that the body has been carrying you around, all this time, with very little thanks. You remember that the body is more capable than the mind, in most moments, of handling what is in front of it. You remember, briefly, that you are an animal. The animal is older than the person. The animal has been around, in various forms, for a very long time. The animal has survived winters colder than this, and mornings darker than this, and rivers wider than this, without any of the modern infrastructure that we have, in our wisdom, arranged around our days. The animal is fine. The animal is, in fact, more than fine.

This is, I think, the part of cold water that the wellness industry does not understand, and the part that matters most. The wellness industry thinks cold water is a tool. The wellness industry thinks cold water is a way of improving your sleep, or your mood, or your immune system, or your athletic performance. The wellness industry is partly right. The wellness industry is also, mostly, missing the point. Cold water is not a tool. Cold water is a teacher. It is a way of being reminded, briefly, that you are a body, that the body is on your side, that the body has, in fact, been on your side, all along, without you ever quite noticing.

## the practice, not the performance

There is a temptation, with cold water, to make it into a performance. The temptation is real. The temptation is, also, almost entirely the point at which the practice becomes something else. The performance is a picture of yourself in cold water, holding a yoga block above your head, with a caption about discipline. The practice is you, alone, in cold water, in the dark, with no audience at all.

The practice is older than the performance. The practice is what humans have been doing, in cold water, for ten thousand years, before there were cameras. The practice has no metric. The practice has no hashtag. There is just you, and the water, and the body, and the moment. The moment is enough. The moment is, in fact, more than enough. The moment is, in most cases, all you need.

I have a friend who swims, almost every morning, in a lake that is, in summer, around sixteen degrees, and in winter, around four. He has been doing this for years. He does not have an instagram. He has not, as far as I can tell, written a book about it. He does not, in conversation, recommend it. He does, when asked, simply say that he swims, every morning, in the lake. That is the entire testimony. That is, I think, the right kind of advice. The right kind of advice is the kind that does not need to convince you. The right kind of advice simply states what it is, and lets you decide.

If you are going to try cold water, try it simply. Do not buy the chiller. Do not buy the watch. Do not measure the time. Do not, especially, do not measure the time in front of a camera. Find a lake, or a river, or the ocean, or, if you must, a bathtub with a bag of ice. Step in, slowly, when no one is watching. Notice the gasp. Notice the clarity. Step out. Let the body warm itself. Repeat, the next morning, and the morning after. That is the practice. That is the entire practice.

## the cold, and the rest of the day

What I notice, on the mornings I have swum in cold water, is that the day has a different quality afterward. The day is not better, exactly. The day is more awake. The day has, in fact, been woken up, in a way that coffee, alone, cannot quite manage. Coffee wakes up the mind. Cold water wakes up the body. The mind, on a normal morning, has been awake for an hour before the body. The body, on a morning with cold water, is awake at the same time as the mind, or a little before. The day, as a result, has a different alignment. The day has, in fact, been put together properly, by the body and the mind together, before the day has really started.

I have come to think of this, in my own quiet way, as a kind of alignment. The alignment is not the point. The cold water is not the point. The body is the point, and the body has been waiting, for most of my life, for me to ask it what it actually wanted, instead of telling it what was good for it. The body wanted, apparently, to swim, in the cold, in the morning, in a lake that I have known since I was a child. The body wanted, apparently, to be cold, briefly, in order to be warm, afterward, in a way that is not just the absence of cold but the presence of something closer to gratitude.

There is no conclusion here. There is no metric. There is no app. There is just the cold water, and the body, and the morning, and the strange small quiet that comes, for ten minutes, when the body has been reminded of itself, and the mind has been reminded of the body, and the rest of the day has been given, without anyone quite understanding why, a different kind of permission to begin.
