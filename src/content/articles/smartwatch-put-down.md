We bought the watch on a Tuesday, the way one buys a thing one has been told is good for you. The salesperson at the small electronics shop was patient, walked us through the bands, the faces, the rings of glowing complications, and we left the store with a modest white box and the quiet sense that we had made an adult decision. The smartwatch, like most wearables, was sold to us as a tool for paying attention — to our steps, our heart, our sleep, our breathing, our standing posture, our skin temperature, the length of our walks, the elevation of our hikes, the average number of times we had stood up between breakfast and lunch. It was sold to us as a way of *becoming more aware* of the body in which we live.

What it actually delivered, over the following months, was a new way of being interrupted. The phone had already trained us to glance at a rectangle several times an hour; the watch moved the rectangle to the wrist, which is the part of the body most likely to be looked at for no reason. We had not, before the watch, had a habit of staring at our wrists. We have one now.

## the anatomy of a wrist tap

The vibration is, of course, the entire point. The marketing teams who design these products have spent years tuning it: a gentle, two-pulse buzz that is meant to feel like a polite suggestion rather than a demand. It is not the rude scream of an old phone alarm. It is not the urgent bleep of a text. It is, in its own quiet way, an apology for being there at all — a tap, a hum, a tiny mechanical cough that says *I would not bother you, except that something has happened that I think you ought to know about*.

That something, almost always, is small. A calendar reminder. A text that could have waited. A step-count milestone congratulating us for walking to the corner and back. A breathing exercise prompt arriving at the exact moment we had finally started breathing normally. The watch has, in the dozen or so months we have owned one, never once delivered a piece of news that could not have waited an hour, or a day, or perhaps an entire lifetime.

And yet — and this is the strange thing — we have come to feel a small, quiet loss when we leave the house without it. There is something in us, something older than the technology, that has begun to associate the watch with being looked after. The wrist, suddenly naked, feels oddly uncared for. The body, suddenly unmonitored, feels somehow forgotten. This is, we have come to think, the actual product. Not the heart rate. Not the steps. The product is the feeling of being continuously *seen* by a small attentive machine, and the inverse feeling, when the machine is removed, of being unaccounted for in the world.

## the loop of reassurance

What we have noticed, in the months of wearing it, is that the watch trains a very particular appetite — an appetite for reassurance. Each glance at the wrist offers, in its own modest fashion, a small confirmation that we are still here, still moving, still on schedule, still in possession of our normal heart rate. The glance is, in its quiet way, a kind of hello from the universe. It is the digital version of the older comfort of touching a wall, or running a finger along a familiar banister — a way of reminding oneself that one is in a real place, in a real body, in a real day.

The trouble is that the appetite grows. We began, as one does, with the occasional glance. Within a few weeks, the glances had become a habit, the habit a tic, the tic a kind of background noise. Now, when we are not wearing the watch, we catch ourselves glancing at the bare wrist anyway, the way a former smoker still reaches for a pocket that no longer contains a pack. The body has learned a new reflex.

This is, on reflection, the older trick of all wearables: they substitute for the natural rhythms of attention that used to be supplied by life itself. We used to be aware of our heart rate because we were, occasionally, frightened, or excited, or winded, or moved by something. We used to be aware of our steps because we were, occasionally, walking somewhere that mattered. We used to be aware of our sleep because we were, occasionally, tired enough to notice. The watch, by offering a steady stream of these data points, has quietly replaced the occasions that used to evoke them. We no longer notice the breath because we are running for a train. We notice the breath because the watch has told us to.

## the cost of the wrist

It is worth asking, even gently, what has been lost in this exchange. Theologians of attention, from Simone Weil onward, have argued that the most important kinds of awareness are the ones that arise without prompting — the spontaneous noticing of a face across a room, the unscheduled attention to a strange sound in the garden, the gratuitous thought that arrives in the middle of a quiet afternoon and changes the shape of one's life. These are the forms of attention that cannot, by definition, be scheduled. They happen, when they happen, because the mind is empty enough to receive them.

The smartwatch, like the phone before it, is in the business of preventing that emptiness. It is, by design, a steady little drip of small demands, each one too minor to refuse but each one sufficient to keep the foreground of attention occupied. The result is that we are, by the end of a typical day, no longer the kind of person who notices the unscheduled thing. We are the kind of person who glances at the wrist.

We are not, for the record, against the technology. Some of our friends have genuine medical reasons for wearing one. Some of them find the gentle accountability useful in the early weeks of a new exercise habit. Some of them, frankly, just like the way it looks. We are, ourselves, a little fond of the small map that lights up when we raise the wrist in a strange city. None of these reasons are silly. We are, however, against the default. We are against the assumption that one ought to wear one. We are against the suggestion that the unmonitored body is somehow, by virtue of being unmonitored, slightly less real.

## the case for the empty wrist

So we have, for the last few weeks, been experimenting with not wearing it. We leave it on the nightstand when we go for our morning walk. We leave it on the bathroom shelf when we sit down with the paper. We leave it at home, entirely, when we go to dinner with old friends we have not seen in a while. The first few days felt, we will admit, slightly odd — like arriving at a party without having put on a proper shirt. The wrist felt exposed. The day felt unvouched-for.

And then, slowly, the oddness began to thin out. The morning walks began to feel, in a way that took us some time to name, more like morning walks. The dinner conversation went further between glances. The afternoon that had previously been punctuated by tiny wrist-taps became, in their absence, simply an afternoon — a long unbroken stretch of time during which, it turned out, rather a lot happened. We remembered things we had forgotten we knew. We noticed the weather. We noticed each other.

This is not, we want to be clear, a manifesto against wearable technology. It is, at most, a modest suggestion. There are hours of the day, perhaps most of them, when the unmeasured body is the more interesting one. The walk that does not check its heart rate is the walk that occasionally surprises us with a hill, or a sudden thought, or the sight of an unfamiliar bird in a tree we have passed a hundred times. The dinner that does not count its minutes is the dinner that occasionally runs long, in the pleasant way that dinners used to, before we learned to schedule them into a calendar that had no room for surprise.

The wrist tap is, in the end, the polite version of every other notification. It is the small quiet voice of a piece of software that wants, very gently, to make sure we are still paying attention to it. The interesting question, we have come to think, is what we might do with the attention it is so politely relieving us of.
