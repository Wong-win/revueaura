The jar is the smallest on the bathroom shelf. It sits behind the cleanser and the serum and the heavier night cream, near the back, where the light from the window does not quite reach. The lid is small. The opening is small. The amount you are supposed to use is, by any measure, almost nothing - the size of a single grain of rice, applied with the ring finger, dotted along the orbital bone in a half-circle that takes, in total, less than a minute. It is, in the larger economy of the bathroom shelf, an almost negligible object. And yet, of all the products on that shelf, the eye cream is the one that has been there longest. It is the one you replace without thinking. It is the one that, if you stopped using it, you would notice within a week.

There is a particular relationship a person has with her eye cream. It is not, in the way the other products are, a relationship of transformation. The eye cream does not, in any visible sense, change anything. It does not plump. It does not lift. It does not, despite the occasional promise of the bottle, undo a year. What it does is more modest, and more durable, and it is the reason the jar has been on the shelf, unremarkably, since you were twenty-eight. It is the smallest jar on the shelf. It is also the one you reach for longest.

## The first time you bought it

There is, in most lives, a particular afternoon when the eye cream first appears. It is rarely before twenty-five. It is rarely after thirty-five. It happens in a department store, or in a pharmacy, or in the small bathroom of a friend whose shelves you have been quietly studying. It happens because, on some Tuesday in spring, you looked in the mirror and noticed, for the first time, that the skin directly under your eye was not quite the skin it had been a year ago. It was, perhaps, slightly thinner. It was, in certain lights, slightly darker. It was, in the mornings, slower to make up its mind about how it wanted to look.

This noticing is, almost always, the first in a long series of small noticeings. The skin around the eye is the thinnest on the face. It has, under it, almost no fat and almost no muscle. It is, in the literal sense of the word, fragile. It is also the part of the face that moves the most - it smiles, it squints, it expresses, with every hour, the entire range of what we feel about being alive. By the time we are twenty-eight, this skin has been doing its job for nearly a decade. It is tired. It is not broken. But it is tired.

The first eye cream is, in this sense, the first acknowledgment that the face is not going to take care of itself. It is a small, almost private act of concession - the concession that the body we have been living in, all this time, is not immortal. The jar is small because the concession is small. The eye cream is a promise, made at twenty-eight, that we will, from now on, pay a certain kind of attention to the part of us that is most visible and most vulnerable.

## The ritual at the basin

The application of eye cream is its own small ceremony, and it is worth slowing down to look at it. The face has just been washed. The skin is slightly damp. The other products have been put on - the serum, perhaps, or the oil, or the heavier cream that lives in the larger jar. The eye cream is the last thing. It comes out of the smallest jar, in a quantity so small it is almost embarrassing to mention. It sits on the tip of the ring finger like a small pearl.

The ring finger is, you will have been told, the right finger for this. It is the weakest of the fingers, and the least able to press too hard on the thin skin under the eye. This is one of the small pieces of beauty knowledge that turns out, on inspection, to be exactly right. The pressure is, in this area, the thing that matters. The eye cream does its work, whatever that work is, in the absence of pressure. The finger that applies it should, therefore, be the finger that cannot press. The hand, in this small gesture, becomes gentle in a way it has not been during the rest of the routine.

The dots are placed along the orbital bone - the bone that runs from the inner corner of the eye, beneath the lower lashes, to the outer corner, and then up toward the brow. The cream is then tapped, lightly, into the skin. Not rubbed. Tapped. The tapping is the gesture. The tapping is the small piece of attention the skin around the eye has been waiting for. The tapping takes less than a minute. It is, in most lives, the quietest minute of the morning.

## What the cream is actually doing

The honest answer, for almost every eye cream on the market, is that the cream is doing very little, in the immediate sense. It is moisturizing the skin, in the same way any moisturizer moisturizes the skin. It is sitting there, beneath the rest of the routine, doing the small work that moisturizers do. It is not, in most cases, doing anything more dramatic than that.

And yet, the eye cream matters. It matters because the skin under the eye is, as we have said, the thinnest on the face. It is the first part of the face to show dehydration. It is the first part of the face to register a bad week. It is the first part of the face to look, on a Monday morning, like the weekend was longer than it actually was. The eye cream is, in this sense, less a treatment than a recognition. It is the recognition that the part of the face most likely to give us away is also the part that most needs, on most mornings, a quiet kind of attention.

There is also, perhaps, something more interesting going on. The act of applying the cream is, as we have said, its own small piece of time. It is the moment, in the morning, when we are not rushing out the door but are, briefly, paying attention to the part of the face that is most expressive. We are, in this small gesture, acknowledging that the eyes - the part of us that meets the world first, that other people look at before they look at anything else - deserve a small portion of the morning. The cream is not what is doing the work. The attention is what is doing the work. The cream is, in this sense, the small permission we give ourselves to pay attention.

## The jar that travels

The eye cream is, of all the products in the routine, the one that travels most often. The cleanser stays at home, mostly because the cleanser belongs to the basin and the basin does not move. The serum, in many lives, also stays at home. The eye cream goes. The eye cream goes in the small bag that goes to the hotel. The eye cream goes in the overnight case that goes to the parents' house for the weekend. The eye cream goes, in the smallest possible tube, in the carry-on that goes on the longer trips.

This is, perhaps, because the eye cream is, of all the products, the most replaceable, in the sense that its absence is the most noticeable. We can, in most cases, skip the serum for a week without the skin particularly minding. We can, in an emergency, use a different moisturizer than usual. The eye cream is different. When we skip the eye cream, the skin around the eye notices within a day or two. The skin around the eye, by Wednesday, looks slightly more tired than it ought to. By Friday, it looks like the week we have just had. By Sunday, we are digging, in the unfamiliar bathroom, for the small tube we forgot to pack.

This is also why the eye cream is, almost always, the first product we replace when it runs out. The serum can wait a few days. The cleanser can be substituted. The eye cream cannot. The eye cream is the one product that has, in the quiet language of the bathroom shelf, become non-negotiable. We may not be able to articulate what it does. We know, however, what its absence does. We know what our face looks like without it. We know, in this sense, that the jar, small as it is, has become part of how the face is held together.

## The promise, kept

There is a reason the title of this small piece is the way it is. The eye cream is, in some quiet way, a promise. It is a promise you made to yourself, at twenty-eight, on the afternoon you first noticed the skin under your eye and decided that the noticing required a response. You went out, that afternoon, and bought a small jar. You brought it home. You placed it on the shelf. You have, with varying degrees of attention, been using it, more or less, ever since.

The promise was not dramatic. It was not, in the language of the beauty industry, transformative. It was, instead, the kind of promise most of us make without realizing we are making it - the promise to pay attention, in a small and regular way, to the part of ourselves that is most visible and most fragile. The promise to put, on most mornings, a small pearl of cream on the tip of the ring finger, and to tap it into the skin, in the same small half-circle, in the same small order, while the rest of the bathroom routine happens around it.

The promise has been kept, mostly, because the jar is small and the gesture is small and the cost of keeping it is small. There is no exercise involved. There is no discipline. There is only the small daily act of taking a tiny amount of cream out of a tiny jar and putting it on the place where the face is most likely to forget, and most likely to forgive. The face, in the years since you first made the promise, has not become younger. The face has, however, become better cared for. The face has, in this sense, kept up its end of the bargain.

The jar is still on the shelf. It is smaller than the other jars. It is, in the corner, near the back. But it is the jar you reach for, on most mornings, before you reach for anything else. It is the jar that has been there, through the moves and the breakups and the new jobs. It is the jar that has been there longer than some of the people in your life. And it is, in this quiet and unremarkable way, the jar that has done the most.
