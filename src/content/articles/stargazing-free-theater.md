There is a moment, somewhere around midnight, when the house has finally gone quiet and the dog has finally stopped asking to be let out, and you are, for no particular reason, standing in the back garden, in your socks, looking up. The roofline of the house is in front of you, black against a slightly less black sky. A plane is crossing, very slowly, with its red light blinking. The wind has dropped. The neighbour's security lamp has, mercifully, gone off. And above all of this, very quietly, the stars are there. They have been there all evening. They have been there all your life. But you have not, in any practical sense, looked at them in months. And now, on a Tuesday in October, in your socks, in the back garden, you are looking at them. And they are, as they always have been, completely extraordinary.

We want to argue, gently, for the stars. Not in the grand way. Not in the astronomical way. We want to argue for the practice of stepping outside, at night, and looking up. We want to argue for it because it is, in our experience, the only entertainment that is still, after a long period of every other entertainment getting worse, completely free. The stars have not raised their price. The stars have not introduced a premium tier. The stars have not, as far as we know, begun selling advertising around the moon. The show is free. The show is the only show that has been free, on this particular patch of earth, since long before there was a show to charge for.

This is what we mean by free theater. The sky, above the roofline, is a theater. The theater has been running, every night, for several billion years. The theater does not require a ticket, although it does, like all good theaters, require the audience to turn up on time, which in this case means at night, in the dark, when the audience is, ideally, slightly underdressed for the occasion. The theater is the most generous cultural institution in your life, and you have, almost certainly, been ignoring it for years.

## what the dark does for you

The first thing the night sky does, when you step outside, is what the dark does to a body that has been indoors for too long. The dark quiets the body. The dark quiets, also, the small, busy apparatus of seeing — the screens, the lit rooms, the streetlamps, the small blinking lights on the kitchen counter that say, in their quiet way, that everything in the house is still connected to the internet. The dark turns off, gently and without asking, the part of you that has been processing input. The dark gives you back, in exchange, the eyes themselves.

This is what we have lost, in the modern city. The dark, in any useful quantity, has become hard to find. We have, with the best of intentions, lit the night. We have lit it with streetlamps, with porch lights, with security floods, with the small blinking diodes on every device. We have made the night, in most of our neighbourhoods, an extension of the day, and the day, in most of our lives, is already too much. The dark has become a small luxury, and a thing we almost never enjoy.

The night sky is the test of whether the dark is still doing its work. If you can see the Milky Way, faintly, from your back garden, the dark is doing its work. If you can see only a handful of the brighter stars, the lights are winning. We are not, in this column, going to argue for turning off the lights. We are going to argue for driving, once or twice a year, for forty minutes, to the nearest bit of dark, and standing in it, and looking up. The dark is, in fact, one of the few things that is worth the drive.

## the constellations, and how slowly they change

There is a particular pleasure, when you look up at the sky for more than thirty seconds, in beginning to recognise the constellations. The pleasure is not in any deep astronomical sense. The pleasure is the small, deeply human pleasure of recognising a thing you have not seen in a while. The pleasure is, in some deep way, the same pleasure as recognising a friend in a crowd — the small lift, the small internal nod, the slight feeling of being at home in a place you have not been in a long time.

You do not need to learn the whole sky. You do not need, in any practical sense, to memorise the celestial map, or to be able to point out, with confidence, the difference between Cassiopeia and Cepheus, although the difference between Cassiopeia and Cepheus is worth knowing. You need, in our experience, only two or three anchors — one in the winter sky, one in the summer sky, one that is always there, somewhere above the roofline, no matter what the season. Once you have the anchors, the sky becomes, briefly, a place you know. Once you have the anchors, the sky becomes, in the slow way of all places you know, a place you can return to.

The constellations, also, change slowly. We mean this in two senses. The constellations change, slowly, across the year, in the sense that Orion is a winter constellation, and Cygnus is a summer constellation, and the slow parade of them across the sky is one of the older calendars we have. The constellations also change, slowly, across the centuries, in the sense that the stars themselves are moving, on timescales so long that no human life will see them move by any visible amount. The constellations are, in this sense, the slowest thing in your life. They have been, more or less, the same constellations since the day your grandmother looked up at them, and the day her grandmother looked up at them, and the day her grandmother's grandmother looked up at them. The constellations are, in this sense, the most reliable thing you will ever look at. The constellations are the only subscription in your life that has not, in any visible way, changed since you signed up for it.

## the telescope, and how it is optional

There is, of course, a version of stargazing that involves a telescope. The telescope is a beautiful object. The telescope is, in our experience, the most rewarding piece of equipment a curious adult can own, with the possible exception of a good bread knife. The telescope brings the sky, in a way the naked eye cannot, into focus. The telescope turns the moon, in particular, from a flat disc into a place — a place with mountains, and craters, and shadows, and a horizon you can almost imagine standing on.

We want, however, to argue gently against the idea that the telescope is required. The telescope is, in fact, the second step in the evening, not the first. The first step, and the step that has been doing its work for several thousand years, is the naked eye. The first step is the person, in the dark, looking up, with no equipment, with no app, with no plan. The first step is the part of the evening that has not been improved, in any meaningful way, by the introduction of instruments. The first step is, in fact, slightly diminished by the telescope, in the sense that the telescope gives you a closer view of one thing at the cost of losing the wider view, which is the view we are arguing for here.

The wider view is the view of the sky as a single thing. The wider view is the view that includes, in the same glance, the Milky Way, the seven stars of the Plough, the slow blinking of a satellite, the red eye of a plane, the silver stillness of a moon that has, without your noticing, come up over the roofline. The wider view is the view that makes you feel, briefly, the small, slightly vertiginous thing you are meant to feel when you look up. The wider view is the view that does not need a telescope, and that does not need a guidebook, and that does not need, in fact, anything except the willingness to stand outside, in the dark, for ten minutes, and look up.

The telescope, then, is for later. The telescope is for the night, a few months from now, when you have begun, slowly, to recognise the constellations, and you want, just once, to see, with your own eyes, the rings of Saturn, which is a thing we would not believe, if we had not seen it ourselves, and which we would, very gently, encourage you to see for yourself, at the earliest opportunity. Saturn, through a small telescope, on a clear night, from a back garden, is one of the few experiences that justifies, in our view, the small expense of the telescope.

## the night, as a quiet companion

The night sky is a companion. The night sky is, in fact, one of the older companions we have, and one of the most reliable, and one of the least demanding. The night sky does not require anything of you. The night sky does not require that you understand it, or photograph it, or post about it. The night sky does not require, in fact, that you do anything other than look at it, briefly, on a Tuesday in October, in your socks, in the back garden, before going back inside to do the dishes.

The night sky has, also, the rare quality of being unchanged by being looked at. The mountains, when you visit them, are slightly diminished, in some small way, by the visit. The sky, when you look at it, is exactly as it was before you looked at it, and will be exactly as it was tomorrow night. The sky is the only thing you can look at as often as you like, without it costing anything — not in money, not in carbon, not in any of the small currencies we have been worrying about, lately.

The night sky is, also, a companion in the older sense. The night sky has been, for the entire span of human history, the thing people looked at when they could not sleep. The night sky has been the thing people looked at when they were worried about someone far away. The night sky has been the thing people looked at when they wanted to feel, briefly, that they were small, and that being small was fine. The night sky is the original therapy, and the original therapy has been, for several thousand years, free, and effective, and available without appointment, every clear night, above the roofline.

## the walk back inside

The walk back inside, after ten minutes in the back garden, is a particular walk. The walk back inside is slower than the walk out. The eyes, having been in the dark, are briefly unable to find the door. The body, having stood outside in the cold, has begun to remember that it was, all along, slightly tired.

You go back inside, and you notice, suddenly, how loud the kitchen is. The fridge is humming. The phone, on the counter, has been accumulating the small urgent notifications of other people's evenings. The kitchen is, suddenly, a lot. The kitchen is, suddenly, the entire modern world, condensed into a small lit room. You stand in the kitchen for a moment, looking at the phone, and you think, briefly, about the sky.

The sky, of course, is still there. The sky is still there, above the roofline, even when you are inside. The sky will be there tonight, and tomorrow night, and the night after that. The sky will be there, in the same way, for the rest of your life, and for the rest of the lives of everyone you love. The sky is the only thing that will not, in any meaningful way, end.

Step outside tonight. Stand in the dark. Look up. The theater is free. The theater has been running, very patiently, for several billion years. It will run again tomorrow night, provided someone keeps looking up.
