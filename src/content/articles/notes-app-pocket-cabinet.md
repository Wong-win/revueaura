We have, in the small notes app that lives on the phone we carry every day, a note we wrote in March of 2017 that simply reads *coriander, not cilantro*. We have, in the same small app, a note we wrote in June of 2019 that consists of nothing but the name of a small restaurant we had been to on a Tuesday evening with a friend we have not seen since, written in the slightly shaky uppercase we were, at the time, in the habit of using when we typed under the table of a noisy bistro. We have a note that is just a single comma, which we wrote in 2021 as a small placeholder we told ourselves we would fill in with the idea we had been having, and which we have not, in fact, filled in because we have completely forgotten what the comma was for. We have a note that is three lines of a poem we are not, in fact, working on. We have a note that contains, in the kind of small dense paragraph one writes at three in the morning, a fairly elaborate set of instructions for the dream we want to remember, which is the kind of instruction one writes precisely in order to forget. We have, in the small notes app, a small archive of nearly a decade of the particular kind of half-thought, fragment, and small grocery list that one does not want to publish, and that one does not want to throw away.

What we have come to think, in the years since we started using the notes app more seriously, is that the notes app is, in some quiet way, the most personal piece of software on the phone. It is the place where, of all the apps on the phone, one is, in fact, most oneself. It is the place where one types without intending to send the typing anywhere, or be read by anyone, or be judged by anyone. It is, in this older sense, the most private room in the digital house — and, we have come to think, the most underrated.

## the messiest room in the house

The notes app, in our experience, is, in structural terms, the messiest room in the digital house. The notes app is the room one would not, in fact, show to a guest. The notes app is the room in which one finds, on a particular Sunday morning, a note one wrote in a hurry three years ago, and which one is a little startled to be reminded one wrote. The notes app is, in this older sense, the kind of room one is, in some quiet way, slightly embarrassed by — and also, in some other quiet way, slightly proud of. The notes app is the room one keeps precisely because one cannot, in fact, keep it tidy.

What the messiness of the notes app is, we have come to think, is a small piece of evidence. The messiness is, in fact, evidence of a particular kind of small permission one has given oneself. The small permission is the permission to write without intending to do anything with the writing, or to read it again, or to share it. The small permission is, in our experience, the permission that the rest of the digital house does not, in fact, give one. It is the permission that one used to give oneself, in an older generation, in the small back pages of a small notebook one kept, in the bottom of a drawer, for no particular reason. The small permission is, in this older sense, one of the more endangered kinds of small permission in the modern digital life — and one of the more valuable ones.

The messiness of the notes app is, in our experience, not a problem to be solved. The messiness is the entire point of the notes app. The notes app is the only piece of software on the phone that does not ask one to organize it. The notes app does not ask one to file things, or tag things, or sort things into folders. The notes app does not, in fact, ask anything of one, structurally, except that one put the writing in and that one occasionally, on a quiet afternoon, look back at what one has written. The notes app is, in this older sense, the most generous piece of software on the phone — and the most underrated.

## the half-thought deserves a home

What we have come to think, in the years we have been using the notes app, is that the half-thought is, in fact, the most underrated form of writing. The half-thought is the kind of thinking one has on a subway, on a Sunday morning walk, in the small quiet of a particular afternoon in which one is not, in fact, working on anything else. The half-thought is, in our experience, the kind of thinking one has in the small gap between two meetings, when one's hand is free, and one's mind is halfway between the meeting one has just left and the meeting one has not yet entered. The half-thought is the kind of thinking that one has, on most afternoons, but which one does not have a place to put. The half-thought used, in an older generation, to go into the small back pages of the small notebook, or on the back of an envelope, in pencil. The half-thought has, in our generation, very few places to go. The notes app is one of the last remaining small public places the half-thought has to land.

What the notes app gives the half-thought, more than anything else, is a place to land that is, in fact, not also a place to be judged. The half-thought is, by its small nature, the kind of thinking one does not want to broadcast, or send to anyone, or turn into a memo. The half-thought is, in this older sense, the kind of thinking that wants, more than anything else, to be set down. It is the kind of thinking that does not want to be developed, or revised, or posted, or exported, or threaded, or made into anything. The half-thought just wants, in our experience, to be quietly written down, somewhere, in a place one is going to be able to ignore it. The notes app is, in our experience, the small place the half-thought has been looking for, for several decades.

What is interesting, in our experience, is that the half-thought has, over the years, turned out to be more useful than we had expected. The half-thought has occasionally, on a quiet afternoon a year or two later, revealed itself to be the seed of something. The half-thought has occasionally, on a Sunday morning while one is making coffee, revealed itself to be the small piece of a larger idea one was, at the time, not yet ready to have. The half-thought has occasionally been the small thing that, when one finally gets around to writing the longer thing one has been wanting to write, one is very glad one wrote down. The half-thought is, in this sense, a kind of small deposit in the small bank of one's own attention. The notes app is the kind of small account one is, on the whole, very glad to have.

## the small filing cabinet

The notes app has, in our experience, an interesting relationship to the older idea of the small filing cabinet. The small filing cabinet is the older piece of small office equipment the notes app is, in some quiet way, a digital descendant of. The small filing cabinet is, in any older office, the piece of small office equipment that sits in the corner near the desk. The small filing cabinet holds, in its small drawers, the small pieces of paper one wants to keep but cannot, in fact, file properly. The small filing cabinet is the place where, in an older generation, one put the small letters one had been meaning to answer, the small receipts one had been meaning to file, the small pieces of personal correspondence that one did not want to lose but did not, in fact, want to organize.

The notes app is, in our experience, a better version of the small filing cabinet. The notes app is, in some structural way, similar to the small filing cabinet — it holds small pieces of paper, in a kind of small loose stack, in a kind of small informal order. The notes app is, in our experience, better than the small filing cabinet in several small respects. The notes app does not, in fact, take up any of the floor space of one's apartment. The notes app does not ask one, every couple of years, to dispose of a dead file. The notes app does not require one to know, in advance, what one is going to file. The notes app has, in our experience, become, for most of the people we know, the small filing cabinet of the modern adult life.

The notes app has, however, one small feature that the small filing cabinet did not have. The notes app has, in our experience, the small feature of search. The search, in the notes app, is one of the better small pieces of personal technology of the last several decades. The search is the small feature that turns the notes app from a small filing cabinet into something better than a small filing cabinet. The search is the small feature that allows one, on a quiet Sunday afternoon three years after one wrote the small note about coriander, to type *coriander* into the small search box and to find, in less than a second, the small note one had written. The search is the small feature that turns the small mess of the notes app into a small and efficient mess — a small mess one can find one's way around in. The search is, in our experience, the small piece of small software engineering that makes the entire small architecture of the notes app work.

## a quiet room one returns to

What we have come to think, in the years of using the notes app, is that the notes app is, in fact, the small piece of software one returns to. The notes app is the small piece of software one opens, on most evenings, in the small quiet of the kitchen, after the dinner has been put away, in the kind of small unfocused time one used, in an older generation, to call the small hour of the small evening. The notes app is the small piece of software one opens, on most Sunday mornings, in the kind of small unfocused time one has just before one really gets going on the day.

What the notes app is, in this older sense, is closer to the older small piece of furniture called the small writing desk than it is to anything else in the modern digital house. The small writing desk is, in our experience, the older piece of small furniture one used to keep, in one's small apartment, for the small pleasure of writing things down by hand, in the small quiet of the evening. The small writing desk is the kind of small piece of furniture one used to be slightly embarrassed about — one did not, in fact, write anything important on the small writing desk, one wrote the kind of small things one does not want to be important. The small writing desk is the small piece of small furniture one keeps, in our experience, because the small pleasure of having a small place to write, in the small quiet of the evening, is the kind of small pleasure one is, on the whole, very glad to have.

The notes app, in our experience, is the digital version of the small writing desk. The notes app is the small place one has, in the pocket, in which one can do the kind of small writing one used to do at the small writing desk. The notes app is, in this older sense, the kind of small piece of personal technology one does not, in fact, talk about very often — and which one is, in this older sense, slightly surprised to realize one is, in fact, very attached to. The notes app is, in our experience, the kind of small piece of personal software one is, in some quiet way, very glad to have access to. The notes app is the small room one returns to, on most evenings, in the small quiet of the kitchen, in the small unfocused time one is, on the whole, very glad to have.
