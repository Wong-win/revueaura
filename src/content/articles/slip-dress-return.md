There is a particular kind of dress that, when you put it on in the evening, requires almost nothing else of you. It does not ask you to choose earrings. It does not ask you to choose a shoe. It does not even ask you, really, to do anything with your hair. You put it on, and the dress does the rest. The dress is, almost by definition, the slip dress.

And it has come back. Not in the loud, logo-stamped way that fashion sometimes returns to its older shapes — not as a costume, not as an ironic revival, not as a thing to be photographed in and put down — but quietly, and almost without announcement. The slip dress has re-entered the wardrobe the way a memory re-enters a conversation: gently, and at the right moment, and without needing to defend itself.

It has re-entered because we have, perhaps for the first time in a long time, become tired of being told what to wear. We have become tired of structure that does not flatter us, of shoulder pads that were designed for someone else's shoulders, of hemlines dictated by a market that no longer quite knows what it is selling. The slip dress does not tell you anything. It asks. It asks what kind of evening you wanted, and then it agrees to give it to you.

This is a small and a rather large thing at the same time.

## A silhouette that does not argue

The slip dress is, technically, an extremely simple piece of clothing. Two rectangles of fabric, joined at the shoulder by a strap or two, falling from there to a hem that hits somewhere between the knee and the ankle. There is no waist seam. There is no dart. There is no shaping of any kind beyond what the fabric itself decides to do. The slip dress is, in the most literal sense, a piece of cloth that has agreed to hang from your shoulders and not much else.

This is the thing that people sometimes miss, when they admire a slip dress from across a room. The dress is not doing anything. The dress is *allowing* something. It is allowing the body underneath to be the body underneath. It is allowing the shoulders to be the shoulders. It is allowing the waist to be the waist, and the hip to be the hip, and the way you stand tonight to be the way you stand tonight. There is no architecture here. There is only cloth and breath.

It takes a certain kind of confidence to wear this kind of dress. Not confidence in the body — although, of course, that helps. But confidence in *the cloth*. Confidence that the dress will do what it is asked to do, which is to fall. Confidence that the wearer does not need to be persuaded into a particular shape by the cut. Confidence that the simple is not the same as the unsophisticated, and that the uncluttered is not the same as the unfinished.

This is, we think, what the slip dress is really returning to us. It is returning to us the possibility of being looked at without having been pre-shaped for the looking. It is the wardrobe's small permission slip to be a body in a piece of cloth, and to let that be enough.

There is also a texture to wearing one. Silk, the cloth the slip dress is most traditionally made of, is cool against the skin. It moves with the body rather than against it. It catches the light in a way that no other fabric catches the light — a soft, almost liquid shimmer that does not sparkle but glides. There is the sound, too, which is the softest of small whispers, the sound of the fabric moving against itself as you walk. The slip dress is the dress you wear when you want to be heard from across a room and almost not heard at all.

## Why it has come back

Every revival in fashion has a reason, even when the season's editors pretend it does not. The slip dress is returning now because we are tired. We are tired of dressing as though every day were a performance. We are tired of the costume logic of the early twenty-twenties, where every outfit seemed to require a subtext. We are tired of being asked, by our clothes, to be a particular kind of person — a particular kind of *interest*. We want our clothes to be quieter, and softer, and more willing to admit that we are simply ourselves.

The slip dress fits this mood perfectly. It is a dress that does not announce the wearer. It does not even properly *show* the wearer. It suggests. It suggests that there is a person under the silk, and that the person is comfortable being suggested at.

There is also, we think, a more practical reason. The slip dress is, almost uniquely among evening clothes, season-proof. Worn with a long-sleeved knit underneath, it becomes a winter thing. Worn with bare arms and a small leather jacket thrown over, it becomes an autumn thing. Worn with sandals on a hot evening, it becomes a midsummer thing. It can be dressed up with a single piece of fine jewelry, or dressed down with nothing at all. It does not need a particular body to flatter it. It does not need a particular mood to be appropriate. It is a very old shape — Kate Moss wore one to a party in the nineties, and her mother might have worn one in the seventies, and her mother's mother might have worn one in the forties — and like all very old shapes, it has outlasted the moment that made it famous.

This is what we like most about the slip dress. It is not the dress of a particular year. It is the dress of *many* years. It belongs to no single decade and is embarrassed by none of them.

## The quiet confidence of the wearer

If the slip dress has a single quality, it is the quality of permitting. It is a dress that permits the wearer to be the wearer. It is a dress that permits the evening to be the evening. It is a dress that permits the room to be the room. It does not insist.

The women — and they are almost always women, in our experience — who wear slip dresses well have this same quality. They are not performing. They are not even particularly announcing themselves. They are allowing. They are allowing you to notice them, if you happen to be looking in their direction. They are allowing the dress to fall the way it wants to fall, and they are allowing themselves to be the people they actually are.

There is a particular discipline here. It is the discipline of not filling the silence. The dress is, in a sense, a long pause in a conversation. The wearer is the breath after the pause. Together they make a sentence. But neither is trying to be louder than the other.

We have watched women put on a slip dress at the end of a long day. There is almost always a small moment of adjustment — the strap settled, the hem straightened, the silk drawn flat across the collarbone. There is almost always a small breath, after the adjustment. And then, very often, a kind of settling. The shoulders come down. The face relaxes. The body, suddenly free of structure, agrees to be the body. And the woman, in this moment, becomes someone a little more willing to be looked at than she was ten minutes before.

This is what we mean by soft tailoring as a quiet kind of confidence. Not the confidence of a woman who knows she looks good. The confidence of a woman who has decided, on a particular evening, that being looked at does not require her to declare it.

## A small style of wearing it

If we were to offer a small set of notes on how to wear the slip dress well, they would be these. There are only a few, because the dress does not require much.

Wear the dress you actually own, not the dress you wish you owned. A perfect slip dress is one that fits the shoulders and falls from there. It does not need to be expensive. It does not need to be silk, though silk is lovely. Viscose and a fine cotton sateen both fall beautifully and are easier to wash. A slip dress that has been worn a dozen times is more beautiful than a slip dress that has been worn twice. Wear it. Let it learn the length of your stride.

Choose one piece of jewelry, not several. A small gold chain at the throat, or a single ring on the second finger of the left hand, or a pair of pearl studs, the kind your mother probably wore. The dress is the ornament. The jewelry should only confirm the dress. A woman in a slip dress and three necklaces is no longer wearing the dress. The jewelry has put on a dress.

Wear the shoes you can walk in. A flat sandal is fine. A low kitten heel is fine. A clean white sneaker, if the evening is the kind of evening that allows for it, is fine. The dress does not need a stiletto. The dress does not need anything that makes the floor interesting. Let the dress be the dress. Let the shoe be the shoe.

And finally, do not look at yourself more than once before you leave the house. The slip dress does not reward the anxious mirror. The slip dress rewards the woman who checked once, looked away, and decided to trust the cloth.

The slip dress, like all gentle things, has very little to say to the person who needs to be persuaded. It has a great deal to say to the person who already knows.
