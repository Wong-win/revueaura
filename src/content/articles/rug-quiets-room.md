The first apartment I rented on my own had hardwood floors and nothing else. No rugs, no runners, no mats at the door. It looked, when I moved in, like a photograph in a magazine — clean lines, pale wood, the kind of floor you might see in a showroom. I was twenty-three and very pleased with myself. I had bought, with great care, a sofa, a desk, a single armchair, and a lamp. I had not bought a rug, because I thought rugs were the kind of thing you added later, when the room was otherwise finished, the way you might add a vase of flowers or a stack of books on a coffee table. Rugs were accessories. Rugs were, in the hierarchy of the apartment, optional.

Within about a week, I began to understand that I had gotten this entirely backwards. The room was not quiet. The room echoed. Every footstep across the hardwood produced a small, sharp report that traveled to the far wall and came back. If I dropped a spoon in the kitchen, it rang. If I set down a glass on the desk, the desk amplified the sound. If I turned the page of a book too quickly, the page snapped. The room behaved less like a room than like a small wooden box in which I was being asked to live at a slightly louder volume than I would have chosen. I began to dread coming home. Not because I disliked the apartment, but because the apartment was, acoustically, a hostile place. It was a place where every small domestic sound was held up to the light and examined.

I bought a rug on a Saturday, the cheapest wool rug the store had, in a deep red that did not quite match anything else in the apartment. I rolled it out under the coffee table, weighted the corners with the books I had been planning to put on the coffee table, and stepped back. Within an hour, the room had become a different room. The echo was gone. The footsteps softened. The spoon, when I dropped it later that evening, made a dull sound rather than a bright one. The room, which had been holding itself at a distance from me, suddenly leaned in. It stopped being a room I was visiting and started being a room I was living in.

## The Floor Is the Loudest Surface in the Room

We tend to think of the floor as a flat thing. It is the part of the room we look down at, the part we walk on, the part that holds the furniture up. We do not, unless we are unusually attentive, think of the floor as something that makes sound. And yet the floor is, by a considerable margin, the largest continuous surface in any room. The walls are broken up by windows and doors and pictures. The ceiling is interrupted by light fixtures. The floor is just the floor — long, unbroken, uninterrupted, ready to amplify anything that lands on it.

This is why an uncarpeted room, even a small one, almost always feels louder than the same room with a rug. The floor is doing nothing to absorb the sound. The floor is doing, in fact, the opposite of absorbing sound. Hardwood bounces sound back up. Tile bounces it back up. Concrete bounces it back up. Even the most beautiful parquet, even the most carefully laid wide-plank oak, is, acoustically, a kind of drum. It takes every footstep and every dropped object and every voice on the telephone and returns it to the room at slightly higher volume than it was originally produced.

A rug, even a thin one, even a cheap one, even one you have not thought about very carefully, breaks this loop. The fibers of the rug absorb. They convert the energy of the sound into a small amount of heat, which is then lost to the air. What reaches your ear is a quieter version of the original sound, and the difference between those two — the loud version and the quiet version — is the difference between a room and a hall. It is also, more importantly, the difference between a room you can think in and a room you cannot.

I notice this most when I travel. Hotels are almost always over-carpeted, and they feel, acoustically, muffled to the point of suffocation. But the apartments I visit that have no rugs — the apartments of friends who have just moved in, the apartments of younger relatives who have not yet accumulated the soft furnishings of an adult life — feel, almost without exception, exhausting. Not because they are ugly. They are often very beautiful. But because every sentence spoken in them has to compete with the room. The room is louder than the conversation.

## A Rug Is a Piece of Architecture You Lay Down

Most of the things we put in a room are vertical. Sofas, lamps, bookshelves, chairs, curtains — they all rise from the floor. The rug is one of the very few things in a room that is horizontal. It is one of the very few things that defines, by its size and its placement, the actual shape of the room as it is experienced.

This is something designers know and most of the rest of us learn only by getting it wrong. A small rug, placed in the middle of a large room, does almost nothing. It looks, in photographs, like a kind of island — a place where the coffee table sits, disconnected from the rest of the furniture. The room around it is unchanged. The feet of the people sitting on the sofa are still on cold hardwood. The conversation is still echoing across three meters of bare floor. A small rug, in a large room, is the equivalent of a single candle in a dark cathedral. It gestures at warmth. It does not produce any.

A large rug, by contrast, does something quite different. A rug that reaches, at least, under the front legs of the major pieces of furniture — that extends most of the way across the seating area, that meets the wall or comes close to it — changes the geometry of the room. It tells you, acoustically and visually, that this is one space. It tells the eye that the seating group is a single thing. It tells the ear that the sound of the conversation will not escape across an expanse of hardwood. It tells the body, on the most basic level, that the floor is a place you can sit on, lie down on, stretch out on, without feeling that you are lying on a hard surface in the middle of an open plan.

This is one of the reasons a rug is, in my experience, the single best gift you can give a room that has just been emptied. An empty room with bare floors is not a room. It is a possibility. A rug, laid down before anything else has been moved in, is the act that turns the possibility into a place. It is the gesture that says, quietly, to the rest of the room: here is where we will be.

## Wool, and the Materials That Quiet

Not all rugs are equally good at the work of quieting a room. The material matters. The weave matters. The thickness matters. A rug made of wool — dense, slightly springy, slightly oily to the touch, with a deep pile that compresses underfoot and slowly returns — is, by a long way, the most forgiving material you can put under your feet. Wool absorbs sound. Wool holds warmth. Wool forgives the small accidents of domestic life — the spilled wine, the muddy paw, the milk that slopped over the side of the bowl and went unnoticed for half an hour. Wool is also slow. A wool rug, well made, will outlast the rest of the room. It will be there, fading gently in the sunlight, when the sofa has been replaced twice and the curtains three times.

A rug made of synthetic material — polypropylene, nylon, the various petroleum-derived fibers that turn up in cheaper stores — is, acoustically and emotionally, a different object. It does not absorb sound in the same way. It does not warm the foot. It does not forgive spills. It does not age. It sits in the room unchanged for twenty years, looking the same on the day it is removed as on the day it was laid down. This sounds, on paper, like a virtue. In practice, it is the opposite. A room in which nothing changes is a room in which nothing has happened. A room in which nothing has happened is, over time, a depressing place to live.

There is also something to be said for the imperfection of a natural fiber. A wool rug, especially a hand-knotted one, will not be perfectly rectangular. The edges will not be perfectly straight. The pile will not be perfectly even. There will be small variations in color that you cannot quite explain. These variations are not flaws. They are the evidence that a person, or many persons, made the rug by hand, over a long period, in conditions you will never see. A rug that has been made by hand carries, in its imperfections, the time of its making. A machine-made rug carries, in its perfection, the time of no one.

## The Room Under Your Feet

There is a way of thinking about a home in which the floor is the foundation — the literal base, the thing the rest is built on — and a way of thinking about it in which the floor is the last surface to be considered. The first way is older and, I think, closer to how most people actually experience a room. We do not experience a room from above. We experience it from inside, from somewhere near the middle of it, at roughly the height of a seated person. From that vantage point, the floor is not at the bottom of our visual field. It is in the middle of it. We see it constantly. We look at it more, in the course of an ordinary day, than we look at the ceiling or the upper walls.

This is why the rug matters so much. It is not a small detail. It is, in many rooms, the single largest visible object in the room — larger than the sofa, larger than the bed, often larger than any single piece of furniture. It is also, in many rooms, the single largest colored object in the room. The walls might be white. The ceiling will almost certainly be white. The sofa might be a neutral. But the rug, if you let it be, can carry a color — a deep blue, a faded rose, an oxblood red, a sage green, an undyed natural — that becomes, over time, the color the room is remembered in.

I have a friend who has lived in the same apartment for fifteen years, and the only thing she has not changed in all that time is the rug. She has changed the sofa. She has changed the curtains. She has changed the paint twice and the kitchen cabinets once. But the rug, a large undyed wool that has slowly darkened at the edges and grown slightly softer under the table where she eats every evening, has stayed. When I ask her about it, she says she has never considered replacing it. She says it is, by now, more her than the rest of the room put together. She says it is the floor she has actually lived on.

I have come to feel the same way about the rugs in my own apartment. They are not, on any given day, what I notice first about the rooms they are in. They are not what a visitor would compliment, or photograph, or ask about. But they are, I think, the reason the rooms feel the way they feel. They are the reason the rooms are quiet. They are the reason the rooms feel like rooms and not like halls. They are the reason I can sit in them for hours without feeling that the room is watching me, or listening to me, or holding me at a slight remove.

A rug, in the end, is not decoration. It is the floor the room was missing. It is the soft surface under the conversation, under the footsteps, under the cat, under the child who is lying on his stomach reading a comic book on a Saturday afternoon. It is the foundation that quiets a whole room, and the difference between a room and a hall is, as I said at the beginning, mostly underfoot.
