We bought our first real home speaker almost by accident. A small studio monitor, technically — the kind of speaker that musicians use, not the kind that audiophiles argue about — and we put it on a low bookshelf in the corner of the living room because it was the only flat surface available, and we wired it, with a long cable we did not bother to hide, to a small amplifier that sat, slightly apologetically, on the floor underneath. We had, at the time, no intention of building anything that could plausibly be called a home sound system. We were just trying, in our small way, to get the laptop to play music without the slightly tinny sound it had been producing for the previous three years.

The first album we played on it was a record we had been listening to, on and off, for the better part of a decade — a record we knew almost too well, a record that, on headphones, had stopped being interesting some years before. We put it on, more or less at random, while we were making dinner, and within thirty seconds we were standing in the kitchen doorway, slightly arrested. It was not, exactly, that the music sounded better. It was that the music sounded like it had been, all along, this. The album, which had been living in our ears, was suddenly living in the room. The instruments were almost dancing — the bass line walking across the front of the speaker, the brushed snare hovering, slightly, in the air above it, the voice sitting somewhere just to the left of where the guitarist was sitting in the room. We stood, for the duration of the side, almost without moving.

That, we have come to think, was the moment we understood what a home speaker is for. It is not, primarily, for listening to music. Music can be listened to on earbuds, on headphones, on the modest speakers of a phone. The home speaker is for the music being in the room. It is for the slow transformation of an interior space into the kind of room in which music has been playing, on and off, for years — the kind of room in which one finds oneself, on a quiet evening, sitting in a particular armchair in a particular corner because the music seems to be coming from that direction. The home speaker, in other words, is not really a piece of audio equipment. It is a kind of atmospheric furniture.

## the room as an instrument

There is an idea, in the older literature on concert halls, that the room itself is an instrument. The same orchestra in the same hall sounds different on a humid evening than it does on a dry one; the same orchestra in a wooden hall sounds different than it does in a stone one. The room, in other words, is part of the music. The frequencies the room absorbs and the frequencies it returns shape what one hears. The way the walls reflect the sound, the way the ceiling disperses it, the way the carpet softens the high end of the snare — these are not, finally, details. They are, in some important sense, the music.

A home speaker, in this older sense, is not so much a source of sound as a participant in a small acoustic arrangement. The room is, in a literal sense, half the instrument. A speaker in a hard, square room sounds different than the same speaker in a soft, irregular one. A speaker on a low table sounds different than the same speaker on a high shelf. A speaker in a corner sounds different than the same speaker pulled out into the room. We did not, when we put our first speaker on the low shelf in the corner, know any of this. We learned it, instead, the slow way, by moving the speaker three inches to the left and listening for a week.

What we have come to think, after several years of small experiments, is that the placement of a home speaker is one of those small domestic decisions — like the placement of a reading chair or the height of a bedside lamp — that has an outsized effect on the daily texture of a room. A speaker that has been carefully placed is, in some quiet way, more present in the room than one that has been merely set down. It is the difference between a piece of furniture that has been chosen and a piece of furniture that has been installed. We have moved our speaker, over the years, to four different positions in three different rooms, and each move has, in some small but reliable way, altered the music and, with it, the room.

## the album as a slow companion

What we did not expect, when we installed our first speaker, was how much the music would change. Not the music itself, which had been the same all along — we still had the same albums, the same artists, the same slightly embarrassing fondness for late-period Talk Talk — but our relationship to it. Music on headphones, we discovered, is a private act. Music on a home speaker is, somehow, a domestic one. It is part of the room. It is the soundtrack of the room. It is what one is doing, on a quiet evening, while one is doing the other things.

The album, in particular, takes on a different character when it is played on a home speaker. We had not, for years, listened to albums. We had listened to playlists — to long, algorithmically curated sequences of songs designed, more or less explicitly, to keep the listener's attention by varying the texture of the music every three or four minutes. The playlist is, in this sense, a piece of software. It is built to retain. It is the musical equivalent of a slot machine. The home speaker, by contrast, encourages the album. The album is, after all, a piece of music designed to be heard in order, with attention, in a particular room, by a particular listener. The home speaker, in its quiet way, restores that older relationship.

We have, over the last several years, begun to play albums on repeat. Not in any obsessive way — not in the way that a particularly enthusiastic teenager plays the same record fifty times in a week — but in the slow, domestic way in which one plays the same record for a month or two before moving on. The album becomes, in this sense, a kind of companion. It is the album of the autumn. It is the album of the winter we moved apartments. It is the album of the year the dog died. We have albums, in our memory, that are pinned to particular rooms. We can hear, sometimes, an album we have not played in fifteen years and be, for a moment, completely and vividly back in the room where we last heard it. The home speaker, in its quiet way, has been the thing that has made this possible. The playlist does not do this. The playlist is, by design, placeless. The album is, by design, attached.

## what the room sounds like when no one is in it

What we have also come to notice, in our years of living with a home speaker, is how much the speaker changes when no one is in the room. The speaker, on a quiet afternoon, with no one in particular listening, plays into an empty room, and the room, in its empty way, listens back. The music that was, a moment before, the soundtrack of making dinner becomes, in the empty room, something slightly stranger — a kind of half-present music that is being played for the room itself, as if the room had asked for it.

There is, we have come to think, something almost old-fashioned about this. The home speaker is, in a small but real way, an heir to the piano in the parlor. The piano in the parlor was not, primarily, an instrument. It was, in many cases, a piece of furniture that happened to be playable — a thing one kept in the room partly because one might, on some quiet evening, sit down and play a few bars of something one had been playing for thirty years. The piano in the parlor was, in this sense, the original home speaker. It was a piece of music that lived in the room. It was not, primarily, a way of listening. It was a way of having music in the house.

The home speaker, in 2026, has inherited, we think, some of this older function. It is, at its best, a small piece of music that lives in the room. It plays an album on repeat. It plays a single piece, on a quiet evening, while the rest of the room does what the rest of the room does. It does not, in any reasonable sense, demand anything of the listener. It is, in the older sense of the word, polite. It asks nothing. It is, like a good cat, simply present.

What we have come to think, after several years of living with one, is that the home speaker is one of the small domestic objects of the century. It is not, in any sense, essential. One can live, after all, without music in the room. But a room with music in it — a room with a single small speaker in a corner, playing an album on repeat, while the afternoon does what it does — is, in some way we cannot quite articulate, a more inhabitable room. It is a room in which one is, slightly more often than not, at home. We have come to think of the home speaker, in our own life, as something between a piece of furniture and a small companion. It does not, in any sense, talk back. It does not, in any sense, have an opinion. It plays music. It is, in this sense, the quietest and most unobtrusive member of the household. We would, on the whole, be very sorry to lose it.
