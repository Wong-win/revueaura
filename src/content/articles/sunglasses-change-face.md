There is a pair of sunglasses on the small table by the front door. They have been on the small table by the front door, in roughly the same position, for long enough that the table has come to expect them. The frames are a deep, almost black tortoise that catches the light at certain angles. The lenses are slightly darker in the center than at the edges, where the dye has begun, in the way dye does on acetate, to soften with the years. There is a tiny scratch across the left lens, just above where the pupil sits, that came, no one quite remembers how, from being put down too quickly on a hard surface. There is a small softening, almost a dent, in the right temple, where the thumb always goes to put them on.

They are not, by any reasonable definition, a new pair of sunglasses. And they are, by almost every working definition, the most worn object in the house.

This is the strange and rather wonderful thing about a really good pair of sunglasses. They are, in the strictest physical sense, almost nothing. Two pieces of glass, or two pieces of plastic. Two small arms that fold. A bridge that sits, slightly pressed, against the top of the nose. The whole object, when folded, fits into the palm of a hand. And yet, in the strange economy of the face, this almost-nothing is, by some distance, the most expressive single accessory a person can put on. Two pieces of tinted glass, perched for a moment on the bridge of the nose, and a stranger is born.

The stranger is, very often, a better version of the wearer. The stranger is composed where the wearer is tired. The stranger is interested where the wearer is bored. The stranger is paying attention where the wearer is, in fact, looking at the second hand of the clock. The sunglasses have, in the small physics of the face, taken on the job of editing the wearer.

This is the gift of the right pair. This is also, in our experience, the reason almost everyone, at some point, becomes a little addicted to them.

## The disguise that flatters

We tend, in our thinking about sunglasses, to file them under the heading of accessories. They are, in this filing system, the same kind of object as a scarf, or a watch, or a piece of jewelry — a small addition to the rest of the outfit that can be swapped out according to the season or the mood. But sunglasses are not, in fact, an accessory in the way a scarf is. A scarf changes how the neck reads. A watch changes how the wrist reads. Sunglasses change how the face reads.

This is a more important change than it sounds. The face, in almost every human interaction, is doing the majority of the work. The face is what the person across the table is reading, when the conversation is at its most attentive. The face is what the camera is reaching for, when somebody at a wedding has pulled out a phone. The face is, in the small economy of being seen, the asset that is most often being spent. And sunglasses, by covering the most expressive quarter of the face, change the cost of being seen.

This is why a good pair of sunglasses is, in a small way, the cheapest disguise in the wardrobe. A hat changes how the head reads. A coat changes how the body reads. A pair of sunglasses changes, almost instantly, how the eyes are read — which is to say, how the face is read — which is to say, how the person is read. The sunglasses have agreed, in a small way, to keep the room out of the conversation.

And yet, at the same time, a good pair of sunglasses flatters. A good pair of sunglasses smooths. A good pair of sunglasses adds a small, almost architectural line to the face that was not there before. This is also why almost everyone, when they put on a pair of sunglasses that suit them, looks, in the mirror, briefly surprised. The surprise is the recognition that the face, with this small addition, has become a slightly different face. The surprise is the moment of meeting the stranger.

## A small history, written in tortoiseshell

Sunglasses, in the form we recognize them, are an old piece of equipment. The earliest versions were snow goggles, used by the Inuit to prevent snow blindness, and made from bone or wood with narrow slits cut for the eyes. The earliest tinted versions were worn, in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, by judges in courtrooms who did not want the people in the dock to be able to read their eyes. The sunglasses of the courtroom were, in this sense, the first modern sunglasses. They were not for the sun. They were for not being read.

The sunglasses became, properly speaking, a fashion object somewhere in the middle of the twentieth century, when aviators and movie stars began to be photographed in them, and when the soft lighting of Hollywood discovered that a face behind a pair of tinted glasses became, in the photograph, a more compelling face. The aviators and the movie stars understood, in their different ways, what the Inuit and the judges had understood. The face, behind the glasses, becomes a more composable face.

The tortoiseshell frame, in particular, became the dominant shape of the twentieth-century sunglasses. The tortoiseshell had, by design, a small warmth to it. The tortoiseshell sat, on most faces, without competing with the rest of the outfit. The tortoiseshell agreed to be present without being loud. The tortoiseshell was, in this sense, the small black dress of the eyewear drawer. It worked with everything. It flattered almost everyone. It was, very often, the one a person kept longest.

### the small question of the face they suit

We are not, in this piece, going to give advice about which pair of sunglasses suits which face. We are, in our own way, skeptical of the whole project. The reason is that the right pair of sunglasses is, very often, not the pair that the chart says suits your face shape. The right pair of sunglasses is, very often, the pair that, when you put it on, makes you feel briefly like a stranger you would like to know.

This is, in our experience, the only reliable test. Put them on. Stand in front of a mirror, in good light, for a few seconds. Notice whether the face in the mirror is, briefly, more interesting than the face you are used to. Notice whether the face in the mirror has, briefly, the small quality of being a face you would look at twice, in a crowd.

If the answer is yes, that is the pair. If the answer is no, even a little, it is not the pair. The face will tell you.

## The other pair that does the same work

There is, in almost every drawer of sunglasses we have ever looked at carefully, a second pair. The second pair is the one that was bought for a specific purpose. The second pair is the one that was bought for a vacation, or for a sport, or for a particular kind of light. The second pair is the slightly more aggressive, slightly more specialized, slightly less universal version of the sunglasses the person actually wears.

The second pair has its place. We are not against the second pair. The second pair is the one that gets pulled out when the light is unusually bright. The second pair is the one that gets pulled out when the more aggressive silhouette of the sunglasses feels appropriate. The second pair is the one that gets pulled out when the tortoiseshell frame, for whatever reason, is not what the afternoon requires.

But the second pair is, almost never, the pair that lives on the small table by the front door. The second pair is, almost never, the pair that gets put on as a default. The second pair is, almost always, the pair that requires a small decision. The first pair, the tortoiseshell, the universal one, the one that flatters almost every face and goes with almost every outfit, is the pair that has agreed to be the answer.

This is, we think, the small structure of a good sunglasses drawer. One pair that is, by design, the answer to most days. One pair that is, by design, the answer to the days when the answer is not the tortoiseshell. The first pair is the one that gets worn. The second pair is the one that gets admired.

## What the lenses hide

It is worth, also, saying a small word about what the lenses hide. The lenses hide, of course, the sun. This is the original job. The lenses, in their best form, are the small piece of tinted glass that lets a person look at a bright day without squinting. The lenses are the small piece of equipment that lets a person walk, for an hour, into a sun that would otherwise have made the eyes water.

But the lenses hide, also, almost everything else. The lenses hide the small softening of the eyes at the end of a long afternoon. The lenses hide the brief expression that crosses the face when the wearer is, in fact, bored, or tired, or uncertain. The lenses have agreed, in a small way, to be the editing layer of the face.

This is also why taking the sunglasses off, in the company of another person, is a slightly more intimate gesture than almost any other small gesture in the modern wardrobe. To take off the sunglasses, in front of somebody, is to allow the editing layer to be removed. It is to say, in the language of the face: I am going to let you see the unreduced version of me, for a moment.

We think about this, sometimes, at the end of a long lunch. The sunglasses have been off, on the table beside the wine glass, for an hour. We pick them up. We put them on to walk through the bright afternoon into the next part of the day. The face, behind the lenses, becomes, briefly, the slightly more edited version. We walk back into the world with the stranger back on.

## The small object that travels

The sunglasses, of all the objects in the wardrobe, are also, by some distance, the most traveled. The sunglasses go in a pocket. The sunglasses get pulled out in the back of a cab. The sunglasses get pulled out on a train. The sunglasses get pulled out at the gate of an airport that is, with its fluorescent lighting and its acres of glass, somehow always slightly brighter than expected. The sunglasses are, in a small way, the only piece of the wardrobe that gets worn in almost every room.

This is also why a good pair of sunglasses has, in our experience, been to more places than the rest of the wardrobe combined. A good pair of sunglasses has been to the beach. A good pair of sunglasses has been to the funeral. A good pair of sunglasses has been to the lunch. A good pair of sunglasses has been to the morning after the morning after. The sunglasses have, in their small way, seen a slightly wider range of the life than almost any other object in the wardrobe.

This is also why the scratches, on the well-worn pair, are not a flaw. The scratches are the record. The tiny scratch above the left pupil is the record of the day the sunglasses were put down too quickly. The small softening of the right temple is the record of the thumb, putting them on, ten thousand times. The slightly uneven darkness at the edges of the lenses is the record of the years. The sunglasses have, in their own quiet way, been keeping the whole story.

This is what the sunglasses have agreed to do. They are the most portable piece of the wardrobe. They are the most expressive. They are the cheap disguise that flatters. They are the small object that changes how a face is read. And they are, by the end of a long life of small uses, the small object that has, in its own quiet way, kept the most accurate record of who you have been, on which afternoon, in which pair of sunglasses.
