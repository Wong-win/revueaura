For most of the history of sunscreen as a product, it was sold as a thing you took on holiday. You bought it in May. You applied it generously on the first hot weekend of the year, when the sun came through the car windscreen and the back of your neck prickled. You reapplied it on the beach. You came home with a tan or a burn, depending on your luck, and the bottle sat on the bathroom shelf for the rest of the year, waiting for its next Mediterranean fortnight. This is no longer how most of us use sunscreen, and it is no longer how most of us should think about it.

The shift has happened quietly, and is now happening quickly. Sunscreen, in the last ten years, has moved from being a beach accessory to being the foundation of almost every thoughtful routine. Dermatologists, having spent decades trying to convince the public of this, finally have something close to consensus behind them. The sun is, by a comfortable margin, the single most important environmental factor in how a face looks at fifty. Not the cleanser we used, or the serum we could afford, or the night cream we forgot to put on half the nights of our twenties. The sun. Everything else, in the long accounting of a face, is a footnote.

## Why the sun matters so much

The skin is the body's interface with the world, and the sun is the part of the world that does the most to it. Ultraviolet light, in particular, is what dermatologists spend most of their careers trying to protect us from. There are two kinds that matter. UVA is the longer wavelength, the one that penetrates deeper into the skin, the one that does most of the slow, cumulative damage we associate with aging - the brown patches, the fine lines, the slight leathery quality that a face takes on after decades of inadvertent exposure. UVB is the shorter wavelength, the one that burns, the one that is the direct cause of most skin cancers. A modern sunscreen protects against both, which is what the "broad spectrum" label is meant to convey.

The reason this matters, day to day, is that the sun we encounter in our normal lives is more than enough to do all of this damage. The myth that you only need sunscreen on bright days, or only when you are visibly hot, or only on holiday, is one of the more persistent pieces of misinformation in the beauty category. UV light comes through clouds. It comes through car windows. It comes through the windows of offices. It is reflected off water, off concrete, off snow. We are, on most days and in most cities, in, it turns out, significantly more sun than we think we are.

This is also why the question of SPF has, over the last decade, stopped being a beach question and started being a daily one. The sunscreen we apply on a Tuesday in February, walking to the train, matters just as much as the sunscreen we apply on a Saturday in July on a boat. Probably more, in fact, because we are more likely to forget it.

## What a number actually means

The SPF number on a bottle is one of the more misunderstood things in personal care, and it is worth pausing on. SPF stands for sun protection factor. It is a measure of how much longer, compared to wearing no sunscreen at all, you can be in the sun before you start to burn. An SPF of 30 means, in theory, that you can be out for thirty times longer than you could without it. An SPF of 50 means fifty times. The relationship, like, says - the key word being in theory - is not linear. The jump from 30 to 50 is a smaller jump than the jump from 5 to 25. By 50, you have most of the protection that any sunscreen can offer.

What this means, in practice, is that SPF 30, applied properly, is enough for most days. The high-protection SPFs - 70, 90, 100 - are mostly a marketing concession to the sense that more must be better. They offer marginally more protection. They often feel heavier on the face. For most of us, most of the time, SPF 30 is the right place to be. We should not, however, take the number on the bottle to mean we are protected for the length of a working day. The number assumes an application that most of us do not achieve. The honest way to think about sunscreen is not in terms of SPF at all but in terms of application.

The amount that actually delivers the SPF on the label is roughly a quarter teaspoon for the face. A quarter teaspoon is more than most of us use. It is the amount that takes a moment to rub in, that leaves a slight cast for a few minutes, that feels, for a few seconds, like too much. This is normal. This is what the right amount feels like. If the sunscreen disappears instantly into the skin, the way a serum does, we have probably used half of what we needed.

## The texture question

One of the most significant changes in the sunscreen category, in the last five years, has been the texture. The old sunscreens - the ones our parents used, the ones that were designed to be applied once a day on a beach towel - were thick. They were white. They had a particular smell that became, for an entire generation, the smell of sunscreen, although it had less to do with sunscreen than with the chemical filters of the era. They sat on the skin. They made the skin look, in photographs, slightly grey. They were, by modern standards, unpleasant to wear, which is part of the reason most of us did not wear them every day.

The modern sunscreen is a different object. The new chemical filters do not have the smell. The mineral filters have been micronized to the point where they do not leave a visible cast, at least in most of the better formulations. The textures, generally, are lighter. The best of them, the ones made by the brands that have spent serious money on formulation, feel like a moisturizer with a job. They are not invisible. They are not meant to be invisible. But they are wearable, in the way the older sunscreens were not, which is why so many of us are now applying them, every morning, regardless of whether we plan to leave the house.

This is, finally, the thing that has shifted the conversation. The technology caught up with the science. The dermatologists had been telling us, for decades, to wear sunscreen every day. We had not been doing it, because the products available had not been good enough to make the habit stick. The newer formulations, finally, are good enough. We are beginning to believe the science because the products have become a pleasure to use.

## The morning ritual

Where, then, does sunscreen go in the routine? This is one of the questions the beauty press has spent some time on, and the answer is simpler than it sounds. Sunscreen should be the last step of the morning routine, applied after moisturizer and before makeup. The reason for this is that sunscreen needs to sit on the surface to work. If we apply it and then apply anything on top, we are partly undoing the application. If we apply it on top of a moisturizer, the moisturizer is doing the moisturizing and the sunscreen is doing the sunblocking, and they are not interfering with each other.

For those of us who wear makeup, the question becomes more complex. Foundation with SPF is better than no sunscreen and worse than an actual sunscreen. The SPF in most foundations is achieved at a much lower concentration than the SPF in a sunscreen. It is a useful backup. It is not a substitute. The most reliable approach is to apply a dedicated sunscreen, give it a minute to settle, and then apply whatever we want on top.

For those of us who do not wear makeup, the question is simpler. Sunscreen, on its own, is a fine finish. The slightly dewy look that a well-formulated SPF leaves on the skin is, in most lights, more interesting than wearing nothing at all. It is also the most consequential thing we will do for our face all day.

## What daily use actually does

It is worth being honest, here, about what daily sunscreen can and cannot do. It cannot reverse damage that has already been done. It cannot, on its own, address the fine lines and the pigmentation changes that have already accumulated. It can, however, prevent most of the damage that would otherwise accumulate over the next twenty years. This is the actual arithmetic of it. We are not making our faces younger. We are keeping our faces where they are.

This is also why the question is no longer whether to wear it, but how to wear it in a way that does not feel like a chore. The texture matters. The packaging matters. The fact that we like the bottle, that we keep it on the bathroom shelf where we can see it, that we are not annoyed by the smell - all of this matters, because the product that stays on the shelf is the one that gets used. The product that gets used is the one that does the work.

There is a quiet pleasure, once the habit has been established, in reaching for the same bottle on most mornings of most weeks. It becomes one of the small fixed points around which the day organizes itself. We brush our teeth. We apply the SPF. We look, briefly, at ourselves in the mirror, in the kind of light that does not flatter anyone, and we leave the bathroom and we go. The sun is out there, as it always is, doing what it always does. We have made our small daily decision against it. That is, in the long run, what the face will remember.
