We lost, in a fairly unremarkable way, the better part of a year of photographs last spring. The laptop, which had been complaining in the polite way that laptops complain when their hard drives are full, decided, on a Thursday morning, that it had had enough. The screen went blue in the way that screens go blue when they have stopped believing in the user. The hard drive made the small unhappy noise that hard drives make when they have decided to retire. We were, on the whole, lucky. The photographs that had been synced, automatically, in the background, to a small cloud service we had signed up for years earlier and mostly forgotten about, were, in fact, mostly still there. The draft of the novel we had been working on, in a folder that we had not backed up because we had not thought about it, was, in fact, mostly not there.

This is, we recognize, the kind of small disaster that people are slightly embarrassed to talk about. The lost novel is, in any commercial sense, a small thing. The lost novel is, in the experience of a real life, a moderately large thing. The draft was, after all, the draft of the novel we had been working on, in our small way, for the better part of three years. The draft contained, in its pages, the better part of a particular kind of attention we had been paying, on quiet evenings, to a particular set of characters we had invented. The draft was, in the small private sense in which such things matter, a part of us. The draft was, in some quiet way, gone.

What we learned, in the weeks that followed the small Thursday disaster, is that the backup is the most underrated piece of personal technology of the last twenty years. The backup is, in fact, one of the few pieces of technology that becomes more useful the less one thinks about it. The backup is the only piece of technology that we have, over the years, gotten steadily more grateful for the more we have stopped noticing it.

## the strange gift of forgetting

The backup, we have come to think, is the strangest form of insurance one ever buys. Most forms of insurance, in our experience, are forms of insurance one thinks about. The car insurance is paid monthly. The home insurance is reviewed at the renewal. The health insurance is selected, and reselected, and argued about, in the slow time of a long adult life. The backup, by contrast, is the form of insurance one is supposed to forget about. The whole point of the backup is that it sits, silently, in the background of the day, doing the small thing it was set up to do, while one does not, in fact, think about it.

This is, on the surface, a small thing. It is, on reflection, a fairly strange one. The whole architecture of modern personal technology, in our experience, is built around the idea that the technology is something one is supposed to think about. The phone is something one is supposed to scroll through. The laptop is something one is supposed to be productive on. The smart speaker is something one is supposed to ask things of. The technology industry, in its broadest form, is the industry of getting one to think about technology. The backup is, in this sense, almost the only form of personal technology that explicitly does not want one to think about it. The backup is, in fact, the only form of technology that becomes, in some quiet way, more useful when one has stopped thinking about it.

What we have come to think, in the months since the small Thursday disaster, is that this is, in fact, the most mature thing a piece of technology can do. The backup is, in some quiet way, the technology equivalent of a good bookshelf. The good bookshelf, after a while, stops being a piece of furniture one thinks about. The good bookshelf becomes, instead, a piece of architecture — a kind of wall one can pull a book from. The backup, in a similar way, becomes a kind of wall — a kind of wall one can pull a photograph from, or a draft from, or a small piece of one's working life from. The backup, like the bookshelf, does not, in any obvious sense, demand anything of one. The backup is, in this older sense, the piece of personal technology that has, over time, quietly graduated from being a piece of furniture to being a piece of architecture.

## the slow project of setting it up

The strange thing about the backup, in our experience, is that the setting up of it is more memorable than the using of it. The setting up is, in fact, a small project. The setting up requires, in the first place, the decision to do it — which is, in our experience, the most difficult part. The decision to do it is, in our experience, often delayed for years. We had, ourselves, been meaning to set up a backup for the better part of a decade before the small Thursday disaster. The backup, when one actually sits down to set it up, takes, in our experience, an afternoon. The setup, that afternoon, involves choosing a service, or a drive, or a combination of the two. The setup involves, in our experience, a small amount of fiddling with software. The setup involves, in our experience, the small mild embarrassment of realizing that there is, in fact, more on the laptop than one had realized — photographs one had forgotten about, drafts one had abandoned, emails one had been meaning to reread for years.

What we noticed, in the afternoon we set up the backup, is that the setup has a particular texture. The setup has the texture of a project one does not, in fact, regret starting. The setup has the texture of decluttering a closet one has been meaning to declutter for years. The setup has the texture of finally filing the small pile of papers one has been moving, from desk to desk, for the better part of an adult life. The setup is, in this older sense, the kind of small domestic project that one tends to put off, and that one tends, when one finally does it, to wonder why one put it off for so long.

We have, in the years since that afternoon, recommended the setup of a backup to a great many friends. The recommendation, in our experience, is usually received politely and then forgotten about. The recommendation, in our experience, is sometimes received with the small embarrassed look of someone who has, in fact, also been meaning to do it. The recommendation, in our experience, is occasionally received with a small story about a small disaster — a hard drive that failed, a phone that fell in a toilet, a small piece of writing that one had not, in fact, saved in any reliable way. The stories, in our experience, are almost always told with the small rueful laugh of someone who has, in fact, learned the lesson the hard way. The lesson, in our experience, is one of those lessons that one can, in fact, only learn the hard way. The lesson is, in our experience, one of the few lessons of modern life that has not, so far, gotten any easier to teach in advance.

## the piece of technology that ages well

What is interesting, in our experience, is that the backup is, in some quiet way, one of the few pieces of personal technology that has gotten steadily better over the years. The backup, in 2006, was a piece of personal technology one had to think about. The backup, in 2006, was a small external drive one had to remember to plug in. The backup, in 2006, was a piece of software one had to remember to run. The backup, in 2006, was the kind of small chore that one was always one step behind on. The backup, in 2026, is, by contrast, a piece of personal technology that one does not, in any obvious sense, have to think about. The backup, in 2026, has become, in many cases, a small background process — a small thing that runs, in the background of the laptop, or the phone, while one is doing other things. The backup, in 2026, is, in many cases, simply there.

This is, we want to be clear, a small piece of progress. The small piece of progress has, in our experience, made an enormous difference to the texture of the modern day. The modern day has, in its quieter moments, become a slightly less anxious place because the small Thursday disasters — the small Thursday disasters that used to be a fairly regular feature of an adult life — have become, in many cases, slightly less permanent. What we have come to think, in the months since our small Thursday disaster, is that this is one of the better pieces of small progress the technology industry has produced in the last twenty years. The piece of progress has made the average adult life slightly less precarious. The piece of progress has given back, to the average adult life, a small piece of attention that used to be spent worrying about the small disasters one was, in fact, not doing anything about. The small piece of attention, in our experience, is now available for other things — for the novel one is working on, for the project one is thinking about, for the small Sunday morning in which one would like, ideally, not to think about the failure modes of one's own hard drive.

## the gift one gives to a future self

What we have come to think, ultimately, about the backup is that it is, in its quiet way, a piece of technology that is doing a particular kind of small moral work. The backup is, in fact, the small piece of personal technology that is the most explicit kind of gift one gives to a future self. The phone is, in some sense, a gift to a present self. The laptop is, in some sense, a gift to a present self. The smart speaker is, in some sense, a gift to a present self. The backup, by contrast, is the only piece of personal technology that is, by definition, a gift to a future self. The backup is, in fact, the small piece of personal technology that has no present-self benefit at all. The backup is, in its quiet way, entirely other-directed.

This is, we have come to think, a fairly remarkable feature of a piece of personal technology. The phone, after all, is one of the most present-self-occupied pieces of technology one has ever owned. The phone, in any given minute of the day, is asking something of the present self. The phone is asking one to scroll, or to tap, or to read, or to send. The phone is, in this sense, one of the more attention-demanding pieces of technology one has ever owned. The backup, by contrast, is one of the least attention-demanding pieces of technology one has ever owned. The backup does not, in any obvious sense, ask for anything. The backup is, in fact, the small piece of personal technology one has, in our experience, gotten steadily more grateful for the less it has asked of us.

The small Thursday disaster, in the end, taught us a fairly simple lesson, which is the same lesson every small Thursday disaster has, throughout the history of personal technology, taught everyone who has lived through one. The lesson is that the small piece of personal technology one should set up, in advance, is the small piece one never thinks about until it is needed. The lesson, in other words, is that the backup is the only piece of technology we should invest a small afternoon in setting up — and then, once set up, get back to the rest of our lives. The backup, in our experience, has become the small piece of personal technology we recommend, to every friend we have, with the steady conviction of someone who has learned the lesson. The backup is, in its quiet way, the gift one gives to the future self one is not going to know — and the future self, in our experience, has, on every occasion we have tested this, been quietly grateful.
