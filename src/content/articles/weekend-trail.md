There is a particular trail, not far from my house, that I have walked on a Saturday or a Sunday in most weeks for several years. The trail is six kilometres long, out and back, with a small hill in the middle and a stream at the bottom. The trail is not, in any objective sense, remarkable. It does not appear in any guidebook. It has no view at the top. It is, mostly, just trees, in a row, in a forest, with a path through them.

I have walked this trail in every season. I have walked it in spring, when the trilliums are out and the streams are loud and the forest smells of mud. I have walked it in summer, when the deerflies are out and the leaves are dense and the path is mostly shade. I have walked it in autumn, when the maples turn and the light comes in low and the forest smells, briefly, like a forest in a book. I have walked it in winter, with the snow on the ground and the trees black against the white, and the silence, when the wind drops, is the most complete silence I know.

I mention this trail because I think there is something true about the practice of choosing one trail, and walking it often, that is not true about the practice of walking many trails once. The first kind of walking is what the hiking magazines write about. The second kind is what we actually do, and what, I think, we are secretly trying to do, even when we say we want to do the first kind.

## the trail as a place you know

We have, in the modern world, almost lost the idea of a place you know. We have replaced it, instead, with the idea of places you have been. We have been to many places. We have known very few. Knowing a place is different from having been to it. Knowing a place is a long, slow, mostly boring accumulation of small observations, in the same place, over many visits. Knowing a place is what happens when you have walked the same trail enough times that you know, without looking, where the roots are, and where the mud is deepest in spring, and where the squirrels have their dens, and where the light, on a particular morning in early November, comes through the trees at the exact angle that makes the path look like a painting.

This kind of knowing cannot be acquired on a single trip. It cannot be acquired on a single season. It can only be acquired by walking, again and again, in the same place, with the same pair of shoes, and the same small bag, and the same modest attention. It is the kind of knowing that is, I think, what the word practice actually means. Practice is not a single performance. Practice is repetition. Practice is the slow accumulation, over years, of the small observations that become, eventually, a place you know.

There is a particular pleasure in this knowing. The pleasure is hard to describe, because it is not the pleasure of discovery. The pleasure of discovery is what we are usually sold, by travel and by adventure writing. The pleasure of discovery is the pleasure of seeing something new. The pleasure of knowing is the pleasure of seeing something old, in a new way. The pleasure is the pleasure of noticing, on the fifteenth time you have walked past a particular tree, that the tree has grown, slightly, since the last time. The pleasure is the pleasure of noticing that the sapling you pointed out to a friend, three summers ago, is now, in fact, taller than you are. The pleasure is the pleasure of seeing the place change, slowly, while you change, slowly, beside it.

The pleasure is also the pleasure of not needing to look at the map. The pleasure of walking a trail so often that you can walk it with your eyes closed, which is not a thing you should do, but which is the kind of thing you can almost do, when you have been going to the same forest for years. The pleasure is the pleasure of stopping, on a particular corner, in a particular light, and saying, to no one in particular, here, this is the spot. The spot has no name. The spot is yours.

## the weekend, as a structure

There is something to be said, also, for the weekend. The weekend is, in our current arrangement, the only time we have, in any predictable way, for the slow practice of walking a known place. The weekend is also, of course, the time we have been told, by every lifestyle magazine, to fill with as much novelty and adventure as we can afford. We want to fill it with flights to a new city, with reservations at a new restaurant, with tickets to a new show. The weekend, we are told, is for living. The weekday is for surviving.

I want to argue, gently, against this. The weekend, I want to suggest, is also, and maybe more, for the slow, unglamorous, deeply ordinary practice of walking the same trail, in the same forest, in the same pair of shoes. The weekend, I want to suggest, is the only time most of us have to be in a place we actually know, instead of a place we have just arrived at. The weekend, I want to suggest, is the only time most of us have to be a person who lives in a particular place, instead of a person who is just passing through.

I do not, by this, mean to argue against novelty. There are places I have been once, in my life, that I will remember until I die. There are places I have been that I will go back to, when I can. There is, of course, a place for novelty, and adventure, and the strange pleasure of being a stranger in a strange city, none of which I want to argue against. I mean only that there is also a place, and this is the place I am interested in, for the slow repetition of the same trail, in the same forest, on most weekends, for a long time.

The slow repetition is, I think, the kind of thing we have lost. We have lost it because the world has told us, for many years, that slow repetition is the same as stagnation. The world has told us that the only thing worth doing, on a weekend, is the new thing. The world has, I think, been wrong about this, in the way that the world has been wrong about many things. The world has been wrong because the world has confused novelty with depth. Novelty is, by definition, shallow. Depth, by definition, requires repetition. You cannot know a place deeply in a single visit. You can only know a place deeply by visiting it, slowly, again and again, until the place has become part of you.

## the small details, accumulated

I have, on the trail near my house, a small catalogue of details. There is the tree, near the second stream crossing, that has a woodpecker's nest in it, which I check on every walk, in April, with the attentiveness of a nervous aunt. There is the rock, just past the third switchback, that has a thin stripe of quartz running through it, which catches the light, on certain mornings, in a way that has made me stop, more than once, and just look. There is the patch of trilliums, on the south-facing slope, that come out, every spring, slightly earlier than the year before, which I have come to interpret, without any evidence, as a sign of something I cannot quite name.

None of these details is, by itself, important. The woodpecker's nest is, in the larger scheme of things, just a hole in a tree. The quartz stripe is, in the larger scheme of things, just a piece of rock. The trilliums are, in the larger scheme of things, just a small white flower. None of them matters, in any way that the books, the apps, the lifestyle magazines would recognise as mattering. All of them matter, in the way that a place you know matters. All of them are the reason you go to the trail in the first place.

The small details accumulate. The accumulation is the point. The accumulation is what makes the trail yours, in a way that no single visit could. The accumulation is what makes the forest, not just a forest, but your forest, in the same way that a friend is not just a person, but your friend. The accumulation is what makes the weekend, not just a weekend, but a particular weekend, in a particular season, in a particular year of your life, which you will, for the rest of your life, remember as the weekend you saw, for the first time, the woodpecker come out of the hole.

## what the trail is for

The trail, in the end, is not for anything, exactly. The trail is not for fitness. The trail is not, mostly, for the view. The trail is not for the photograph. The trail is, mostly, for the practice. The practice is the slow repetition, the slow accumulation, the slow knowing of a place. The practice is, in the end, what the trail is for.

The trail is also, I think, for the weekend. The weekend is the only time, in most of our lives, when we can do a thing without justifying it. The weekday, we have to justify. The weekday has the meeting. The weekday has the deadline. The weekday has the school run. The weekend, briefly, does not have to justify anything. The weekend can be, if we let it, the time we give ourselves, in return, for the weekdays. The weekend is, in this sense, a small weekly gift, from us, to us.

I have come to think that the best way to spend this gift is to walk a known trail. Not a new trail. Not a trail that has been recommended by a podcast. A known trail. A trail you have walked, in your own shoes, in your own weather, in your own company, for many years. A trail that has, in the slow accumulation of small details, become part of your life. A trail you could walk with your eyes closed. A trail you have walked with your eyes open. A trail you will walk, if you are lucky, next Saturday, and the Saturday after, and the Saturday after that, until the trail has become, in some quiet way you cannot quite name, the spine of the week.

So choose a trail. A place you can walk to, on a Saturday, in less than an hour. A place that is not, in any objective sense, remarkable. A place that has, in the slow accumulation of small details, become yours. Walk it. Walk it again. Walk it until the trail has become the weekend, and the weekend has become the trail, and the small details have become the spine of a life you were not, when you started, quite sure you were building.

The trail will be there. The weekend will be there. The small details will accumulate. The rest will follow.
