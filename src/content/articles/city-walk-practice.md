There is a particular block in my neighborhood that I have walked past, on average, four hundred times. There is a fire escape on it. I cannot tell you when I first noticed the fire escape, but I can tell you, now, that it is painted a colour I do not have a name for, and that the paint is peeling in a way that looks, on certain afternoons, like the side of a cliff, and that the bottom step has a small plant growing out of it that I have watched, over several summers, get slightly larger. None of this is information. None of it is useful. None of it is on the map. All of it is, somehow, the reason I live here.

Walking a city slowly is an act of devotion. I do not mean this in any religious sense. I mean it in the older, simpler sense. It is an act of giving something up — speed, efficiency, the constant pressure to be elsewhere — in exchange for something we cannot quite name. We have, most of us, lost the vocabulary for this exchange. We talk about cardio. We talk about step counts. We talk about whether a neighborhood is walkable, which is, in its own way, a fine phrase. None of it touches the practice.

The practice, when it works, looks like this. You step out of your door at a particular hour. You go in a particular direction. You do not, today, take the most efficient route. You take the less efficient route, which is almost always the more interesting one. You walk for forty minutes. You look at things. You do not, mostly, photograph them. You let them pass through you instead, into whatever part of the mind holds the colour of peeling paint on a fire escape in October.

## the same neighborhood, again

There is a quiet truth in the phrase same neighborhood. We are taught, by every travel magazine, that the value of a city is in its novelty, in its hidden corners, in the parts we have not yet seen. This is true, partly. But it has a bias, the way most travel writing has a bias. The bias is toward the new. The bias is against the known. The bias is, I think, wrong.

Walking the same neighborhood slowly is its own kind of travel. It is a travel into a place that is changing while you watch. It is a travel into the way your own perception of a place changes, year by year, season by season, walk by walk. The same block in spring is not the same block in autumn. The same block in rain is not the same block in sun. The same block, on a Tuesday morning in February, when the bakery is closed and the dog walker is the only other person outside, is not the same block on a Saturday afternoon in June, when the street is full of voices and the windows are open. You see all of these blocks, eventually, by walking slowly enough, often enough, in the same place.

There is also, in the same neighborhood, a slow accumulation of acquaintances. The man who runs the newsstand on the corner, who waves at you by the third week. The woman who walks a small white dog at the same hour you walk, who once stopped to ask if you were lost. The barista at the cafe you pass but do not enter, who has begun to nod at you, even though you have never bought anything. These are not friendships, exactly. They are something gentler. They are the texture of a city as experienced by someone who has decided to live in it, instead of passing through it. They are what a city actually is, when you get below the postcard.

I have come to think that this is one of the great secrets of city life, and one we have lost. We move too often. We leave neighborhoods before we know them. We move to new neighborhoods and try to know them by going to their proper restaurants and their proper coffee shops, which is one way of knowing a place but not, I think, the most useful way. The most useful way is to walk, slowly, past the same fire escape, four hundred times, and to notice, on the four hundredth time, that the plant on the bottom step is, finally, in bloom.

## the attention economy, in reverse

There is a phrase that has become inescapable in the last decade, which is attention economy. The phrase is mostly used by people who are trying to sell you back your attention. The premise is that your attention is being harvested, by apps and platforms and a thousand small algorithmic systems, and that you are being impoverished by this harvesting. The premise is correct. The prescription is usually wrong.

The prescription, in most books on the topic, is to log off. To delete the apps. To meditate. To buy the journal. To buy the watch that reminds you to breathe. There is, of course, value in all of this. There is also, I think, a quieter prescription, which is to walk, slowly, in your own neighborhood, and to look at things that are not on a screen. The screen is, in the end, the issue. The screen is what is competing for your attention. The walk is the cure.

A walking cure does not, technically, exist. But the practice of walking, slowly, through a known neighborhood, has something of the same effect as the cure. It returns attention to its original owner. It reminds you that you have eyes, and that your eyes can do something other than scan. It reminds you that the world has texture, that has texture you can see and feel and smell, and that none of this texture requires a login.

The attention economy is real. The cure for it is not, mostly, digital. The cure for it is analog. The walk is the analog cure. There is no app for it. There is no subscription. There is no metric. There is just the walk, and the street, and the eye that has remembered, after a few minutes, what it was like to look at something without performing the looking.

I am not against phones, exactly. I am against phones in the hand, on the walk. The phone in the pocket is fine. The phone in the hand is the end of the practice. The phone in the hand is, also, a kind of admission. It is an admission that the world in front of you is not enough. It is an admission that, without the screen, you would be bored. Boredom, on a walk, is the point. Boredom is the door. Walk through it, and you will find, on the other side, the same neighborhood you have always lived in, slightly more interesting than you had allowed yourself to notice.

## the small sightings

There are certain small sightings that only happen to the walker. The sighting of a cat behind a curtain, in a third-floor window, who has been watching you from the same angle for years. The sighting of a particular pigeon, with a slightly bent toe, who has been frequenting the same corner for as long as you have been frequenting it. The sighting of a child on a bicycle, learning to ride without training wheels, who pedals past you with the expression of a person who has just discovered something about the world.

You will not photograph any of these. You cannot, in most cases. The moment is too fast, or too small, or too quiet for the camera. The moment is, in any case, not really for the camera. The moment is for you, the walker, who saw it, and who will remember it, in a way photographs do not allow you to remember things, because the photograph has done the remembering for you. The unaided memory is more fragile than the photograph, and also, somehow, more durable. The photograph forgets. The memory does not.

I have a friend who has kept, for thirty years, a small notebook in which she writes down one observation from her morning walk. Not a journal, exactly. Just one observation. The colour of the sky on a particular Tuesday. The name on a particular doorbell. The way a particular dog greeted a particular squirrel. The notebook is not, in any obvious sense, useful. It is also the most read book in her apartment. She reads it, sometimes, on Sunday mornings, with coffee. It is, she says, the closest thing she has to a record of having lived in this city, in this apartment, on this block, for these years. The notebook is the proof. The walk was the practice. The small sightings were the material.

You do not need to keep the notebook. You do not need to write down the sightings. You need only to keep walking, and to keep seeing, and to keep letting the small sightings accumulate, in the part of the mind that holds the colour of peeling paint on a fire escape in October. The mind will hold them. The mind has, in fact, been waiting for them. The mind has been waiting, for years, for you to slow down enough to receive them.

## the walk as citizenship

I want to argue, finally, for the walk as citizenship. The walker is the citizen the city was designed for. The walker is the citizen who ratifies the design. The walker is the citizen who knows, by walking, whether the curb cuts are wide enough, whether the street trees are tall enough, whether the crosswalk lights give you enough time, whether the bench at the bus stop actually faces something worth looking at.

The walker is also the citizen who keeps the city alive in a way the driver does not. The driver experiences the city as a sequence of routes, optimised for getting from one place to another. The walker experiences the city as a place, with people in it, and windows open onto it, and small dramas playing out on its corners. The walker is the one who notices that the laundromat has changed hands, that the bookshop has closed, that the new cafe on the corner has painted its shutters a colour that does not quite work. The walker is the one who mourns, briefly, who knows, who adjusts. The walker is the one who, by walking, keeps the city the kind of place in which walking is possible.

This is not activism. It is something gentler than activism. It is the daily practice of being the kind of person for whom the city was designed, and by being that person, of reminding the city, with every step, what it was supposed to be. The walk is not, in the end, an escape from the city. The walk is the city, experienced at the only speed at which the city is actually itself. The walk is the city, returned to itself, by the citizen who has slowed down enough to see it.

So walk. Tomorrow morning, set the alarm a little early. Put on the most comfortable shoes you own. Do not bring the phone, or bring it in your pocket, and pretend it is a stone. Step out of your door. Walk. Notice the plant. Notice the cat. Notice the colour of the sky, which is different today from yesterday. Walk for forty minutes. Walk back inside. Notice, by the end of the day, that the day has been slightly better than it would have been otherwise. This is the practice. This is the entire practice. This is enough.
