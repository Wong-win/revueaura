We had, in our working life, a lamp argument that went on for longer than it should have. It was not the loud kind of argument, the kind where a thing is said and cannot be unsaid. It was the low kind, the kind that happens at ten o'clock on a weeknight to two tired people who both need the same forty pages finished, and where the object at the centre of the disagreement is a small piece of hardware that costs less than dinner. We had, in fact, before the flat we live in now, three lamps that were each, at one time or another, the reading lamp. We had, in our experience, one more reading lamp than any household needs, and it took us several years to understand that the third one was not generosity. It was indecision, in the kind of small quiet way one is often indecisive about the small things that end up mattering.

The reading lamp is, in our experience, the most argued-about object in a house, and this is because it is the only object in the house that two people ask to use at the same hour, in the same room, with different bodies and different expectations of what a page should look like. The kettle is not contested. The radio is not contested. The reading lamp is contested every evening, quietly.

## the lamp and the argument

The argument, once we had it laid out in front of us, was not really about colour temperature or about the price of the thing, whatever we said at the time. The argument was about what a lamp is for. There are, in our experience, two lamps that can stand on the same table and be doing entirely different work, and the confusion between them is the source of most domestic lighting dissatisfaction.

The first lamp lights a book. It makes a small bright pool, no wider than the width of a page, and it places that pool precisely where the page is, and it does almost nothing else. This is, in the strict sense, the better lamp for reading. The eye is not fighting a bright room. There is no glare off a pale page at an awkward angle, because the light arrives from over the shoulder of the book rather than from the far side of the room. The rest of the house can be dark, and the dark is doing you a favour, in the small quiet way that darkness always does in the last hour of the day.

The second lamp lights a room. It throws light up and outward, washes the wall behind the chair, softens the ceiling, and turns the corner of the flat into a small warm country with the reader inside it. This is, in the strict sense, the better lamp for being in. Almost nobody reads for an hour beneath one of these with any great success, because the page ends up at the edge of the pool, and the edges of pools are, in our experience, where reading goes to die.

We had, in the old flat, both kinds, and we had never, for all the years we owned them, in the same lamp. That was the mistake. We had a lamp for reading in the corner and a lamp for the room in the ceiling, acquired separately over several years, in the way one acquires such things — without ever asking the one question that would have joined them.

That question, in our experience, is whether one lamp can do both, and the answer we have arrived at, slowly and somewhat reluctantly, is yes, but only if the lamp is designed around the pool rather than around the bulb. Almost everything else about a lamp, the stem, the base, the switch, the shade, the price, is decoration on top of a single geometric fact: a patch of light, of a certain size and warmth, landing on a certain surface.

## the pool, and the radius

A pool of light has a radius, and the radius is the whole argument. Too small and you keep moving the book, minute by minute, through the hour, a small restless negotiation you are not conscious of until the shoulder begins to ache. Too large and you lose the page in a general glow, and the page, in a general glow, is not a page any more. It is a rectangle of slightly more brightness in a rectangle of slightly less brightness, and the eye will not settle.

The useful pool, in our experience, is roughly the size of a folded sheet of paper, maybe a little wider, and it should sit on the lower half of the page rather than the upper half, because the upper half is where the eye moves most. It should be soft at the rim. A hard rim, a visible circle of light with a shadow at the edge of it, makes the page look like a stage with a spotlight on it, and after twenty minutes the reader becomes aware of the mechanism rather than the sentence.

Getting the pool where you want it is almost entirely a matter of the shade, and this is where the cheap lamps fail. A shade that is too shallow and too opaque does one job well — it makes a bright disc — and the disc lands where the physics of the shade send it, which is usually on the far side of the book, in the reader's eyes. A shade that is too deep and too translucent does the opposite, and sends light everywhere, so that the page is lit as evenly as a table, which sounds like a virtue and is in practice the death of reading, because a page lit evenly is a page with no hierarchy, and a page with no hierarchy is a wall.

We learned, in the end, to think of the shade as a small piece of architecture with a job, and to adjust the lamp until the pool landed where the reading happens, which is a moving target, because people do not hold books still for an hour.

## the colour of the hour

Then there is the temperature, which is the thing people argue about most loudly and understand least. The bulbs we are sold now come in a small numbered range, and the numbers mean something fairly simple: the lower the number, the warmer the light, and the warmer the light, the more the room seems to be made of wood and cloth rather than of metal and glass. Around twenty-seven hundred kelvin is the pale yellow of an old bulb, the colour of a fire seen from a distance. Around four thousand is the flat neutral of an office.

For a reading lamp we have settled, in our experience, on the warm end of the range, and not because warm light is intrinsically more pleasant. It is because the warm end is the end that agrees with the hour. A cool bulb at ten o'clock in the evening is a small act of institutional authority, in the kind of small quiet way that a room full of screens is always reminding us of things we had stopped thinking about. A warm bulb says that this part of the day is not the working part. It says the day has ended and something smaller has begun.

The more useful discovery, and the one we did not expect, is that a single bulb in a single fixed colour is nearly always wrong, and that the small dimmer on the wall or the little knob at the base of the lamp matters more than the choice of colour. The same bulb turned down to a quarter is a different kind of light from the same bulb turned up to full. It is smaller, warmer in feeling, and it throws a shorter pool. And the honest truth of a reading lamp, in our experience, is that its brightest setting is used for about four minutes — finding the page, putting the book down — and then it is turned down and left there for the remaining forty.

There is also the slow drift of the bulbs themselves, which nobody warns you about. A bulb that was warm when it was new is warmer and dimmer two years later, and if the lamp is on a dimmer already, the two changes compound. We replace our bulbs, in our experience, not because they fail but because the room has changed colour without our permission.

## the objects that gather around it

A reading lamp is, in the end, a small piece of infrastructure, and infrastructure attracts things. We had, in the old flat, a corner where a lamp stood and where, over eight years, a small archaeology assembled itself without anybody deciding to assemble it. There was the book, of course, and then a second book, the one you are not reading but are reading instead, and a third that had been there since the previous flat and could not be thrown away with a clear conscience.

There was a glass of water, which in our experience is the single most important object in the room, and which we have learned to place where the lamp's spill makes it visible without it being lit. There were reading glasses that had lost their case. There was a small tin of the sort of thing that accumulates near a chair — a spare button, a folded receipt, a foreign coin — which we have never once looked at in daylight and have never once thrown away. There was, for about a year, a plant that survived entirely because it was in the light.

What none of these objects have in common is function. What they have in common is that the lamp is the reason they are there. The lamp is a claim on a corner, and everything that accumulates in the corner is partly the lamp's doing. This is, in our experience, the thing that makes a reading corner different from a chair in a room. A chair in a room is furniture. A chair under a reading lamp, with the pool landing on it and the objects gathering under it, is a place.

## what we changed

When we moved, we did the obvious thing first and it was a mistake. We moved the lamp from the corner to the centre of the wall, because the corner was dark and the centre was not, and we left it there for a few months, and the room felt, the entire time, like a room with a lamp in it rather than a room someone reads in.

What we changed, in the end, was not one thing but six or seven small things, and none of them were expensive. We moved the lamp back to the corner, low, at the height of the shoulder rather than the head, so that the pool falls onto the lap and the book and not onto the face. We replaced the shade with a slightly deeper one, which cut the pool down and softened its rim, and the difference between the two shades was, in the small quiet way, the difference between a room that is lit and a room that is being read in.

We changed the bulbs to the warm end of the range, and we put a dimmer on the switch, which is the single change that other people, when we mention it, seem to consider excessive. We moved the chair six inches to the left, so that the pool no longer landed on the join of the page. We took four objects off the table, and it felt, for a few weeks, like the corner had been emptied for something that was never coming, and then it felt like the corner had been cleared, which is a different thing entirely.

And we started turning the lamp on before we sat down. This is the smallest change of all and possibly the most consequential. A room with a lamp already on when you arrive is a room that has been expecting you. A room where you must switch the lamp on after you have sat down requires an act, and the act, however small, delays the reading by the length of the reaching and the click.

We did not, in fact, buy anything new that mattered. We moved four objects and changed two bulbs and one shade and one switch, and in our experience the room now does something at nine in the evening that it did not do before, which is that it holds still.

## the small quiet conclusion

We had, in our working life, the idea that a lamp was a thing you turned on, and a room was a thing that was either lit or not lit. It took us several years, and a few disagreements about a small pool of light on a page, to understand that the two are a single subject, and that a room at nine in the evening is made mostly of one small patch of warm light falling on one small surface, and that the quality of that patch, and where it lands, decides more about the hour than anything else in the flat.

The warmth, in the end, is not in the lamp. We had believed, for a long time, that warmth was a property of the bulb, and it is not. Warmth is a property of the wall the lamp happens to be pointing at. A lamp that throws light up at a wall makes the wall glow, and the wall makes the room warm, and the room makes the corner warm, and the corner makes the hour warm, and this whole chain runs on nothing more than the size and the placement of a pool of light.

We have, in our experience, come to think of the reading lamp as the most modest and the most architectural object in the flat. It asks for one job, at one hour, in one corner. And in the small quiet way one does not expect of an object that costs less than dinner, it decides, more than the rug or the paint or the chair, what the last hour of the day feels like — and it will keep deciding that, patiently and without any opinion of its own, for as long as we leave it there.
