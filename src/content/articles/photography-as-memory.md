There are, by most estimates, something like four trillion photographs taken every year now. That is a number that the mind refuses to hold — it is somewhere between the number of stars in the Milky Way and the number of grains of sand on a small beach, and it is, in either case, vastly larger than anything the species has ever produced in the way of images before. The cave paintings at Lascaux, the painted vases of classical Greece, the formal portraits of the Dutch Golden Age: all of it, together, amounts to a small fraction of what is uploaded to a single photo-sharing service in an afternoon. We are, for the first time in human history, a culture that produces more images per day than it can possibly look at in a year.

The question that has been quietly forming in the back of our minds, as we have watched this happen, is not really about photography. It is about memory. The phone camera, we have come to think, is not primarily a piece of camera equipment. It is the most-used diary in human history — a kind of extended autobiographical notebook that most of us keep without quite realizing that we are keeping it. We photograph our breakfasts not because the breakfasts are interesting but because the photographing is, in some subtle way, the remembering. The image becomes a small anchor for a moment that we would otherwise, almost certainly, forget. The question is what to do with all of it.

## the camera as an extension of attention

There is an old idea, from the philosophy of memory, that to remember something is not to have a recording of it but to be able, on some future occasion, to reconstruct it. Memory, in this older sense, is a kind of small act of imagination; it is the mind's way of rehearsing a past that no longer exists. Photographs, by contrast, are not reconstructions. They are, almost by definition, pieces of the past preserved more or less intact. They relieve the mind of the work of remembering. They substitute, for the small effort of recollection, the much smaller effort of looking.

This is, in many ways, an enormous gift. We have, in the camera roll of even a modest smartphone, a richer archive of our own life than any previous generation could have assembled in a lifetime of keeping diaries. The first day of school. The dog as a puppy. The grandmother on her last visit. The kitchen on the morning of the wedding. The window of the apartment one has just left. These are not, in any sense, important images, individually. They are, in aggregate, something like a life.

And yet the gift comes with a small, almost hidden cost. The philosopher Roland Barthes, in his small book on photography, observed that the photograph of a person one loves becomes, in time, a kind of second body — a separate version of them that floats free in the world and is, in some unsettling sense, also the person. The same logic applies, on a larger scale, to the photograph of a moment. The moment becomes a thing one possesses rather than a thing one has had. We have come to suspect, in our own lives, that there is something almost sad about the way in which the proliferation of photographs has changed our relationship to having been somewhere. There is, increasingly, a small gap between the moment and the photograph of the moment. The moment, sometimes, feels as though it is being held at one remove. The photograph is the souvenir; the memory is, in some quiet way, the souvenir of the souvenir.

## the album that does not exist

What is interesting, in our own case and in the case of most people we know, is how few of the four trillion photographs ever get looked at again. They live, instead, in the long quiet of a phone's storage, in the slightly less quiet of a cloud archive, in the absolute quiet of an external backup drive one has not plugged in for two years. They are, in aggregate, an enormous archive that is also, in some important sense, almost completely inaccessible. There is no time, in a life, to look at forty thousand photographs. There is no attention, in a week, to spend more than a few minutes revisiting last summer. The archive grows faster than the attention that could ever give it shape.

We have started, in our own small way, to think of this as a kind of slow private crisis. The diary that the camera is supposed to be keeping is, in fact, mostly unwritten — or rather, written in a language we cannot read, in a hand we do not recognize, at a pace we cannot follow. The most-used diary in human history is also, in many cases, the least-read. It is a vast library to which we are, in practice, returning the books without ever having checked them out.

The traditional answer to this, in the photographic tradition, has been the album. The album is the technology of selection. It is the small, intentional act of choosing, from among the many possible photographs, the few that one will keep in a form one can revisit. It is the difference between a pile of unsorted negatives and the careful two-volume set of a grandparent's life. The album is the slow second pass — the moment at which the camera's eye is joined, briefly, by the mind's. The album is what turns the archive into a memory.

## a slower kind of keeping

What we have started to do, over the last year or so, is to print. Not many — a dozen or so a year, mostly from a single trip or a single season — and to put them in a small wooden box on a shelf in the living room. The photographs do not, in any sense, represent the year. They are not even, in many cases, the best photographs of the year. They are the photographs that, when we come across them again, return a moment to us in a way that the screen never quite does. They are the photographs that have made it past the small second pass of selection and are, now, allowed to exist as physical objects in a physical room.

This is, we recognize, an unfashionable thing to do. Printing photographs, in 2026, is a little like writing letters: it is the kind of slow, deliberate, slightly old-fashioned act that the world has been persuaded, over the last twenty years, is no longer necessary. And yet we have found, in our own case, that the act of printing has changed, in some small but real way, our relationship to the camera itself. There is a small hesitation, now, before the shutter. The question is no longer *will I want this* but *will I want to print this*. It is the question that used to be asked, in the era of film, by every photographer in the world; it is the question that the digital camera, in its original promise, was meant to make unnecessary. We have come to suspect, gently, that the question was more useful than we were led to believe.

The printed photograph, when one takes it out of the box a year after the trip, does something that the screen photograph does not. It asks one, gently, to remember. It does not just show the moment; it returns one to the moment. The light on the wall. The smell of the kitchen. The grandmother's laugh. The dog, that summer, before she got old. The screen photograph, by contrast, tends to register as a record. It is, in some sense, evidence that the moment happened. It is not, in the same sense, the moment itself. The archive has been preserved; the memory, somehow, has not been.

What we are arguing for, in the end, is not against the camera. The camera is one of the great instruments of the century, and we are, like everyone else, heavy users of it. What we are arguing for is the slow second act — the small, deliberate, slightly out-of-fashion act of choosing, from among the four trillion, the few that one is actually going to live with. The camera, we have come to think, is the easy part. The choosing is the work. The choosing is what turns a pile of photographs into a life.

There is, we have come to think, something almost appropriate in the slowness of the photograph. The image ages. The paper yellows, gently, at the edges. The faces of the people in it age, far faster than the photograph itself. The grandmother gets older; the dog gets older; we, holding the photograph, get older. The photograph is, in some quiet way, a record of the rate at which time has been passing. It is a small piece of evidence, in a physical archive, that one was once, briefly, young. There is no app that does this. There is no cloud service that does this. There is only the photograph in the wooden box, and the slow, slightly melancholy, completely ordinary pleasure of taking it out, every now and then, and looking at it for a few minutes in the afternoon light. That is enough. After several years of taking many thousands of photographs, we have begun to think it is, in fact, almost all there is.
