The first time I rode a bicycle through a city I thought I knew, I realised I had not known it at all. I had known it as a pedestrian, which is to say I had known it as a sequence of short walks between destinations. I had known it as a driver, which is to say I had known it as a series of obstacles and one-way streets. I had not known it as itself, the way you know a sentence when you actually read it instead of scanning it for keywords. The bicycle is the speed at which a city finally makes sense.

There is something almost magical about this. You leave your building at the corner you have lived on for years. You turn down a street you have walked a hundred times. But at bicycle pace — which is faster than walking, slower than driving, and crucially, at eye level with the buildings — the city becomes a different thing. You notice the carved capitals above the bank that you never looked up to see. You notice the small park tucked behind the church that is invisible from the main avenue. You notice the way a particular tree, planted eighty years ago, has grown into the shape of the building behind it, so that you cannot really see one without the other.

## The Speed of Reading

A car goes too fast. A pedestrian goes too slow. Both are honest speeds for what they are doing, but neither is the speed at which a city reveals itself. The car is the speed of commerce, of arrival, of not-quite-being-there. The pedestrian is the speed of intimacy, of the small details. The bicycle is the speed of reading. It is fast enough that you cover meaningful ground in a single outing. It is slow enough that you can register what is on the ground. It is, in the literal sense, the pace at which the city was meant to be inhabited.

This is why the great cycling cities are also, almost always, the great walking cities. Copenhagen. Amsterdam. Kyoto. Lyon. The cities where you can move through them on a bicycle without fear are the cities that have not lost their human scale, even if they have grown enormous. The cities that have prioritised the bicycle, even a little, are the cities that have decided to remain legible to their inhabitants. A city that is illegible — that can only be crossed by car, that can only be entered by highway — is a city that has begun to become a postcard of itself.

I am not interested, here, in the policy arguments about bike lanes and parking. I am interested in the experience. And the experience is that the bicycle is the only vehicle in which you are simultaneously a part of the city and in motion through it. In a car, you are sealed off from the city, looking at it through glass. On foot, you are too slow to feel your own movement through it. On the bicycle, you are part of the texture. You smell the bread bakery. You hear the children in the playground. You feel the small wind that the city's own architecture creates. You are, briefly, in the city instead of looking at it.

## The Bicycle As a Kind of Literacy

There is a writer I admire who once said that the bicycle taught her to read landscape. Before she started cycling, the countryside was something she passed through on a train. After, it became something she could parse. She learned to recognise the difference between a chalk stream and a clay-bottomed river, between a hedgerow oak and a parkland oak, between a ridge path and a valley path. The bicycle gave her a pace that allowed these distinctions to register.

I think the same is true of cities, with a different set of distinctions. Cycling teaches you to read a city. It teaches you which neighbourhoods are flat and which are hilly, which streets have trees and which are bare, which squares are living rooms for the surrounding blocks and which are leftover spaces. It teaches you where the city breathes, and where it holds its breath. It teaches you the gradient, the wind, the rhythm of the lights. These are not things you learn by reading about them. They are things you learn by being in them, repeatedly, at the right pace.

After a few years of cycling in a city, you have a relationship with it that is qualitatively different from the relationship of the driver or the pedestrian. The driver knows the fastest route. The pedestrian knows the most pleasant route. The cyclist knows both, and a third thing, which is the route that feels right — neither fast nor pleasant exactly, but right, in some way that has to do with the sun, the wind, the particular kind of attention you brought to the day. This is a kind of knowledge that cannot be acquired any other way. It is the knowledge of someone who lives in a place.

## The Body in the City

There is also, of course, the body. Cycling is a physical activity, and you feel it after a few years. The knees. The lower back. The hands, which start to develop calluses on the palms from years of gripping handlebars. These are not complaints. They are the body's honest record of a life lived partly in motion. The cyclist's body is a particular body, marked in particular ways, and the marks are mostly good. The cyclist tends to have stronger legs than the driver, stronger lungs than the commuter on the subway, better balance than the runner. The cyclist's body is, in a small way, a body that has been kept in conversation with the world.

I have come to think of this as one of the great unspoken gifts of the bicycle. The cyclist's body is a body that knows where it has been. You can close your eyes and think of any of your regular routes, and your body will remember the slight rise after the bridge, the cobblestones on the corner, the long flat stretch along the canal. The body remembers the city the way the body remembers anything it has done often enough. The body is, in this sense, a kind of personal cartography. The cyclist's body is a map of the city, written in muscle and breath.

This is, I think, one reason cycling is so beloved by people who love their cities. It is not just that the bicycle is convenient, or cheap, or good for the planet. It is that the bicycle writes the city into your body. You become, in a small way, the city. Its hills are your hills. Its weather is your weather. Its streets are the streets you know in your hands and feet and calves. There is no other way to acquire this kind of belonging. You can only acquire it by moving through, slowly enough, often enough, that the place becomes part of you.

## The Quiet Civic Practice

Cycling, in a city, is also a small political act. This is not a thing I would have said ten years ago, and I say it now with some care. Cycling is not, in itself, a politics. But it is, in many cities, a quiet vote against the dominance of the car, and a quiet vote for the kind of city in which it is pleasant to live without one. Every cyclist on the street is a small argument that the street could be a different kind of street.

I do not want to overstate this. The vast majority of cyclists I know are not activists. They are just people who have discovered that the bicycle is a good way to get around, and who are quietly, persistently, doing it. They are not making speeches. They are not chaining themselves to lampposts. They are simply riding to work, riding to dinner, riding home. The accumulation of these small acts is what eventually changes a city. A bike lane is built because enough people are riding. A traffic-calming project goes through because enough people are walking. A street is closed to cars on Sundays because enough people asked. None of this would happen without the quiet daily practice of the cyclists themselves.

I think there is something beautiful about this. The bicycle is, among other things, an instrument of slow civic change. You do not need to win an argument. You just need to keep riding. The city bends, slowly, around the practice. The streets get a little more hospitable. The air gets a little cleaner. The neighbourhoods get a little quieter. None of this is dramatic. All of it is real.

So if you live in a city, and you do not yet cycle, I would gently suggest you try. You do not need a particular bicycle. You do not need particular clothes. You do not need to commute, or to be an athlete, or to be any particular kind of person. You just need a bicycle, and a destination that is close enough to ride to, and a Saturday morning with no particular plan. You will discover a city you did not know you lived in. You will be surprised by how much there is to see, once you are moving through it at the speed the city was built for.
