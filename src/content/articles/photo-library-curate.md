The phone informed us, on a recent Tuesday afternoon, that we had taken 9,847 photographs in the past year. It told us this in the gentle, slightly disapproving tone that software uses when it wants us to do something about a number. There was, it suggested, a project available — to clean up our photo library, to delete the duplicates, to organize what remained into something more navigable. The suggestion, like most suggestions from our devices, was framed as a service.

We did not, in the end, take the suggestion. What we did instead, over the following weeks, was think rather carefully about what a photo library actually is. The phone treats it as a kind of inbox — an accumulating pile, sortable, searchable, deletable. We had begun, slowly, to think of it as something closer to an archive — a record, however imperfect, of the small particular life we had been living while we were not paying attention.

The difference between an inbox and an archive, it turns out, is the difference between a thing one maintains and a thing one keeps. The inbox is meant to be emptied. The archive is meant to be carried.

## the phone and the shoebox

Our grandmother, who would now be older than we can comfortably imagine, kept her photographs in a shoebox under the bed. There were perhaps two hundred of them in total, accumulated across sixty years of marriage and parenthood and emigration and the small ordinary travails of being alive in the twentieth century. They were not in any particular order. They were not captioned. Many of them were slightly creased. A few of them were, by any reasonable standard, badly composed.

But she knew what was in them. She knew, without looking, that the third one from the top was the photograph of her older sister on the day of her wedding, and that the one in the back, the one that was a little dog-eared, was of the house in the old country before it was sold. She could pick up the box and put her hand on a specific image and tell you, in some detail, what was happening in the room at the moment the shutter clicked.

The phone cannot do this. The phone, with its 9,847 images and its scroll and its search bar, can find the image of her older sister — we have checked, the algorithm is correct — but it cannot make the finding feel like anything. The image is delivered, in the same flat thumbnail, with the same metadata, in the same second the search completes, alongside a dozen other images of her older sister and an unrelated ad for a wedding photographer in our zip code. The memory has been, in some technical sense, preserved. It has not, in any human sense, been *kept*.

This is, we have come to think, the actual cost of the phone camera. Not that we take too many pictures — though we do — but that we take them without keeping them. The shoebox was small enough to be carried, and so it was carried, and so it became, over decades, a kind of portable biography. The phone is too large to be carried in any meaningful sense, and so the images on it remain, for all practical purposes, uncarried. They are stored. They are not kept.

## the slow project

What we have begun, then, is a slow project. It is not, we want to be clear, a digital project. The point is precisely to take the photographs off the phone, where they exist in some technical state between remembered and forgotten, and to put them somewhere where they have to be *chosen*.

We print a few each week. We keep them in a real folder, a soft leather one we bought at a stationery shop in a town we used to live in. We write, on the back of each print, in pencil, a small note — the year, the place, the name of the person if there is one. The project does not have a deadline. There are 9,847 of them, after all, and we will die before we are done. The project is, in that sense, generational. It is the kind of thing one does not so much as start as simply enter into.

There is a strange pleasure in this work, a pleasure we did not expect and cannot entirely explain. It has to do, we think, with the act of choosing. To print a photograph is to say: this one mattered. This one is worth the small cost of ink and paper. This one I would like to be able to find, in fifty years, in a folder on a shelf, without having to scroll through nine thousand other photographs to do so. The choosing is, in some quiet way, an act of attention. It is the photographic equivalent of underlining a sentence in a book.

What we have noticed is that the photographs we choose to print are not, by and large, the photographs the algorithm would have us choose. We do not print the ones with the most faces in them, or the ones taken in the most expensive places, or the ones that look the most like the photographs other people take. We print the ones that mean something to us, even when the meaning is small, even when the photograph is technically bad. The kitchen counter at 7 a.m. in February, when the light came through the window at a particular angle for a single week and then never came that way again. The dog asleep on the rug. The handwriting on a note that we have since lost. The back of our mother's head, photographed without her noticing, on a walk we took together one summer and have not yet talked about properly.

These are not, in any commercial sense, good photographs. They are not the kind of photographs one posts. They are, however, the kind of photographs one keeps.

## the folder as autobiography

What we are slowly assembling, we have begun to realize, is something like an autobiography. Not the public kind, the kind one would write for a publisher, but the small private kind — the kind one assembles in a folder over a lifetime, the kind that gets discovered, eventually, by someone who is trying to understand who one was.

There is a tradition of this in the older art of photography. The great project of someone like Vivian Maier was not the single image but the accumulating body of images — the thousands of rolls of film, the shoeboxes in storage lockers, the slow portrait of a life taken by the person who lived it. The tradition has not, despite the phone, entirely disappeared. People still keep, in folders and albums and boxes, the photographs that matter to them. The difference is mostly that the boxes have gotten much bigger, and much harder to carry.

What the phone has done, in its way, is to take the shoebox and turn it into a hard drive. The shoebox was small enough that one could pick it up and look through it. The hard drive is too large. The hard drive is searchable, but the search is a different thing from the looking. The looking is what makes the memory. The looking is what turns a flat thumbnail into a Sunday afternoon in 2004, when the light was a particular way, and the cat was young, and one's grandmother was still alive, and one did not, at the time, know that any of it mattered.

## the question of curation

What we have come to believe, after a few months of the project, is that the question of the photo library is not a question of storage. Storage is easy. Storage is cheap. Storage is something the technology companies have already solved. The question is a question of *curation* — of deciding, image by image, which ones we want to carry with us.

This is, in a way, an old question. It is the question one used to ask, at the kitchen table, while sorting through the prints from the holiday. Which ones do we want to put in the album? Which ones do we want to put in the drawer? Which ones are we going to send to the cousins? The question was small, and the answers were small, and the smallness of the answers was, we have come to think, the entire point. The act of choosing is what makes the photograph a memory. Without the choosing, it is just a file.

We are aware, as we say this, that we are not going to convince anyone to print their photographs. The world has moved on. The phones are good now. The cameras are good now. The storage is endless. The cost of a print is, in any economic sense, irrational. None of this is wrong, exactly. It is just, we have come to think, slightly sad.

The shoebox under the bed was not a sophisticated piece of archival technology. It was, by any reasonable standard, a piece of junk. But it was a piece of junk that held, in some intimate sense, the life of a person who had decided to keep it. The phone, for all its sophistication, does not do this. The phone is a place where images go to be forgotten. The folder on the shelf is a place where they go to be remembered.

It is, in the end, a small difference. But we have come to think it is the difference.
