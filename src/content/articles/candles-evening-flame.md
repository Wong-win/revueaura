There is a moment, near the end of most evenings I spend at home, when I light a candle. It is not a ritual I planned. It happened slowly, across years, until one day I noticed that no evening felt finished without it. The candle is usually small — a votive left over from a holiday, or a slim taper that has been in a drawer since the last dinner I hosted, or one of those thick pillar candles a friend once gave me in a glass jar with a wooden lid. It is rarely expensive. It is rarely the centerpiece of anything. But the room, the moment the flame is set down and catches, becomes a different room. The walls recede. The corners soften. The lamp that had been doing the job all evening — the bright lamp, the working lamp, the lamp of the part of the night when someone might still be reading — quietly steps aside, and the candle takes over, and the house, for the rest of the evening, is lit by something older than electricity.

I think a candle is the cheapest interior renovation in any house. Not in dollars, although it is also that. A candle costs less than a pillow. A candle costs less than a single bunch of flowers from a market. A candle costs, in any reasonable accounting, almost nothing. And yet the candle changes a room more thoroughly than any piece of furniture I have ever bought. Furniture rearranges a room. A candle rearranges the light, and the light is what the room actually is. A room that has good light is a different room than the same room with bad light, even when nothing else about it has changed. The candle, for the hour it burns, gives the room the kind of light it does not usually have — light that flickers, light that warms, light that makes the people in the room look slightly more like themselves than the overhead lamp ever quite managed to.

## The Light That Used to Be the Only Light

It is easy to forget, in a house wired for electricity, that the candle was, for most of human history, the only light a room had. People ate by candle. They read by candle. They wrote letters by candle. They courted by candle. They had children by candle, sometimes by the light of a single candle held by the midwife. They argued by candle. They grieved by candle. They sat with the dying by candle, and they sat with the dead by candle, and they carried candles to the grave. The candle was not, in those centuries, an accent. It was the only source of light the house had, and the room was organized around the candle the way a kitchen today is organized around the stove.

What that means, I think, is that the candle is older than any other object in most living rooms. The sofa is a hundred years old, maybe. The lamp is forty. The candle is several thousand. The candle is the one piece of furniture in the room whose design has not changed in centuries because it did not need to change. The shape is right. The wick is right. The wax is right. The flame is right. Everything else has been iterated on, redesigned, replaced, reconsidered. The candle is the same object it was when the Romans used it. We trust it. We light it without thinking. We expect it to do exactly what it did in the room it was lit in three thousand years ago.

This is why the candle feels different from any other light source in the house. The lamp is a piece of technology. The lamp could, in principle, break. The lamp requires a bulb. The lamp needs to be plugged in. The candle is fire, and fire has been doing this job since before there were houses, and the room knows it, even when we do not.

## The Hour a Candle Names

A candle, more than almost any object in the house, names a specific hour. The overhead lamp names no hour. The overhead lamp is on at six in the evening and at six in the morning and at every hour in between. The candle names the hour when the day has ended. The candle names the hour when there is nothing left to do. The candle names the hour when the lamp is too much and the dark is too little. The candle is the light of the very last part of the evening, when the work is finished and the dinner is finished and the cleaning is finished and the household has finally, gently, come to rest.

This is why a candle at dinner feels different from a candle in the middle of the afternoon. The dinner candle is part of the meal. The afternoon candle is, somehow, slightly off. The dinner candle is the candle the room is built for. It signals to everyone at the table that the meal has moved past the feeding part and into the talking part, the slow part, the part that takes an hour and a glass of wine and is the part of the evening most people remember the next morning.

I have come to think that this is the actual work of the candle. It is not to provide light. A lamp provides more light, more efficiently, more reliably, and at less expense. The candle's work is to provide a transition. The candle marks the line between the productive part of the evening and the resting part of the evening. Once the candle is lit, the day is over. Not officially, not legally, not by the clock. But the candle has said so. The candle has named the hour. The candle has set the room down.

## What the Flame Does to the People in the Room

There is a small thing a candle does to the people in the room that I have not seen any other object do. The candle makes the people in the room soften. People under overhead light are people who are working, or who are about to be working, or who are aware of being looked at. People under a candle are people who have been allowed to stop. The candle removes, gently, the slight vigilance that overhead lighting imposes. The candle replaces it with the warmth that the small flame gives off, and the warmth, more than the light, is what the people in the room feel.

I notice this most on the nights when someone has come to dinner. The dinner starts under the bright light, because the bright light is the light of cooking and serving and clearing. By the time the plates are put down, the overhead light has been turned off. By the time the second glass of wine is poured, the only light in the room is the candle, and one lamp in the corner that has been left on because the room would otherwise be too dark to find the door. The people at the table, under the candle, are quieter. The conversations are longer. The laughter is lower. The table feels closer. None of this is something anyone planned. None of this is something anyone could have arranged with a different light source. The candle did it. The candle, alone in the room, did it.

It is also true that a candle, perhaps more than any other light, makes the people in the room look like themselves. The overhead lamp is honest in the wrong way. The overhead lamp shows every small thing the daylight would have softened — the tiredness around the eyes, the uneven skin, the slight crease of a long day. The candle does the opposite. The candle hides the tiredness in the same shadow it hides everything else. The candle makes the face, somehow, more like the face of the person the people at the table remember from earlier in the day. The candle is, in a small and unfashionable way, kind.

## The Quiet Habit, and the Candles That Remember

The habit of lighting a candle is one that accumulates. The first candle is lit almost by accident — left over from a dinner, set in the middle of the table on a Tuesday, allowed to burn down while someone read in the armchair across the room. The second candle is lit the next Tuesday. The third candle is lit on a Friday. By the end of a year, there is a small stock of candles in the house — three or four in a drawer in the kitchen, two or three on a shelf in the living room, one or two left over from the last dinner party, each one a slightly different size and color and scent, none of them matching, all of them in use.

I like this about candles. The matching set is for lamps. The matching set is for the parts of the house that are designed. The candles can be the leftover ones, the ones that came as gifts, the ones that were bought in a moment and then sat in the drawer for two years before being lit. The candles, more than almost any other object in the house, are allowed to be what they are. The candles do not have to coordinate. The candles do not have to be the right height or the right color or the right scent for the season. They just have to burn.

And the candles, over a year, become a small record of the house. The candle from a friend's birthday in February. The candle from a dinner in May. The candle that a guest brought back from a trip to the coast. The candle that was lit the night something happened that the household still, sometimes, talks about. The candles, like the spines of a bookshelf, are a slow inventory of the evenings the house has contained. The drawer they live in is not a junk drawer. It is a small museum, and the museum is lit, when it is open, by the candles themselves.

This is the deepest work of the candle, I think. The candle is not just a piece of light. The candle is a piece of time. The candle marks the hour. The candle softens the room. The candle makes the people in the room more like themselves. And the candle, when the evening is done, has burned down just enough to remember the evening by. The candle is the smallest object in the house, and the one that does the most work for the hour it is given.
