There is a shirt in our wardrobe that we have owned for ten years. It has been washed so many times that it now feels like a second skin. The collar has softened into something almost liquid. The cuffs have worn to a faint translucence at the edges. The color, once a clear flaxen cream, has drifted toward something warmer, something closer to the inside of a seashell. And every time we put it on, in the early morning, before the day has quite begun, we are aware of being held by something that knows us.

This is the strange gift of linen. Most fabrics in a wardrobe try, very politely, to remain what they were on the day they were purchased. They resist the hours. They resist the wash. They try to be new for as long as possible. Linen does the opposite. Linen agrees, almost from the beginning, to become something else. It agrees to age in your hands, to soften with your body, to remember the shape of every summer it has spent with you.

It is the only fabric, in our wardrobe at least, that we have ever trusted to do this.

## The fiber that decides for itself

Linen comes from flax, a plant that has been cultivated for cloth for something close to ten thousand years. It does not grow quickly, and it does not grow easily. The plants must be pulled, not cut. They must be retted, which means left in the field, in the dew, for weeks, so that the bacteria can loosen the long bast fibers from the woody core. Then they must be scutched, then hackled, then spun, then woven. It is a slow, almost impossibly patient process, and the cloth that results carries that slowness in its very weave.

You can feel it when you first touch a piece of linen. There is a slight resistance to it, a small graininess under the fingers. The new linen does not slide. It does not drape the way silk does. It stands a little away from the body, holding its own shape with a kind of polite stubbornness. It is breathable, far more breathable than cotton, which is why it has been worn for centuries in the places where the heat is worst. It is also slightly stiff. It does not want to be draped yet. It wants to be worn in.

This is the thing nobody tells you about linen, when you first buy a piece of it. The first wear is rarely the best one. The first wash is rarely the best one. The shirt will be a little too crisp at the collar the first time. The trousers will hold their crease a little too aggressively. Linen needs to be broken in, the way a leather shoe needs to be broken in, the way a house needs to be lived in. It needs your mornings to become soft. It needs your hand on the iron. It needs the small, repeated gesture of being put on and taken off again.

Then, somewhere around the fifth or sixth summer, you notice that the linen has changed. The stiffness has gone out of it. The cloth has learned to lie close to the body. It has begun to fall rather than to stand. The creases, which were once sharp and a little defensive, have softened into gentle folds that look like the drift of water. You have not done anything special to it. You have worn it, and washed it, and worn it again. The fiber has done the rest.

This is what we mean when we say linen ages well. We do not mean it stays new for a long time. We mean it agrees, gracefully and without complaint, to become an accurate record of where it has been.

## A wardrobe that knows where it has been

If we had to choose a single fabric to summarize what a wardrobe is for, it would be linen. Not because linen is the most beautiful cloth — silk is more beautiful, and velvet is more dramatic, and wool is more serious. But because linen is the most honest. It is the fabric that will tell you, twenty years from now, exactly who you were on every summer afternoon you wore it.

There is the linen shirt from the holiday in Puglia, the one you wore the day you learned to make pasta properly, the day you sat at a long table under a fig tree and drank cold white wine in the late light. The shirt is still hanging in the closet. The faint olive stain from the chef's apron is still on the left cuff, very slightly green, the memory of a single afternoon that has survived half a dozen washes. You keep the shirt because the stain is part of the shirt now. It is part of you.

There is the linen trouser from the first proper job in a city you have since left. It held its crease so aggressively that first summer that you almost stopped wearing it. But by the third summer it had softened, and by the fifth it had become the most comfortable thing in the drawer, the trousers you reach for on the morning you cannot decide what to put on. By now the cloth has faded to a tone that has no real name — not beige, not cream, not stone, but something warmer and slightly more intimate than any of those. You do not remember what color it was when it was new. You only remember this color, the color it became through being worn.

There is the linen throw at the end of the bed, which started life as a wedding gift and which has now been through three apartments, two cats, and one very small person who, for a long time, used it as a tent. The throw is threadbare in places. The selvedge has begun to unravel along one edge. You have considered mending it, and then decided against it. The threadbare places are part of the throw. They are the record of the bed it has lived on, and the people it has kept warm.

This is what linen gives you that no other fabric quite gives you. It gives you a record. It gives you a wearable archive. It is the only cloth in the wardrobe that you would refuse to replace with a newer version of itself, even if the new one were objectively nicer. The old one has become something the new one cannot be.

## How to let it age gracefully

There is a small art, here, that we have learned over the years. Linen, more than almost any fabric, rewards how it is treated. Cotton, in our experience, will forgive almost anything. Linen will only forgive what it agrees to forgive.

Wash it often. This is the first rule. Linen does not improve with infrequent washing. It improves with frequent washing. Every wash softens the fiber a little more, breaks the stiffness down, allows the cloth to settle into its final shape. The Japanese have a word for the small, repeated pleasure of washing the same textile over and over — and linen, more than any other fabric we own, is the textile that benefits from being loved this way.

Do not be afraid of the creases. Linen creases. Linen is meant to crease. A perfectly ironed linen shirt is, in a way, a slightly unhappy linen shirt. The creases are not a flaw in the cloth; they are the cloth's way of saying where it has been. They are the imprint of the morning, the line of the arm, the place you sat down. A linen garment without creases is a linen garment that has not been lived in yet.

Choose to mend it. When the elbow goes on the linen shirt, do not throw it away. Mend it. Linen mends beautifully. The darning will be visible, and that is the point. The visible mend is part of the garment's record. It is the place where the cloth admits to having been worn, and where your hand has agreed to keep it alive. There is a long European tradition, particularly in the Scandinavian countries, of treating the visible mend as a small point of pride. We agree with this tradition.

Hang it to dry when you can. The sun is not kind to all fabrics, but linen can take it. Sun drying will bleach the cloth a little more, which is part of what you want. The summer sun is what turns linen the warm, almost-amber cream that no chemist can quite reproduce.

And finally, when the time comes — and it will come, because nothing lasts forever, not even linen — do not be embarrassed to let the cloth go. Linen returns to the earth cleanly, the way a leaf returns to the earth. It does not need to be recycled. It does not need to be made into insulation. It can simply be allowed to biodegrade, the long bast fiber returning to the soil it came from. There is something quietly correct about this. A fabric that was patient enough to become a record of your summers is patient enough to end.

## The long friendship

We have worn linen for long enough now to have stopped thinking of it as a choice. It is more like a relationship. There is the shirt, and the trouser, and the summer jacket, and the throw at the end of the bed. They have all aged at slightly different rates. They have all become slightly different colors. They know us, the way a piece of cloth can know someone, which is to say they have become an accurate record of the shape of our days.

When we buy a new piece of linen now, we no longer expect it to look like the picture. We expect it to look like the picture after about four summers. We expect it to soften, and fade, and crease, and become part of the wardrobe's slow memory. We expect to wear it for a decade, and then to mend it, and then to wear it for another decade.

Linen, like the rest of us, is better for having been lived in. The cloth that refuses to age is the cloth that has refused to know you. The cloth that ages, slowly and without complaint, is the cloth that has become, in some quiet way, a friend.
