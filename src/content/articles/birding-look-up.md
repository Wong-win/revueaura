There is a particular moment, on a Sunday morning in late autumn, when you have stepped out of the house for no particular reason — not to walk the dog, not to walk to the bakery, not to walk anywhere specific — and the air does a small thing. The air, which has been grey and silent for the entire week, finds, somewhere above the roofline of the house across the road, a small bright phrase. The phrase is not a word. The phrase is three notes. The phrase repeats, once, twice, with a small rest in between, as if the bird is making sure you have heard. And then, before you have managed to find it with your eyes, the bird is gone. The branch it was on is the branch it is still on. The branch is, this morning, empty. The phrase is the only evidence that the bird was there at all.

This, we think, is what birding actually is. Birding is not the hobby it has been sold as. Birding is not, in our experience, the hobby of serious equipment, and rare sightings, and a competitive relationship with the person in the next village who has ticked more species on the year list. Birding is, properly understood, the slow practice of being outside, often with no particular destination in mind, and looking up at the right moment. Birding is the practice of becoming, over a few years, the person who notices the three notes. Birding is the practice of becoming, over a few years, the person who, on a Sunday morning in late autumn, can tell, without binoculars, which small bird was in that branch, just by the shape of the phrase.

This is the practice we want to argue for, gently and at length.

## what you hear before you see

The first thing about birding, and the thing nobody tells you in advance, is that most of it happens in the ear. The first layer of birding is not the rare warbler in the deep of the hedge. The first layer is the small number of birds who are always, in any reasonable garden or park, somewhere nearby, doing something. The first layer is the great tit, and the blue tit, and the dunnock, and the wren, and the robin, and the blackbird. The first layer is the magpie, who you would rather not have, but who is there anyway, on the lawn, in a slightly aggressive posture. The first layer is the wood pigeon, whose voice is the slow five-note phrase that everyone in Britain, and most of Europe, has heard through a closed window for their entire life.

Most people, when they begin to pay attention, are surprised by how much song is already there. Most people, when they begin to pay attention, are surprised to find that the small suburban garden they have been sitting in for twenty years contains, on an average morning, somewhere between eight and fifteen different bird voices. They are surprised because the voices were, until now, in the background of the morning. They are surprised because the voices have been doing their work, in the background, all along, and the only thing that has changed is that the person on the patio chair has finally stopped scrolling.

This is, in our view, one of the cheaper pleasures of modern life. The pleasure does not require travel. The pleasure does not require equipment, although a small pair of binoculars is helpful, and we will return to this. The pleasure requires only the willingness to sit outside, on a chair, in the morning, for fifteen minutes, and do almost nothing. The birds, in our experience, do the rest. The birds have been doing the rest, in the small garden, in the local park, in the oak tree at the end of the road, since long before you were paying attention. The birds are, in fact, slightly relieved that you have finally started.

## the binoculars, and what they actually do

There is a piece of equipment that goes with birding, and the piece of equipment is binoculars. We want to argue, gently, both for and against them. We want to argue, first, against the idea that you need a serious pair to begin. You do not. A small, light, slightly cheap pair of 8x42 binoculars is, in our experience, everything most people need, and we would gently suggest that any pair costing significantly more is, for the first few years, mostly a way of feeling like a more serious person than you are. The bird does not look better through an expensive pair. The bird looks the same. The bird looks, in fact, slightly better through a pair you are actually carrying with you than through the expensive pair you left at home because it was too heavy to bring on the walk.

What the binoculars do, more than anything, is allow you to confirm. The binoculars allow you to look at the small bird in the hedge and see, with a little more clarity, whether the breast is streaked or unstreaked, whether the wing bar is there or not, whether the small eye stripe is the colour you think it is. The binoculars turn birding, in other words, from a guess into an identification. The binoculars are the small piece of equipment that turns "something interesting in the hedge" into "a female blackcap, which is the first one I have seen in the garden."

But the binoculars, we should also say, are not the most important thing. The most important thing is the eye. The most important thing is the patience. The most important thing is the small willingness to stand still, in a wood, on a Friday morning, and let the bird come to you, instead of chasing the bird through the undergrowth. The most important thing, in our experience, is the ten quiet minutes of looking at nothing in particular, which is the ten minutes during which the bird, almost always, decides to appear.

## looking up, and looking up again

The thing about looking up is that it takes a while to remember how. We are, in modern life, mostly looking down. We are looking down at the phone. We are looking down at the pavement. We are looking down at the small task list we have been carrying around the house all day. We are looking, in short, at a horizontal plane of about four feet, which is the plane of the desk, the screen, the dog lead, the bag, the pram, the takeaway coffee. We have, in modern life, almost completely lost the habit of looking up.

Birding is, among other things, a way of recovering the habit. Birding is, in fact, an excuse to look up. Birding gives you permission, on a Sunday morning, to stop, in the middle of the path, in the middle of the park, and tip your head back, and look at the tree canopy. Birding gives you permission to look, in a slightly unfocused way, at the sky, and to wait, with no particular urgency, for something to appear in your field of view. Birding, in this sense, is one of the few hobbies that doubles as a posture correction. After a few months of looking up, you find yourself, in the supermarket, looking at the ceiling. After a few months of looking up, you find yourself, on the train, looking out of the window at the bird on the overhead wire. After a few months of looking up, you find that looking up is no longer something you have to remember to do, and is now just a thing you do.

This is the practice, in its deep form. The practice is, eventually, less about the bird, and more about the looking. The practice is the slow recovery of the upper half of your visual field. The practice is the slow recovery of the part of the morning that was always there, above the roofline, and that you had stopped noticing because you had, in the small daily way of modern life, started living in the lower half of the window.

## what the birds teach you about season

Birds, if you let them, are a calendar. Birds are, in fact, one of the older calendars we have. The birds will tell you, on a Tuesday in late February, that spring is on the way, because the great tit has begun to sing the spring song. The birds will tell you, on a Sunday morning in early April, that the migrants are back, because there is a chiffchaff in the oak tree, saying its name over and over in the slightly insistent way that chiffchaffs have. The birds will tell you, in early May, that the swallows are back under the eaves. The birds will tell you, in late July, that the swifts are leaving, because the swifts are screaming over the roof at three in the morning, as if they are trying to fit in one last loop before they go.

The birds are, in our view, every month of the year, telling you what is going on. The birds know the month better than you do. The birds know, more accurately than any calendar, that today is the morning the cuckoo returned, or the morning the first redwing came down from the north, or the morning the wren in the hedge has decided to start building.

The practice of listening to this is, we think, one of the more underrated pleasures of paying attention. The practice turns the year from a flat thing into a layered thing. The practice turns March from "March" into the month when the song thrush starts up before anyone else. The practice turns October from "October" into the month when the fieldfares arrive in the hawthorn hedge at the end of the road. The year, after a few seasons of birding, is no longer the same year.

## looking up, and what the walk teaches you

The walk home, after a morning of looking up, is a particular walk. The walk is slower than the walk out, partly because the neck, after an hour of tipping back, has a small gentle complaint, and partly because the eyes, after an hour of finding small things in trees, have begun to look, involuntarily, at every bush and chimney. You notice, on the walk home, things you did not notice on the walk out. The great tit on the overhead wire. The two magpies on the lawn of the house on the corner. The small bird, possibly a wren, in the hedge by the bus stop, who is making a sound more complicated than his body has any right to produce.

You put the small bag by the door. You open the notebook, later, and add the date. The notebook becomes, slowly, a small record of mornings — the kind of object you would not part with, because it has, in the small way of all personal records, the entire shape of the year in it. The notebook is the year, written down by hand. The notebook is the quiet evidence that the looking up, which began as a Sunday morning experiment, has been doing its work.

This is the small, soft argument we wanted to make. Not for a particular patch of land. Not for a particular field guide. The argument is for the looking up — the Sunday morning, the small binoculars, the two or three entries in the notebook, and the slow recovery of the upper half of the world. The birds are there. They have always been there. Look up, on a Sunday morning. They will be there tomorrow as well, and for a long time after you have stopped.
