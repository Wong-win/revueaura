The first apartment I rented on my own, after years of sharing houses with other people, had a single mirror in it. There was the mirror in the bathroom, which was the obligatory mirror above the sink, the one every apartment has because every apartment is required to have one. And there was, on the wall of the living room, a tall narrow mirror that the previous tenant had left behind, hung a little too high, with a frame that had been painted over so many times that I could not tell what color it had originally been. The mirror was not particularly attractive. The mirror was hung at the wrong height. The mirror was, on the whole, an afterthought of the room.

Within a month of moving in, I had noticed that the room, despite being one of the smallest in the apartment, felt more generous than it had any right to feel. The ceilings were low. The window was modest. The walls were painted the slightly cold white of a rental that had not been repainted in a decade. And yet the room, when I sat on the sofa in the evening, felt larger than it was. The room had a sense of depth it had not earned from its square footage. The room felt, in some way I could not quite name, doubled.

It took me almost a year to realize that the mirror was what was doing it. The mirror was, in the most literal sense, doubling the room. The mirror was reflecting the wall opposite, and the window, and the lamp, and the chair in the corner, and the person sitting on the sofa. The mirror was returning, to the room, the room itself. And the room, with the mirror in it, was suddenly larger because the mirror had given the room back to the room.

This is, I think, the work a mirror does better than almost any other object in the house. A well-placed mirror is the only interior element that gives a room more of itself. A lamp gives the room more light, but the lamp does not give the room more room. A rug gives the room more warmth, but the rug does not make the room larger. A mirror, hung well, makes the room larger. Not by adding anything. By reflecting back what is already there, in a way that the eye reads as more.

## What the Mirror Does to Light

The deepest thing a mirror does is to light. Not the mirror's own light — the mirror has none. The mirror's light is borrowed. The mirror takes the light that is already in the room and moves it. The mirror redirects the window across the wall. The mirror redirects the lamp into a corner that would otherwise be dark. The mirror pushes the daylight further into the room than the window, alone, could have pushed it. The mirror is the cheapest lighting renovation in the house, and the most underrated.

I have a friend who has a small apartment with a single window in the living room and almost no natural light reaching the kitchen, which is at the back of the apartment, around a corner, behind a wall. For years she fought this by adding more lamps to the kitchen, and by painting the kitchen white, and by replacing the heavy curtains in the living room with lighter ones. None of these worked as well as the small mirror she eventually hung on the wall between the living room and the kitchen. The mirror was no larger than a paperback. The mirror was hung at eye level. The mirror was, on its own, a fairly modest object. But the mirror, from the moment she hung it, returned the light from the window back into the living room, and the living room, from the moment the mirror was hung, became a brighter room. The mirror had not added a window. The mirror had simply made the existing window work harder.

This is the particular gift of the mirror. The mirror does not need to be large. The mirror does not need to be decorative. The mirror does not need to be in a frame. The mirror, in most rooms, works best when it is hung quietly, at the height of the objects it is reflecting, in a position that catches the most light from the most useful source. The mirror is a tool more than it is a piece of decor. The mirror, properly used, is the cheapest electrician in the house.

## The Mirror That Doubles a Room

There is a particular trick, used in old houses and in houses designed by people who had spent time in old houses, of hanging a mirror on the wall opposite a window. The trick is older than interior design. The trick is older than the modern idea of a room. The trick is the observation that a window and a mirror, hung across from each other, do not just brighten the room — they double it. The window makes the wall it sits in the brightest wall in the room. The mirror, on the opposite wall, returns the window back to the room in a way that the eye reads as another window. The room, from any angle that can see both, has suddenly two windows. The room has more depth. The room has more air. The room, despite being the same square footage it was the day before, has been doubled by the smallest possible object.

I think about this in the small houses I have loved. My grandmother's house, in a town I have not been back to in twenty years, had a tall mirror hung on the wall opposite the front window. The mirror was, by the standards of any magazine, an ugly mirror — the frame was a heavy gilt that had been touched up in the wrong places by the wrong person at the wrong moment. But the mirror did its job. The mirror made the front room, which was the room where the family gathered on Sundays, feel twice the size it was. The mirror reflected the curtains, and the lace at the window, and the lamp that was usually on in the evening, and the head of the person sitting in the chair closest to the window. The mirror did not, technically, add anything to the room. The mirror made the room appear to contain the room a second time.

This is the deepest thing the mirror does. The mirror does not increase the room's square footage. The mirror increases the room's perceived square footage, which is, in any room where people are trying to live, the more important of the two. The room that feels larger than it is, feels easier to be in. The room that feels larger than it is, holds conversations differently. The room that feels larger than it is, holds light longer in the evening. The room that feels larger than it is, is the room people want to sit in. The mirror is, almost alone among objects in the house, an object that improves the room without occupying it.

## The Mirror That Doubles the Light From a Candle

There is a small thing a mirror does that is connected to the candle, and that I have come to think is one of the deeper reasons to have a mirror in a living room. A candle in a room with a mirror is, somehow, two candles. The flame of the candle is reflected back into the room by the mirror, and the reflected flame is, to the eye, as good as the original flame. The room, with the candle on the table and the mirror on the wall, has been given a second candle without anyone having to light it. The room, with both, is twice as warm as the room with only the original.

This is true of any small light. The candle is the most dramatic version of it, because the candle is the smallest light, and the doubling of the smallest light is the most visible. But the same thing happens, more quietly, with any lamp. The lamp on the side table, reflected in the mirror on the opposite wall, becomes, to the eye, two lamps. The lamp on the desk, reflected in the mirror across the room, brightens the corner of the room the lamp was not actually pointed at. The mirror, in a room with any light at all, makes the light do more work than the light could have done alone.

I have a small mirror in the hallway of my apartment, hung at the height of the lamp on the table at the end of the hall. The mirror is, on its own, unremarkable — a round mirror in a thin brass frame, hung a few inches higher than I would hang it in any other room. But the mirror, in the position I hung it, reflects the lamp back into the hallway at the exact moment the lamp is needed most, which is the moment when someone is walking from the front of the apartment to the back of the apartment late at night, half asleep, not wanting to turn on the overhead light. The mirror, in the hallway, has made the lamp do the work of two lamps. The hallway is brighter. The hallway feels shorter. The hallway, on the late-night walk to the kitchen for a glass of water, has become a hallway that is easier to be in.

## The Mirror That Is Honest About the Room

There is a final thing the mirror does, and this is the thing I think about most when I think about why I want a mirror in the rooms I live in. The mirror is the only object in the house that tells the truth about the room. The lamp tells the truth about the lamp. The chair tells the truth about the chair. The painting on the wall tells the truth about the painting. The mirror tells the truth about the room, because the mirror is the room. The mirror is the lamp and the chair and the painting and the window and the wall and the floor. The mirror is, in a small and unfussy way, the room's self-portrait.

This is why the mirror, more than almost any other object in the house, is not a piece of decoration. The mirror is a piece of the room's accounting. The mirror shows what the room is, in this light, at this hour, with the people in it. The mirror shows the room as it is, without flattering it. The mirror is the only object in the house that the room cannot lie to.

I have come to think that this is the deepest reason to have a mirror in a room. Not to make the room brighter. Not to make the room feel larger. Not to make the room more interesting to look at. To make the room honest. To give the room a place where it can see itself. The room, with a mirror in it, is a room that knows what it is. The room, without a mirror, is a room that has to take the room's word for it.

This is, I think, the work a mirror does. The mirror gives the room more of itself. The mirror returns the light to the room. The mirror doubles the room without occupying it. The mirror, hung well, is the smallest, quietest, most underestimated object in any house. The mirror, hung well, is the room's other half.
