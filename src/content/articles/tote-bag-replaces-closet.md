There is a tote bag on the chair by the door. It has been on the chair by the door, in roughly the same position, for long enough that the chair has begun to remember it. The leather, which started life a pale, almost foal-like tan, has gone the color of strong tea. The handles have darkened where the hand has lived. There is a small ink stain on the inside, near the seam, from a pen that leaked one Tuesday and was never quite cleaned. And the bag itself, even empty, has taken on the slightly slumped, slightly weighted posture of a thing that is rarely completely empty, and is rarely completely full, and is rarely asked to be either.

This is the strange and rather wonderful thing about a really good tote. It is, in the most literal sense, a piece of architecture for carrying things. But it is also, in a less literal sense, a small piece of autobiography. The tote bag is the piece of your wardrobe that contains, at any given hour, the working evidence of how you actually live. There is the notebook, slightly bent from being carried too long. There is the small tin of something that you keep meaning to throw out. There is the pair of sunglasses you lost three months ago and which reappeared, last week, at the very bottom. There is the receipt from the lunch you had on Tuesday and which, for reasons you cannot quite explain, you have not thrown away. There is the book you have been meaning to finish, the pen you have been meaning to replace, the charger that works with exactly one device and which is, for this reason, indispensable.

The tote is the place where all of those things go. The tote is the place where the day, in its small and rather ordinary way, has been agreeing to live.

## The bag that was not a handbag

It is worth pausing for a moment to notice that the tote bag, in the modern sense, was not always a thing that women carried. The tote began, as best anyone can tell, as a piece of equipment — an open-topped bag made of heavy canvas, sometimes leather, designed for carrying things that a smaller bag could not. The ice went to market in a tote. The library books came home in a tote. The groceries, before the era of the small cart and the large car, were carried up the front walk in a tote.

The tote was, in other words, a piece of utility. It was not, in any sense, a piece of fashion. And this is, we think, the secret of why the tote still works in a way that the more elegant handbag sometimes does not. The tote did not begin as an object of desire. It began as an object of use. And this means that when you pick one up, even today, you are picking up a bag that is not asking to be admired. The tote is a bag that has agreed to be helpful.

This is also why the tote has, in the last ten or fifteen years, returned so thoroughly to the center of the modern wardrobe. There was a long stretch, in the nineties and the two-thousands, when the small, very structured, very expensive handbag was the dominant object of the working wardrobe. These bags had a job to do, and the job was to be beautiful. They were beautiful, mostly. They were also almost impossible to carry a paperback in.

The tote has come back, we think, because we have stopped, at last, pretending that we live the kind of life that fits into a small bag. We have stoppers, we have snacks, we have chargers, we have small children. We have weather. We have books we are reading and books we are saving to read. We have, in short, things. And the tote, more than almost any other bag, has agreed to hold them.

## What the day looks like, packed in cloth

There is an Italian expression, non so dove, which roughly translates as I don't know where, and which is what the Italian person says when they have forgotten where they put something. The Italian tote bag, when made honestly, is the cure for non so dove. The Italian tote knows where things go. The Italian tote has, in its soft architecture, places for each of the things you are likely to need in a day. There is the long inner pocket for the laptop. There is the smaller outer pocket for the phone. There is the place for the keys, the place for the wallet, the place for the thing you keep meaning to mail. The tote, when it is well-designed, has a small geography. The tote, when it is yours, has a small geography you already know.

This is the second secret of the tote. The first is that it is, by history, a bag of use. The second is that the bag of use, over time, becomes a kind of organized drawer. The tote develops, in the way that every well-loved container develops, a particular order. Not the order of somebody else's design. The order of yours. The order, in fact, that has been slowly negotiated between you and your days, over the last three years, every time you have reached into the bag for something and found it.

This is also why changing a tote, after a few years of loyalty to the old one, is such a strange and slightly melancholy undertaking. You are not just changing a bag. You are changing the geography where you keep the small, working evidence of how you live. The new tote will, in time, develop its own geography. But for a few weeks, you will be reaching into the wrong pockets. You will be looking, by reflex, for the pen on the left side, and finding it on the right. The transition, like the transition between one apartment and another, is a small grief.

It is worth saying that this grief is also, eventually, a kind of pleasure. The tote, like any good piece of architecture, will adapt to its occupant. The new tote will, within a season, have become the old tote. The new tote will have its own ink stain, its own telling place. The relationship will rebuild itself. But the rebuilding, like all rebuildings, takes a moment.

## The leather is the long conversation

If we had to choose a single material from which a tote should be made, we would choose leather. Not because canvas does not work. Canvas, in fact, works very well. But because leather, in the way that almost no other material does, agrees to age with you.

A canvas tote is, by its nature, a kind of seasonal bag. It is the bag you take to the market in June. It is the bag you put in the corner of the closet when the weather turns. It does not have a long biography. It does not develop a patina. It does not become more interesting the longer you carry it. The canvas tote is, in this sense, a young bag. It is the bag you keep for a year or two before passing on.

A leather tote is the opposite. A leather tote, if you have chosen it with any care at all, will be the bag you are still carrying in a decade. It will go dark at the handles. It will soften at the corners. It will pick up a small mark on the front, from the day you put it down too hard on the bench at the cafe, and it will keep that mark forever. The leather will, slowly, agree to take the shape of your shoulder. The leather will, in the end, become something you cannot quite imagine replacing.

This is the long conversation that leather has with the person carrying it. The conversation is slow. The conversation is sometimes a little costly. The conversation will, however, outlast any number of trend bags.

### the small things to look for

If you are buying a leather tote this season — and we will say only that, in our experience, most people who have not already bought one are, at some point, going to — there are a few small things worth attending to.

Look, first, at the handle attachment. The handles should be sewn into the bag, not riveted. A riveted handle is a handle that is, statistically, more likely to come loose in the third year. A sewn handle is a handle that has been attached the way a tailor would attach it, and which will, with reasonable care, be the handle that is still there in the tenth year.

Look, second, at the lining. The lining, ideally, should be a piece of cloth, not a piece of plastic. A cotton lining will, over time, settle into the bag in a way that a synthetic one will not. A cotton lining will let you find things by feel. A cotton lining will, after a season, have taken on the particular smell of whatever you keep in the bag — which is, in our view, a feature and not a bug.

Look, third, at the strap. The strap, on a tote that you intend to carry for any length of time, should be wide enough to sit on a shoulder without cutting. It should be soft enough at the edges to fold slightly. It should not, by any means, be the kind of strap that leaves a small red mark on a bare shoulder at the end of an hour. A bag that is comfortable to carry is a bag you will, in fact, carry. A bag that is uncomfortable to carry is a bag that ends up on the chair by the door.

## A confession, in soft leather

The tote bag, in the end, is a confession. It is the piece of your wardrobe that admits, more honestly than anything else, to the kind of life you actually live. A small, elegant handbag is the kind of bag that says you live the kind of life where you leave the house with nothing but a credit card and a phone. A tote is the kind of bag that says you live the kind of life where you leave the house prepared.

This is, when you stop to think about it, the more interesting confession. The tote is, in a small way, an argument against the modern fantasy of minimalism. The tote is, in fact, the bag that has been allowed to be maximal. The tote is allowed to be a little heavy. The tote is allowed to slump on one side because there is a thermos in it. The tote is allowed to have a small stain, a small ink mark, a small corner of notebook peeking out.

The tote is the bag of the person who has, somewhere along the way, stopped trying to be a curated object. It is the bag of the person who has agreed, quietly and without announcement, to be a real one. And this is, we think, why we keep coming back to the tote year after year, and why we find that the tote, even when we have bought another bag, is the one we still reach for.

It is the bag that knows what we need before we do. It is the bag that has, in some quiet way, made room for the day. And it is the bag, when we get home at night and put it back on the chair by the door, that sits there waiting, patiently, for the next morning, when we will reach for it again, and it will be exactly where we left it. It will be the same bag it was the day we bought it. And it will also, by then, be a slightly different bag. The leather will have softened another fraction. The handles will have darkened another fraction. The pocket will hold, in a slightly different position, the small working evidence of how the day has been.

This is the long conversation of the tote. This is what it knows. And this is why, of all the bags in the closet, the tote is the one we would have the most trouble doing without.
