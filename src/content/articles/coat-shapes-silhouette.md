A coat is the first thing anyone sees of you in winter and the last thing you take off at night. Between these two moments — the door you walk out of in the morning and the hook you hang it on when you return — the coat is the part of the wardrobe that does the most public work. It is also the part of the wardrobe that, more than any other, decides the silhouette.

You can buy the trousers that flatter you. You can choose the shirt that softens the face. But the coat, by the time anyone has noticed the rest, has already written the first sentence of how you are seen. It is the architecture of the day, and it is worth taking seriously.

## why the coat is bigger than the outfit

It is strange, when you stop to think about it, how much weight we put on a piece of clothing we are, in some ways, the least attached to. The coat does not touch the skin. It does not travel in the bag. It does not get adjusted during the day. It is, in a sense, the part of the wardrobe that has been allowed to remain the most impersonal. And yet it is also the part that gets noticed first.

This is because the coat is large. Because it is the part of the outfit that frames the body rather than the part of the outfit that the body wears. Because it is what the eye reads before the eye has had a chance to read anything else.

There is, in fact, an old piece of advice from the dressing rooms of the early twentieth century — advice that has been mostly forgotten, but that holds up surprisingly well — which is that a woman or a man should never buy a coat without first having in mind the person they expect to be when they are wearing it. Not the body. The person. Because the coat, more than any other garment, will be read as the outermost expression of that person. It is the version of you that is presented to the world before the world has met the rest of you.

This sounds like a high bar. It is also, when you stand in front of the mirror wearing the coat for the tenth time, surprisingly accurate. The coat either agrees with the person you are trying to be, or it does not. And no amount of standing in front of the mirror is going to fix the disagreement.

## the silhouette is a kind of sentence

If we were to think about coats the way a tailor thinks about them, we would think first about silhouette. Not color. Not fabric. Not even fit, in the small sense of the word. Silhouette is the broader shape the coat makes against the body and against the rest of the outfit, and it is the single most important decision the coat asks you to make.

There are, broadly speaking, four silhouettes that have dominated the long history of the coat. The fitted coat, which follows the body closely and ends at the hip or just below. The tailored coat, which allows a small allowance for movement and ends, classically, just below the knee. The cocoon, which is loose throughout the shoulder and falls, often dramatically, to mid-calf. And the long line, which can fall almost anywhere on the body but insists, by its length, on being the most important thing in the frame.

Each of these silhouettes says something different about the wearer, and each of them is, in its own way, true.

The fitted coat says: I am here, and I am precise. The tailored coat says: I have considered this. The cocoon says: I am not in a hurry. The long line says: I am not yet finished arriving.

You can wear any of them, of course. The point is not to be loyal to one of them forever. The point is to know which one is being read when you walk into the room. Because the room is, whether you like it or not, reading it.

### the long coat and the short coat

Within the long history of the coat, the question of length has been, perhaps, the most argued over. There was a time, in the middle of the twentieth century, when nearly every woman and many men wore a coat that ended somewhere just below the knee. There was a later time, in the eighties and the nineties, when the short coat came back into fashion and the long coat felt, briefly, old-fashioned. And there is now, in our own moment, a kind of wide range in which almost any length is allowed as long as it is worn with conviction.

We would not try to choose for you. But we would suggest that length is one of the easiest ways to change the silhouette without changing the cloth or the color. A coat that hits just below the hip says one thing. A coat that hits mid-thigh says another. A coat that falls to mid-calf says yet a third. And the difference is large enough that most of us own, in fact, more than one.

This is also, in part, why we would gently argue against the cult of the single perfect coat. The single perfect coat is a lovely idea. In practice, the wardrobe is served better by two, or even three, coats of different lengths. Each one of them has its weather and its occasion. And on the morning when the weather and the occasion are both uncertain, the coat you reach for will do a great deal to set the tone of the day.

## the cloth is a kind of weather

If silhouette is the sentence, the cloth is the weather of the sentence. It is what tells you, when you put the coat on in the morning, what kind of day you are dressing for.

There is wool, of course, and there is wool of different weights. There is cashmere, which is wool but lighter, softer, and almost without exception more expensive. There is the boiled wool, dense and weatherproof and a little severe. There is the felted wool, which has the particular quality of turning rain into something almost invisible. There is the loden, which is what every Alpine grandmother will tell you is the only proper cloth for an honest winter. There is the wool-and-silk blend, which has the slight shine of something more formal. And there is the wool with a small percentage of something synthetic, which we will not pretend to recommend but which, in our own colder moments, has occasionally kept us warm.

We are not going to tell you which cloth is the right cloth. We are going to say only that cloth is the part of the coat decision that should not be made in a hurry. A coat is, after all, the garment you wear most frequently across the longest stretch of the calendar. The coat you buy this November will, with any luck, still be the coat you are wearing in February and again next November. It will, in this sense, be one of the longest relationships you have with any piece of clothing.

This is also why the coat is worth the better cloth. The difference between a coat you can wear for three winters and a coat you can wear for ten is, almost always, the cloth. The buttons can be replaced. The lining can be relined. But the wool that has been woven tightly enough to hold its line through a decade of weather is not a thing you can reproduce by spending a little more.

### the small things the cloth does

The cloth, beyond its warmth and its weight, also does something that is harder to name. It absorbs sound. It absorbs the small social friction of being out in the world in winter. A coat made of good cloth does not have to be new to feel reliable. It does not have to be expensive to feel considered. The cloth, in this sense, is the part of the coat that does the most invisible work.

There is also the question of how the cloth drapes. A cloth with good drape will hang from the shoulder without effort. A cloth without drape will hold the shape of the hanger, and you will wear the coat as if it were still on the hanger. We have all worn coats like this — coats with shoulders that pointed slightly outward, coats with hems that refused to fall. They were made of cloth that did not know how to drape, and the silhouette they made was, by definition, less graceful than it could have been.

The drape of a cloth is, in this sense, an honest measure of its quality. If you can, when trying on a coat, hold it up by the shoulders and let it fall, you can learn almost everything you need to know about the cloth. A good cloth will fall like a held breath slowly released. A lesser cloth will hold itself up and wait to be told what to do.

## the coat as the architecture of the door

We have been thinking, in this piece, of the coat as something to be worn. But the coat, in fact, spends a lot of its life not being worn. It spends its evenings and its weekends on a hook by the door, or on a wooden hanger in a closet, or, in some houses we admire, draped over the back of a chair in the room where it was last taken off.

It is in these moments that the coat does, perhaps, its most interesting work. The coat is the piece of the wardrobe that has been allowed to be present even when it is not being worn. It is the piece that is allowed to hold the shape of the wearer even when the wearer has stepped out of it for the day.

A good coat, hung by the door, is a kind of small architecture. It suggests the kind of person who lives in this house. It suggests the kind of morning this person had, and the kind of evening they are expecting to return to. It is, in a very gentle way, a piece of furniture.

This is why we tend to choose coats with care. Not because we are particularly vain, or particularly concerned about the opinion of the postman. But because the coat, more than the trousers or the shirt, will end up holding the shape of our lives outside the house. It will be the most visible thing about us on the longest stretch of the year. It will be the thing that hangs by the door, waiting, while we are inside doing the work of being whoever we are inside.

A coat, then, is not a thing to be acquired quickly. It is something to be chosen slowly, over time, the way you would choose a chair or a piece of music. We hope, if you are choosing one this season, you will give yourself enough Saturdays to do it well.

And we hope, when you hang it by the door, you will pause for a moment to notice how it falls, and what kind of morning it is anticipating, and what kind of evening it will be ready for when you return. The coat, in its quiet way, is the architecture that holds all the rest.
