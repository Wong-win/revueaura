The first apartment we lived in as adults had, by the time we moved out, sixteen power strips plugged into sixteen different outlets, distributed more or less evenly across the living room, the bedroom, and the small back hallway that had been, before we moved in, did not, in any obvious sense, have any electrical function. There were, in addition to the power strips, the cables themselves — a small ecology of black and white cords that had, over the years, arranged themselves along the baseboards in patterns that were not, on inspection, entirely explicable. There was the cable that ran from the modem to the router to the laptop dock. There was the cable that ran from the laptop dock to the second monitor. There was the cable that ran from the second monitor to the small speaker that sat, on a stand, in the corner of the desk. There was the cable that ran from the speaker to the small amplifier that sat, on the floor, underneath the desk. There was the cable that ran from the amplifier to the wall. We did not, at any point in the five years we lived there, ever really understand the cable system. We were, in a literal sense, living in a small museum of our own electrical indecision.

The new apartment, we promised ourselves, would be different. The new apartment would have a plan. The new apartment would have, in particular, a single, carefully chosen location for every device, every charger, every brick, and every cable, and the cables would, in the new apartment, be hidden. The new apartment would, in short, be the apartment in which the visible tech was, as much as possible, not visible. We had, in the previous apartment, the sense that the visible tech was slowly taking over the visible apartment — that the apartment was, in some quiet way, becoming a workspace rather than a home. The cables, in particular, had begun to feel like a small, low-level accusation. The apartment was not, we had begun to suspect, ours. The apartment was, in some quiet way, the cables'.

What we learned, over the year it took to set up the new apartment, was that hiding visible tech is, in 2026, both easier and harder than it has ever been. It is easier because the equipment that one needs has, in many cases, gotten smaller. The laptop, in 2026, charges through a single small brick; the phone, in 2024 through a single small cable. The router, in 2026, is the size of a small book. There are, in many cases, fewer cables to hide. It is harder because the equipment one needs has, in many cases, multiplied. The lamp now charges through a small cable. The headphones charge through a small cable. The e-reader charges through a small cable. The speaker charges through a small cable. Each of these cables is, individually, small. The aggregate of them, in many apartments, is considerable.

## the small black boxes

The cables, in our experience, are not, in fact, the worst offenders. The worst offenders, in any technology-conscious interior, are the small black boxes. The bricks, in the older parlance — the small rectangular objects that sit, half-attached to the wall, half-attached to the cable, in a slightly precarious relationship with the floor. The small black boxes that are the power supplies of the small electronic things one has accumulated over the years. There is, in the average apartment of 2026, something like fifteen of these. The laptop charger uses twelve. The lamp charger uses six. The speaker charger uses twelve. The phone charger uses twelve. The aggregate, in any apartment with a person in it who has lived in it for more than a year, is considerable.

What is interesting, we have come to think, is how much these small black boxes have come to define the look of the modern apartment. The exposed brick, the in-cupboard lighting, the careful rug on the floor — all of these can be, and often are, defeated by a small black box sitting, slightly forlornly, on the kitchen counter. The small black box is the visual equivalent of the typo on the cover of the book. It is the small thing that the eye, almost involuntarily, returns to. It is the thing that makes the room, in some quiet way, not quite a room.

We spent, in the first month of the new apartment, an embarrassing amount of time on the small black boxes. We bought small cloth bags to put them in. We bought small plastic boxes with cable cutouts to hide them in. We bought, in a moment of slight desperation, a small basket with a fake plant on top. None of these worked particularly well. What worked, in the end, was the simplest thing. We moved every small black box, where possible, off the counter. We mounted, behind the desk, a small wooden shelf that held, out of sight, the laptop brick. We moved the lamp's brick behind the lamp. We put the speaker's brick inside the speaker stand. We drilled a small hole in the back of the bedside table, through which the phone charger disappeared. None of this is, in any reasonable sense, advanced engineering. It is, in fact, the kind of thing one might have done in 1956. It worked.

## cables as architectural features

The cables, once the small black boxes were out of sight, were considerably easier. We bought, over the course of several weekends, a small inventory of cable management tools — cloth sleeves, adhesive clips, cable trays that attached, with small brackets, to the back of the desk. None of these were expensive. None of them, in any obvious sense, were exciting. All of them, in the aggregate, made an enormous difference. The cables, in the new apartment, now run, almost without exception, along the back edges of the furniture. The cables, where they must run along the floor, run along the baseboards, held in place with small adhesive clips. The cables, where they must cross a doorway, run through small rubber channels that one barely notices. The cables, in short, are not gone. They are simply, in the older architectural sense, integrated.

What is interesting, in our experience, is how much this changes the visual character of a room. A room in which the cables are visible is, in some quiet sense, a room that has not been finished. It is a room in which the technology is still in the middle of being installed. It is a room that is, in the older sense of the word, under construction. A room in which the cables are integrated, by contrast, is a room in which the technology has been completed. It is a room in which the small electronic things are, in fact, doing their work, and the room, having accommodated them, has moved on.

We have come to think, in fact, that the integration of the cables is one of the small signs of an apartment that has been thought about. The exposed cable is, in this sense, like the exposed pipe in the basement. The exposed pipe is fine. The exposed pipe is, in many cases, perfectly serviceable. The exposed pipe is, however, the sign of a house that has not quite decided whether it is, in the relevant sense, a finished house. The integrated cable, by contrast, is the sign of a house that has decided it is. The cable is still there. The cable is still doing its work. The cable is simply, in the small domestic sense, part of the architecture.

## the difference between a room and a workspace

What we have come to think, after several years of small experiments, is that the difference between a room and a workspace is, in large part, a question of the visible tech. The workspace, almost by definition, is a room in which the technology is foreground. The desk is foreground. The computer is foreground. The cables are a foreground. The room, almost by definition, is a room in which the technology is background. The lamp is background. The speaker is background. The charger is background. The small black boxes are background. The cables are background.

This is not, we recognize, an absolute distinction. There are apartments in which the technology is foreground and which are, on the whole, beautiful. There are workspaces in which the technology is background and which are, on the whole, unproductive. The distinction is not, in fact, a moral one. It is a question of which room one is, in one's mind, building. A workspace is a room that has been optimized for the technology that is in it. A room is a room in which the technology has been optimized for the rest of the room. Both are valid. The point is that one cannot, in the same room, do both. The technology, in 2026, asks to be foregrounded. The room, almost by definition, asks for it not to be. The small domestic problem of our century is, in this sense, the small problem of how to keep the technology from quietly taking over.

What we have come to think, after several years of trying, is that the answer to this question is mostly a question of the cables. The cables, more than any other piece of the technology, are the visible signature of the technology's presence in the room. The cables, more than any other piece of the technology, are the visual cue that the room is, in some quiet way, on its way to becoming a workspace. Hiding the cables — or, more honestly, integrating the cables into the architecture of the room — is, we have come to think, the single most effective thing one can do to keep a room from becoming a workspace. The cables are, in this sense, the canary in the coal mine. When the cables are visible, the room is, in some quiet way, working. When the cables are integrated, the room has, in some quiet way, arrived.

We are not, in the end, anti-technology. We are not even, particularly, against the visible tech. We have, after all, a fair amount of it. We have, on the desk, a laptop, a lamp, a speaker, a small plant. We have, on the bedside table, a phone, a charger, an e-reader, a clock. We have, in the kitchen, a small radio that has been, for the better part of a decade, slowly losing its ability to receive radio stations. We are not, in any reasonable sense, technology minimalists. We are, in fact, the opposite of technology minimalists. We are technology maximalists who have, over the years, come to believe that the visible tech is, in the end, a kind of clutter — and that the kind of clutter one has, in 2026, is mostly the kind of clutter one can hide. We have, in our own small way, learned to hide it. We have, in our own small way, come to think that the room, in 2026, is the better for it. The cables are still there. The cables, in some quiet way, are always going to be there. The point is not that they are not there. The point is that one has, in some small domestic sense, come to an accommodation with them.
