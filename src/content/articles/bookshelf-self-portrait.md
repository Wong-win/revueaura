The bookshelf in my apartment is, like most bookshelves I have known, slightly out of control. It runs along one wall of the living room, a tall pine unit that was built in a flat-pack box in an afternoon about fifteen years ago and has, in the years since, accumulated in a way I have never quite managed to organize. There are the books I read in college, still mostly in the place I put them when I first moved in. There are the books I bought in my late twenties in a long burst of enthusiasm for a kind of writing I no longer read. There are the books that have followed me from three different apartments, each one placed on the shelf in roughly the same position it occupied in the apartment before, so that the bookshelf is not just a record of the books but a record of the rooms the books have been in. There are the books I bought for the apartment I do not yet have, the books about cooking or gardening or home repair that I have never quite opened but that I keep meaning to. There are the books a few friends have given me, each one a small document of a particular friendship at a particular time.

I have come to think that this is what a bookshelf actually is. A bookshelf is not a piece of storage. A bookshelf is the only self-portrait in the house that improves with age. A photograph, hung on the wall, fades. A painting, hung on the wall, fades more slowly but still fades. A bookshelf does the opposite. Every year a book is added to the shelf, the shelf becomes a slightly fuller record of who the person in the house has been. A bookshelf at twenty-five is one self-portrait. A bookshelf at forty is another. A bookshelf at sixty is yet another, and the differences between them are not small.

## The Spines as a Memoir

If you know what to look for, you can read a person's bookshelf the way you read a long letter. The height of the books tells you what kind of reader the person was at the time — paperbacks for commuting, hardcovers for staying, large art books for the years when the person could afford them. The color of the spines tells you which publishers mattered in which decade. The condition of the spines tells you whether the books were read carefully or read quickly or, in some cases, not read at all. The dog-eared pages tell you which books were carried around. The cracked spines tell you which books were read and reread until the glue gave out. The books still in their shrink wrap tell you the years when the person was buying more than reading.

None of this is, strictly speaking, private. A bookshelf in a living room is, by definition, the bookshelf the household is willing to be seen by. It is not the secret bookshelf. The secret bookshelf, in most houses, is in the bedroom, or in the office, or in the back of a closet. The living-room bookshelf is the bookshelf the household has agreed is part of how the household presents itself to the world. And yet the bookshelf, even the curated one, even the matching-color one, even the one organized by decade or genre or author, is more honest than almost any other surface in the house. The bookshelf cannot easily be redone. The bookshelf takes years to assemble. The bookshelf, by the time it is full, has been added to by many different versions of the same person, and the shelf does not bother to pretend otherwise.

I find, when I look at other people's bookshelves, that I learn more from them than from most of the conversations I have had with those people. The bookshelf tells me what they have been thinking about for the last decade. The bookshelf tells me who they have been arguing with. The bookshelf tells me what they have been trying to become. The bookshelf is the closest thing most of us have to a public record of our private reading life, and the reading life is, for most people who read, the largest single part of the interior life. The bookshelf, more honestly than the rest of the house, is who we have been.

## What Belongs on the Shelf, and What Has Earned It

There is a question every serious reader has had to answer, often more than once. The question is not what to put on the shelf. The question is what has earned the shelf. A bookshelf has a finite amount of space. A bookshelf, in most houses, has been full for years. A new book added means an old book removed, or a new book added means a row squeezed a quarter inch tighter, or a new book added means a stack on the floor next to the shelf that nobody has been able to bring themselves to address.

I have a friend who solves this by having two shelves. There is the shelf in the living room, which is the curated shelf, the shelf of the books that have been deliberately chosen to be seen. And there is the shelf in the bedroom, which is the working shelf, the shelf of the books that are currently being read or are about to be read or are being kept in case they might be read again. The books on the bedroom shelf are not on display. The books on the living-room shelf are. The transition from the bedroom shelf to the living-room shelf is, in her house, the transition from a book that is still being thought about to a book that has been thought about enough.

This is, I think, a wise distinction. Most bookshelves do not make it. Most bookshelves treat every book the same way, and the result is a shelf that is, by the end, half books that have been read carefully and half books that have been kept out of guilt. The shelf that distinguishes between the two is a more honest shelf, and a more usable shelf, and a more useful self-portrait.

I have started, in the last few years, to be more deliberate about this. The books that have earned the living-room shelf are the books I have read, or the books I am going to read, or the books I am keeping because the household needs them to be kept — the cookbooks, the dictionaries, the books of poems a friend once gave me that I am not going to reread but that I want on the shelf when I die. The books that have not earned the shelf are the books I bought in a moment of optimism in a bookshop and then never quite opened, the books I was given that did not end up being the books I needed, the books from a part of my life I have mostly left behind. Those books have gone, in small waves, to the used bookshop down the street. They have gone to the small library I read in as a child. They have gone to the friends who, I know, will read them. The shelf is a little emptier. The shelf is a little more honest.

## The Books That Were Given to You

There is a category of books on every shelf that I have come to think is more important than the books that were bought. The books that were given to you. A book that was given to you is not just a book. It is a small document of the friendship, the moment, the reason. A friend gave me a slim paperback of poems the year I graduated from college, and I have carried it through four apartments since, and the book still has, in the front, a thin inscription in pencil that I read once a year and that still means something to me that I could not have predicted when I was twenty-two. Another friend gave me a thick biography the year my father died, and the book has stayed on the shelf even though I have not opened it in fifteen years, because the book is, on the shelf, a record of the year. A cousin gave me a translation of a novel the year I got married, and the book has stayed on the shelf because removing it would feel, even now, like a small act of ingratitude I am not willing to commit.

These are not, mostly, the books on the shelf that get read again. These are the books on the shelf that get looked at. They are the books whose spines are familiar in a way the spines of the bought books are not. They are the books that have, over years, become a record of the household — the friendships that mattered, the events that happened, the years when something shifted. The shelf is, in part, a museum. The given books are the donated objects. The given books are the things that have ended up on the shelf not because of what they contain but because of who gave them.

This is one of the deeper reasons the shelf matters. A shelf that has been built entirely out of purchased books is a record of a reader. A shelf that has been built partly out of given books is a record of a life. The given books turn the shelf from a piece of furniture into a document. The given books turn the shelf into the kind of object that children, going through the house after the person is gone, will pause at longer than almost any other. The given books are why the shelf, more than any other piece of storage in the house, is a portrait.

## A Shelf That Has Been Lived With

I think the best bookshelves are the ones that have been lived with. A shelf that has been organized by an interior designer is, in most cases, a handsome shelf. A shelf that has been organized by the person who has read the books is, almost always, a more interesting shelf. The interesting shelf is the one with the small irregularities — the poetry collection shoved sideways because it is taller than the novels beside it, the cookbook that has migrated to the front of the shelf because it is being used this week, the slim green book that has been there so long that nobody quite remembers where it came from. The interesting shelf is the shelf that has been quietly edited, over years, by a person who has not been editing it on purpose but who has been, every few months, moving one book a quarter inch to the left because the book was catching the light.

The shelf that has been lived with is also the shelf that gets used. The shelf is not, in this case, a display. The shelf is the place where the books live, and the books are pulled off the shelf, and read, and put back, and pulled off again, and read again. The shelf, like the rest of the house, is in use. The shelf has been opened, and closed, and dusted, and reorganized, and allowed to slump, and reorganized again. The shelf is, in other words, the kind of object a house has when the house has been lived in for a long time. The shelf is not the kind of object a house has when the house has been decorated.

A bookshelf that has been lived with for twenty years is, I think, one of the most satisfying objects in any home. It is more satisfying than the matching couch. It is more satisfying than the curated gallery wall. It is more satisfying than the kitchen that was renovated two years ago. The shelf, after twenty years, has become an honest record of a life. The shelf cannot be faked. The shelf cannot be bought. The shelf is, in the end, the only piece of furniture in the house that gets more honest with age. And that is, I have come to think, the deepest thing the shelf does. The shelf is the place where the house grows more like the person who lives in it, year by year, until the house and the person have, quietly, become the same.
