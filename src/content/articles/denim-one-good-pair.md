There is a pair of jeans folded on the bench at the foot of the bed. They have been folded on that bench, in roughly the same position, for the better part of a decade. The denim has gone pale at the thigh and almost white at the knee. The right back pocket has a small, soft pull where a wallet has lived for years. The selvedge line, that narrow white-and-blue stripe that runs down the inside of the outseam, is just barely visible when the cuff turns up. They are not, by any reasonable definition, a new pair of jeans. And they are, by almost every working definition, the best pair of jeans in the house.

This is the strange and rather beautiful thing about a really good pair of denim. Most of the clothes in your closet have a clear sense of when they are at their best. They arrive looking their best, they look their best for a season or two, and then they begin to decline toward a quiet retirement. Denim does the opposite. Denim agrees, from the first wear, to become something better than it was when it left the store. It agrees to be broken in. It agrees to fade. It agrees to take the shape of the person who is wearing it, and to hold that shape, and to be improved by it.

There is no other piece of clothing, in our wardrobe at least, that we have ever trusted to do this.

## What the indigo remembers

Denim, properly understood, is a piece of cloth that has been engineered to age. The warp yarns — the vertical threads that run the length of the bolt — are dyed all the way through with a particular kind of indigo that sits on the surface of the cotton rather than soaking into it. This is, as far as fabric chemistry goes, an unusual choice. Most dyes try to become part of the fiber. Indigo, in denim, refuses to. It stays, stubbornly, on the outside of the yarn. It agrees to come off, slowly, every time the cloth is worn or washed.

This is why a pair of raw denim, taken off the shelf at the denim shop, is so dark. The cloth has not yet given up any of its color. The cloth has not yet been asked. And this is why, a year later, the same pair of jeans looks completely different. The indigo has begun to migrate. The thighs, where the cloth has been pulled and rubbed against itself most often, have begun to fade. The knees, where the cloth has been bent a thousand times, have begun to soften. The seat, where the cloth has done the most sitting, has gone almost cerulean. The indigo has not disappeared. It has simply been redistributed, by the slow, repeated geography of how you live.

This is what we mean when we say denim remembers. Most cloth, by the time it has been worn for two years, has only a very general sense of having been worn. Denim has a precise sense. Denim knows where your wallet sits. Denim knows which leg you cross over which. Denim knows, by the distribution of its fading, whether you are the kind of person who keeps things in the front pockets or the back.

The cloth, in this sense, is keeping a record of you. And the record is not embarrassing. The record is, in fact, the point.

## The break-in is the relationship

There is a moment, somewhere in the first month of a new pair of jeans, when you realize that the jeans have started to feel like yours. The waist, which was a little too tight at the shop, has begun to give. The seat, which sat stiffly across the back of a chair on day one, has begun to relax. The inseam, which used to pull every time you squatted, has begun to break in along the crease of the hip.

This is the moment the jeans stop being an object and start being a relationship.

Most pieces of clothing, in our experience, are largely transactional. You buy them. You wear them. You wash them. You throw them out when they wear out. The relationship between you and a piece of clothing is, in most cases, a relationship of use. Denim is different. Denim asks for a longer courtship. Denim asks that you wear it, ten or fifteen times, before the first wash. Denim asks that you live in it before you judge it. Denim asks for a small, repeated investment of attention that most cloth does not require.

And in return, denim gives you something that almost no other cloth gives you. It gives you a pair of trousers that knows you. It gives you a pair of trousers that no one else, in the world, has a pair of. Every faded crease on the thigh is yours. Every softened spot on the seat is yours. The way the knee has begun to bag is, finally, the way your knee bags.

This is what the denim people, when they get particularly enthusiastic, call the break-in. We would call it something else. We would call it the long beginning of the friendship.

### the small choices that decide the pair

Not all denim is built to do this. Most of the denim you can buy today, at the kind of shop where jeans are sold in stacks and priced by the dozen, is built for a much shorter relationship. It is built, in fact, to look, on the day of purchase, like a pair of jeans that has already been broken in for a year. It is sold pre-faded, pre-softened, pre-distressed. It has, in a sense, been broken in by someone else before you.

This kind of denim is fine for a Saturday. It is not the denim we are talking about.

The denim we are talking about is the kind you buy from a maker who still, somewhere in the back of the workshop, has a roll of unsanforized cotton. It is the kind whose tag tells you, in small type, the weight of the cloth in ounces. It is the kind whose selvedge line, if you turn up the cuff, looks like a small flag of intent. It is denim that has been woven slowly, on old shuttle looms, by people who still think of cloth as a thing that has a biography.

You can, of course, find a hundred reasons not to buy this kind of denim. It costs more. It fits less easily, at first. It looks, the first month, slightly too dark to wear anywhere but the kind of places where slightly too dark is appropriate. We would argue, mildly, that these are the reasons to buy it.

The first month is, after all, the only month where you have to put anything in. The next hundred are the months where the jean gives everything back.

## The economics of the long pair

It is hard, when you are standing in the dressing room of a denim shop, holding a price tag that suggests a small vacation, to remember that a pair of denim is one of the few pieces of clothing that is genuinely an investment. A coat is an investment. A tailored jacket is an investment. But most of the clothes in your closet are, in economic terms, recurring expenses. They are paid for, worn for a season or two, and replaced.

Denim, when it is the right denim, is paid for once.

A pair of well-made jeans, worn an average of twice a week and washed every few weeks, will last fifteen or twenty years before it begins, finally, to give up. The selvedge will begin to fray along the hem. The knee will thin to the point where it asks to be mended. The cotton will, in places, become almost sheer from the slow abrasion of being worn.

This is, in fact, the moment you have been waiting for. This is the moment the jean becomes, finally, the most interesting thing in the closet. A faded, broken-in, half-spent pair of selvedge jeans is a more beautiful object, in almost every way, than the pair you brought home from the shop. It has become a record of the years. It has become an artifact. It has become, in the same way a leather shoe becomes, something that is now genuinely yours.

We would not, of course, suggest that you wait twenty years to enjoy the jeans. You can enjoy the jeans tomorrow. You can enjoy them in the first week. But the long version of the joy — the joy that comes from watching a piece of cloth slowly learn the shape of your life — is not available in the first month. It is available in the second decade.

## Why one good pair is enough

There is a small temptation, in the modern wardrobe, to own several pairs of jeans. We have fallen for this temptation ourselves. We have, in the closet behind us, somewhere around four pairs of denim in various stages of life. None of them do the work of the original.

The original is the one we reach for when we do not know what to wear. It is the one we travel in. It is the one that goes to the kind of dinners where the host has asked for casual. It is the one we put on, on a Sunday morning, to walk the long way to wherever the coffee is. It does not ask us to think. It asks us, only, to be ourselves.

This is the strange, almost invisible, gift of the one good pair. It is the pair that has learned how to disappear into its own usefulness. It does not announce itself. It does not ask to be admired. It simply shows up, day after day, and asks to be of service.

There is, in the end, a particular kind of luxury in having nothing to choose from. The luxury of reaching into the closet, pulling out the one pair of jeans that has been there for ten years, putting them on, and walking out into the morning without having to make a single further decision about what to wear. The luxury of knowing the denim already knows. The luxury of letting the cloth do its small, slow, persistent work of being, by the time you have walked to the corner, exactly what the day needed you to wear.

This is the long friendship of the one good pair. This is the thing no other piece of clothing quite knows how to offer. This is the reason we keep coming back, year after year, to the same indigo. It has become us. And we, in some quiet way, have become it.
