The shelf above the bathroom door, where the spare towels used to be, has become, over the years, something else. There are, perhaps, eight bottles there now, and another four in the small drawer by the bed, and a single sample vial in the pocket of the coat I wore most this autumn. None of them is the same as any of the others. Each of them was bought for a specific reason, in a specific month, sometimes for a specific version of a person I was, briefly, trying to be. Together, they are not a collection. They are a calendar. The scent I reach for in January is not the scent I reach for in late June, and neither of those is the scent I want, in a particular way, on the long week in March when the work has been heavier than usual and the only kind thing I can think to do for myself is to smell, when I lift my wrist, of something gentler than the day.

This is what a perfume wardrobe is. It is not, despite what the glass cabinets of certain department stores would have us believe, a matter of owning many bottles. It is a matter of owning the right bottles, in the right order, for the right season of being alive. It is, in this sense, less about the perfume and more about the calendar the perfume keeps.

## A wardrobe, not a shelf

The first thing to understand about a perfume wardrobe is that it is built the way a real wardrobe is built. You do not, when you wake up in January, reach for the same dress you reach for in August. You do not, when you are preparing for the meeting that will define the quarter, wear the same shirt you would wear to the long lunch with the friend you have not seen in two years. The clothes in a wardrobe are, in this sense, not redundant. They are sequential. Each one is for a particular occasion, a particular temperature, a particular version of you that the day is asking for.

A perfume wardrobe is built on the same logic. There is the scent you reach for on the first cold morning of the year, when the light has the particular thinness that suggests the year is about to begin. There is the scent you wear to the weddings, which is, almost always, something softer than your everyday scent, because the weddings are not the days to be yourself too loudly. There is the scent you keep at the office, in the small drawer where the safety pins live, for the meetings that go on too long and the afternoons when you need, by three o'clock, a small piece of evidence that you are still a person. There is the scent, almost never the same as any of these, that you wear when you are alone in the house and you want, for reasons you cannot quite explain, to smell like the version of yourself that nobody else gets to see.

None of these is the same as any of the others. The wardrobe holds them all. The wardrobe is the small, quiet acknowledgment that you are not, in fact, the same person in February as you were in October, and that the right response to this is not to settle on a single scent and commit to it for life. The right response is to build, slowly, a small library of smells that you can put on, the way you put on a coat, depending on the weather of the day.

## The bottle that belongs to January

There is, in every perfume wardrobe, a bottle that belongs to a particular month the way a coat belongs to a particular season. It is, almost always, a heavier scent than the others - something with a base that lingers, something with the kind of warmth that the early year, with its thin light and its overcast afternoons, seems to ask for. It is, often, the first bottle of the year to be opened, and it is, often, the bottle that the rest of the year is judged against.

The bottle that belongs to January is rarely the most expensive one on the shelf. It is rarely, in fact, the most impressive. It is, more often, the bottle that smells, in some reliable way, like the inside of a coat you have worn for years. It is the bottle that does not announce itself. It is the bottle that you forget you are wearing by lunchtime, and that other people, in passing, mention at three o'clock - that something about you smells, today, like the right kind of steady.

This is also the bottle that takes the longest to replace. The January bottle is, in this sense, less a perfume than a small piece of private infrastructure. You do not, when it runs out, rush to replace it. You wear the other bottles, for a while, in its place. You notice, by the second week of its absence, that the mornings are slightly less settled than they were. By the third week, you are in a department store, or in the small perfume shop on the corner, asking, in a slightly embarrassed voice, for the same bottle you have been wearing since the year you cannot quite remember.

## The summer rotation

There is, in the wardrobe, a different kind of bottle for the summer. It is, almost always, lighter. It is, almost always, something that smells, at first spray, of something green - of fig leaves, perhaps, or of cut grass, or of the particular kind of citrus that smells, on the skin, like the inside of a cold drink. The summer bottle is, in this sense, the antidote to the January bottle. It is the bottle that says, in the language of scent, that the year has turned and that the body, which has been wrapped in wool and heavier coats for months, is now being asked to breathe.

The summer rotation, in many wardrobes, is wider than the winter rotation. There are more bottles for the warm months than there are for the cold. This is because summer asks more of us, in this respect. The heat makes the scent sit closer to the skin. The light makes the bottle more visible. The summer is, in this sense, the season in which the perfume wardrobe is most actively in use, and the season in which the wrong bottle is, more visibly, the wrong bottle.

There is, often, a particular bottle for the long holiday. There is, often, a particular bottle for the wedding in August. There is, almost always, a particular bottle for the trip to the place where it is, finally, hot enough to wear nothing but the smallest possible dress. None of these is the same. The wardrobe, in the summer, opens up. It becomes, briefly, a more generous version of itself.

## The scent that belongs to a person, not a season

There is, in many perfume wardrobes, a single bottle that does not belong to any month. It is, usually, the oldest bottle on the shelf. It is, often, the bottle that has been there so long that you have forgotten, in some quiet way, that it is there. It is the bottle that smells, to you, like a particular person - and the person it smells like is, almost always, someone you used to be.

This bottle is rarely the most beautiful. It is rarely the most expensive. It is, more often, the bottle that you bought, in a particular year, because someone wore it, or because someone gave it to you, or because you wore it, for a single season of your life, when you were a version of yourself that the rest of the wardrobe has since moved on from. It is, in this sense, the bottle that the wardrobe keeps for reasons that have nothing to do with the season.

You do not, in fact, wear this bottle often. You wear it, perhaps, twice a year - on the birthday of the person it reminds you of, or on the day when you are feeling, for reasons you cannot quite name, like the version of yourself you were when you bought it. You wear it, in other words, on the days when you need to be, briefly, the person you used to be. The bottle is the small piece of evidence, on those days, that this person is still, somewhere on the shelf, available.

## How the wardrobe is built

A perfume wardrobe is not built all at once. It is built, slowly, in the way a real wardrobe is built - one bottle at a time, over years, in response to specific days. There is the bottle bought on the trip to the city you have since left. There is the bottle bought in the airport, in the long layover, on the way to the wedding that did not, in the end, happen. There is the bottle that someone, for reasons you have never quite understood, gave you for a birthday you do not particularly remember. Each of these is, in its way, a small piece of history.

The wardrobe is built, in other words, the way a life is built - by accumulation, by accident, by the slow layering of one thing on top of another. It is not curated, in the way the glass cabinets of certain shops are curated. It is not a collection, in the sense of a collection. It is, more usefully, a record. It is the olfactory version of the photograph drawer - the place where the smells of the years are kept, in bottles, on a shelf, ready to be reopened on the days when the year, briefly, asks you to be who you were.

This is also why the wardrobe is, almost always, smaller than it looks. There are, perhaps, twelve bottles in a fully built perfume wardrobe. There are, perhaps, six that are worn regularly. The rest are kept, in the back, in the way that certain clothes are kept in the back of the closet - not because they will be worn again, but because getting rid of them would be, in some quiet way, a kind of forgetting. The wardrobe is the small piece of architecture that holds, in glass and atomizer, the years you have already lived.

## The scent of the next year

There is, every December, a moment when the wardrobe is, briefly, reviewed. The bottles that have not been worn, all year, are pushed to the back. The bottles that have been worn, every month, are brought to the front. The empty bottles are, finally, washed out and recycled. The new bottle - the one bought, in the small shop on the corner, in the last week of the year, for the year that is coming - is placed, with a certain amount of ceremony, on the shelf.

This is, in many lives, the most important moment of the perfume year. It is the moment when the wardrobe is, briefly, reimagined. It is the moment when you decide, for the year that is coming, what kind of person you are going to try, at least in part, to be. The new bottle is, in this sense, less a purchase than a quiet promise - the promise that the year ahead will, in some small way, smell different from the year that has just ended.

The promise is, almost always, kept. The new bottle is opened, on the first cold morning of the new year. The scent, briefly, fills the bathroom. You lift your wrist, later, in the kitchen, and the year is, in some reliable way, beginning. The wardrobe, on the shelf above the bathroom door, has done its quiet work. It has held, in glass, the year that has passed. It is now, with the new bottle, ready to hold the year that is coming. The calendar of who you have been is, in this small way, also the calendar of who you are about to be.
