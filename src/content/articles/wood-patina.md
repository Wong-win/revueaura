If you run your hand across the surface of a wooden table that has been in use for fifty years, you can feel the entire history of the table under your palm. The grain is still there — that part does not change — but the surface is no longer flat. There are slight valleys where the soft grain has been compressed by years of elbows, plates, hot pans, water glasses, newspaper sections spread out for breakfast. There are ridges where the harder grain has held its line against everything that has been set on top of it. The table no longer has a single surface. It has many surfaces, layered, the way a piece of land has many surfaces once you begin to look at it closely.

This is what people mean, I think, when they speak of patina. They mean the way a material begins to record the life that has passed over it. Not damage, exactly — or not only damage. More like the slow accumulation of being useful. A wooden table that has been used, spilled on, sanded, and used again is not less beautiful than the day it was made. It is more beautiful, in a way that no photograph of the new table can quite capture, because the beauty it now holds is the beauty of having mattered.

## Why We Want the Surface to Remember

I think the longing for patina is one of the deeper longings in domestic life, and we rarely admit it to ourselves. We say we want a wooden table because wooden tables are warm. We say we want a solid oak floor because oak is durable. We say we want a hand-thrown clay bowl because clay is honest. These are true reasons, but they are not the whole reason. The whole reason is that we want to be in a room that is changing in the same direction we are changing. We want to be surrounded by materials that age the way we age — slowly, unevenly, in ways that record what has happened to them.

A new piece of furniture is, in this sense, the saddest piece of furniture. It is a piece of furniture that has not yet been loved. It is a piece of furniture in its first costume, before any of the lives it will participate in have left their mark on it. We see it in the showroom and we feel a small flicker of tenderness for it — for the version of itself it is about to become — and we take it home, and we use it, and a year later we cannot remember why we ever found it cold.

The table that matters in a house is never the table that arrived last. It is the table that has been there through the most. It carries the silver scratches of the year the children first started helping in the kitchen. It carries the dark circle from the year the pipe leaked and someone set a pot under it. It carries the faint smell of lemon oil that belonged to the previous owner, twenty years ago, and has never quite faded. It is not a surface. It is an archive.

## On Forgiving and Unforgiving Materials

There is a distinction, in domestic materials, between those that forgive use and those that fight it. Wood is at the forgiving end of the spectrum, along with linen, leather, brass, wool, and unglazed clay. These materials are not destroyed by being touched. They are improved by being touched. They accept scratches as additions rather than subtractions. They age into themselves. They wear in, the way a pair of good shoes wears in.

At the other end of the spectrum are the unforgiving materials — polished granite, lacquered MDF, stainless steel with a mirror finish, anything that has been sealed under a layer of plastic or glass. These materials do not absorb use. They deflect it. The first coffee ring that lands on them stays, faintly, forever. The first knife scratch reveals a different color underneath. The first dent becomes a permanent accusation. The room made of these materials begins to feel, after a few years, like a room that is disappointed in the people who live in it.

You can see the difference immediately when you walk into a house. A house full of forgiving materials feels lived-in, in the good sense — settled, worn, warm under the hand. A house full of unforgiving materials feels staged, even when no one is trying to stage it. The surfaces have an edge to them. The light bounces off them with a kind of impatience. You feel that you have been admitted, temporarily, to a room that does not really want you there.

The cost difference, interestingly, is not always as large as we think. A solid oak table, bought once and used for forty years, costs less per year than the engineered-wood table that has to be replaced three times in the same span. A linen slipcover, washed and rewashed, will outlast three sets of synthetic upholstery. We have been taught to associate quality with shine, and shine with newness, but the materials that actually improve our daily lives are usually the ones that lose their shine first.

## The Table as a Slow Companion

A wooden table is a slow companion. It does not announce itself. It does not demand attention. It does not change week to week. But if you are paying attention, you can watch it change year to year. The darkening at the center where the afternoon sun reaches. The lightening along the edge where no one ever sits. The small crack that opened one winter when the heating was off for a week, and that has since closed again, but left a faint line. These are slow changes. They are the kind of changes that make a home feel inhabited rather than decorated.

There is a particular pleasure, on a quiet Sunday morning, in running a cloth over a wooden table and feeling, under the cloth, the grain. The grain is a kind of map. The softer parts feel slightly different under the cloth than the harder parts. You can trace a line with your fingertip and follow it across the table for almost a foot before it disappears into another line. No two tables have the same grain. No table has a grain that is meaningless. Every piece of wood is a record, frozen in cellulose, of how a tree once grew — which direction the prevailing wind came from in the year this ring was laid down, which years were wet and which were dry, which years the canopy above this branch was thin and which years it was crowded.

When we set a glass on top of all that, we are setting it on a piece of time. We are using a piece of forest as a side table. The gesture is so ordinary that we forget how remarkable it is. A two-hundred-year-old oak, still bearing the history of every year it lived, now bearing the coffee rings of every breakfast since. This is what we are asking of wood, when we bring it into our houses. We are asking it to carry us, too, the way the tree once carried the wind.

## Living with Imperfection, On Purpose

Choosing a wooden table, then, is also a choice about imperfection. We are choosing to live with a surface that will, over time, look less perfect than the day it arrived. We are choosing a surface that will record the mistakes — the hot pan set down without a trivet, the candle that dripped, the wine that was not wiped up quickly enough. The table will not be ruined by these events. It will absorb them. It will integrate them. But it will not be the same table it was.

Some people cannot tolerate this. They want their surfaces to remain as they were. They buy the lacquered versions, the sealed versions, the versions that promise, in their marketing, to remain pristine. These tables do remain pristine, for a while. And then, because life happens, they do not. The first scratch lands, and the table becomes a thing that has been ruined. It cannot be restored to its original state, because its original state was a sealed, finished state that was always going to end.

The wooden table, by contrast, can always be sanded. It can be re-oiled. It can, in extremis, be planed down by a handyman who knows what he is doing, and the new surface will go on accumulating its own patina from that point forward. The table does not have a single perfect state. It has a sequence of states, each one a little more marked than the last. There is no version of the table at which the patina is complete, because the table is still being used, and will be used tomorrow, and the day after that, for as long as the house stands.

This is, I think, what we are really choosing when we choose wood. Not a surface. A relationship. A relationship with a material that will change in our hands, that will carry our marks, that will become, in time, a kind of mirror of the years we have spent with it. We are choosing to live with something that is becoming itself, slowly, in the same room in which we are becoming ourselves.

That is a generous thing to ask of an object. Wood, fortunately, is generous enough to accept.
