There is a small bag — not a tote, not a handbag, not anything that the glossies would photograph, certainly not anything that announces itself — that sits on a hook by the door of the apartment, and that, when you put it on, somehow changes the grammar of the day. You walk out the door intending to go to the chemist. You walk out the door intending to go to the bakery, to pick up a loaf, to come straight home. You walk out the door intending, in the modest way of an ordinary Tuesday, to do one thing. And then, somewhere between the chemist and the bakery and the small park you had not planned to walk through, the day — and this is the only word for it — opens. The bag is the reason. The bag is a permit. The bag is a small, soft permission slip, granted to yourself, to walk further than you had planned, to stay longer than you meant to, to let the morning, for once, be longer than the morning.

The bag in question is, almost certainly, a backpack. Not the enormous backpack of the mountaineer, with the aluminium frame and the ice axe loop and the straps that sing when you walk. Not the technical backpack of the trail runner, with the hydration bladder and the whistle on the sternum strap. A small backpack. A backpack that holds a paperback, a water bottle, a folded jumper, a wallet, a phone, and not much else. The kind of backpack that, when empty, weighs almost nothing and rolls up into a ball you could lose in another bag. The kind of backpack that does not appear in any catalogue under the heading of gear, because it is not, in any sense, gear. It is a piece of clothing you happen to wear on your back.

This is the bag we want to argue for, gently and at length. Not because it is more efficient than a tote, though it is. Not because it is more comfortable than a shoulder bag, though it is. We want to argue for it because of what it does to the day, which is something no other bag, in our experience, does at all. The small backpack does not announce that you are going somewhere. The small backpack announces that you are open to going somewhere. The difference is the entire bag, and the entire day.

## a permit to wander

The thing about a small backpack, when you put it on, is that the day becomes walkable. You did not, this morning, when you put the bag on, decide to walk for three hours. You decided to walk for twenty minutes, to the chemist, to buy the thing you forgot to buy last night. But the backpack has, in some quiet way, expanded the radius of what is reasonable. The chemist is twenty minutes away. The bakery is forty. The park is an hour. The bookshop, the one on the other side of the park, is an hour and twenty. None of these was in the plan. All of them have become possible, simply because you are wearing a bag that could carry anything you might find along the way.

This is what we mean by a permit. The permit is not to do anything in particular. The permit is to do anything that arises. The permit is to walk into the park and stay for forty minutes because there is a heron in the pond. The permit is to walk into the bookshop and buy a paperback you will probably not read for six months but that you want to own. The permit is to walk past the bakery and decide, on a Tuesday, that today is the day you buy the almond croissant, even though the chemist was the plan, and the chemist will still be there in an hour. The backpack is the bag that allows these small derangements to happen, without your having to justify them, and without your having to carry the evidence.

The tote bag does not do this. The tote bag is too empty — it announces, to every passerby, that you have come out without the intention of buying anything, and that you are therefore a person who will not be diverted from your errand. The shoulder bag does not do this either. The shoulder bag is too full — it is the bag you have already loaded with the day's necessities, and it has, in a sense, already decided the day for you. The small backpack, by contrast, is neither too empty nor too full. The small backpack can hold the paperback you might find. The small backpack can hold the loaf you might pick up. The small backpack can hold the jumper you might need, if the afternoon turns, the way October afternoons do.

## the difference between a bag and a posture

There is something else going on, beneath the practical. The small backpack changes your posture, literally. The tote bag, in use, pulls on one shoulder. The shoulder bag pulls on one side. Both of these are asymmetries, and asymmetries, over the course of a long walk, become aches, and aches, over the course of a long day, become attitudes. You walk slightly crooked, with a tote bag, even if you do not know it. You walk slightly tilted, with a shoulder bag. You walk, after an hour, with a small complaint somewhere in the trapezius, and the complaint, by the end of the afternoon, has become an irritation, and the irritation has become, by the evening, a mood. This is not the bag's fault. The bag is doing its job. But the bag is doing its job in a way that produces, by the end of the day, a body that is slightly less patient with the world than it was in the morning.

The small backpack, by contrast, distributes its weight across both shoulders. The small backpack, when you put it on, levels you. The small backpack lets you walk the way you walked as a child, before anyone taught you that bags were carried on one side. The small backpack lets you walk upright, with both arms free, with both hands available for holding a coffee, for adjusting a hat, for pointing at a heron, for taking the hand of a small child. The small backpack is a bag that returns your body to you.

We think this is part of the reason the small backpack is the bag of walkers, and has been for many centuries. The monks carried their books on their backs. The postmen carried the mail on their backs. The small backpack is the bag of people who are going somewhere on their own two feet, and who would like to arrive without having spent the journey arguing with their luggage. It is, in this sense, the most democratic bag there is. It does not care who you are. It cares only that you are going on foot, and that you would like both shoulders to think about it.

## the things the bag has collected

If you keep a small backpack by the door, as we are gently suggesting you might, you will discover, over a few months, that the bag has begun to accumulate things. There will be the paperback you bought on a Tuesday, in the bookshop past the park, that you are still carrying around because you have not finished it. There will be the small umbrella you bought at a chemist on a rainy afternoon, because the chemist was having a sale. There will be a packet of tissues, a tube of hand cream, a pair of folding sunglasses you have owned for seven years. There will be a notebook you are not writing in, and a pen you are not writing with, and a piece of string you cannot explain, and a small stone from a beach you cannot quite remember. None of these things, individually, is important. All of them, together, are the bag. The bag has become, in some quiet way, a small portable room.

This is, we think, one of the deeper pleasures of a small backpack. The bag is not a vessel. The bag is a small domestic interior, the kind of interior that is small enough to carry and large enough to feel like a place. The bag is somewhere to put the book you bought, so that the book is not just a book but a bookmark in your day, the day you bought it, the day you were wearing the bag when you walked past the bookshop, the day you walked in and came out again fifteen minutes later, slightly poorer, slightly happier, with a paperback that smelled of the shop it came from. The bag is somewhere to put the stone from the beach, so that the stone is not just a stone but a stone from a Tuesday in October, when you walked further than you meant to and found, on a small beach past the harbour wall, a stone that was the exact colour of the sky that morning.

The bag is, in this sense, a kind of memory you can wear. The bag is what holds, across the days, the small evidence of the days. We do not, of course, recommend that you let the bag become a hoard. But we do think there is something to be said for a bag that is allowed to be, in some small way, yours — a bag that, over time, accumulates the small artefacts of the life you have been living while you were wearing it. The small backpack becomes, slowly, a small biography of yourself.

## the walk home

The walk home, in a small backpack, is a particular walk. The walk home is the walk in which the day begins to settle. The chemist has been visited. The almond croissant has been bought. The park has been walked through, and the heron has been watched, for slightly too long, and the bookshop has been visited, and the paperback has been added to the bag, and the afternoon, which began as twenty minutes, is now an hour and a half, and the bag, which began empty, is now slightly fuller, in a way that is satisfying without being heavy.

You notice, on the walk home, that the bag has done its work. The day has been, because of the bag, slightly longer than the day. The day has been, because of the bag, slightly fuller than the day. The day has been, because of the bag, slightly more yours than the day would have been if you had worn a tote, or carried nothing at all, or stayed at home. The bag has, without your noticing, edited the day in your favour. The bag has cut away the part of the day that was going to be merely errand, and has replaced it with a part of the day that was going to be, instead, an actual life.

You take the bag off, when you get home. You hang it on the hook by the door. The bag is slightly heavier than it was this morning. The bag has, in it, the paperback and the croissant and the small stone from the harbour and the tube of hand cream and the piece of paper on which someone, in the bookshop, wrote the date in a fountain pen because they did not have a stamp. None of this was the plan. All of it is what the bag is for. Tomorrow morning, the bag will be on the hook, and you will put it on again, and the day will open again, and the day will be, again, slightly longer than the day, and slightly fuller than the day, and slightly more yours than the day would have been.

This is the small, soft argument we wanted to make. Not for gear. Not for any particular brand of small backpack, of which there are many, and which we will not name, because the bag is not the point. The point is the permit. The point is the small, soft permission slip, hanging on a hook by the door, that lets you walk further than you planned, and stay longer than you meant to, and let the day, for once, be longer than the morning. The bag is the bag. The walk is the walk. The day is yours. It has been, all along, yours.
