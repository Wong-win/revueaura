Most of us wear more than one perfume. We have the one we bought for a particular winter, the one we associate with a particular person, the one a friend gave us for a birthday we did not entirely remember. There is the travel atomizer we keep in our bag for a particular kind of evening, and the sample vial from a department store that has lived, slightly incongruously, on the dresser for two years. We have, in short, a relationship with fragrance that is closer to a library than to a wardrobe - many small volumes, arranged on a shelf, each one pulled down on the appropriate occasion.

There is another approach, which is rarer and which takes a particular kind of conviction. It is the approach of the person who wears the same scent every day. Not on special days. Not on the days when they can be bothered. Every day. The same bottle, in the same place, applied in the same way, for years at a time. This person, when you meet them, smells like one thing. It is the thing they have chosen, on purpose, to be associated with. The French have a word for it. They call it a *signature*.

## The choice of one thing

To commit to a single scent is to give up something most of us have been taught to want. We have been taught, by the beauty industry and by our own restless habits of consumption, that variety is the point. That we should own a wardrobe of fragrances the way we own a wardrobe of clothes. That the appropriate response to a new launch, or a new sample, or a friend's recommendation, is to add it to the rotation. We have been taught that the grown-up perfume wardrobe is large.

The signature says the opposite. The signature says that what we want, from a fragrance, is not novelty but recognition. That the point of wearing the same scent for years is that other people will, over time, associate the scent with us. That if we change our scent every day, no one will ever associate any scent with us. The signature is, in this sense, a long bet. We are betting that the people we encounter today will still remember, in five years, what we smelled like when we met them.

This is, of course, exactly how fragrance actually works. The reason scent is the most powerful trigger of memory is precisely because it has had time to associate with a particular person. The smell of our mother's coat. The smell of our first lover's sweater. The smell of the colleague we used to pass in the hallway at the old office. None of these are smells we encountered once. They are smells that were, for a sustained period, attached to particular bodies, in particular rooms, on particular days. The signature is what we are offering, on behalf of the people who will, in some future year, try to remember us.

## What makes a signature work

Not every scent lends itself to being a signature. A signature needs to be, at minimum, distinctive enough to be identifiable. There are scents, in the modern market, that have been engineered to smell like nothing in particular - to be unobtrusive, to be "like fresh laundry," to be invisible. These do not make good signatures. They are, by design, the scents no one will ever be able to recall.

A signature also needs to age well. The fragrance we choose today will, in five years, have evolved - both on our skin, where it has gotten used to the chemistry of us, and in our own relationship to it, where it will have acquired, through repetition, a kind of patina. A scent that seemed bracing and interesting in the first weeks of wearing it can, by the third month, begin to feel sharp. By the second year, it has either become a part of us or it has not. The signature has to be one we are willing to wear in memory, when the memory is the only thing left of it.

There is also a question of compatibility. Some scents react, on certain skins, in ways the perfumer did not intend. A fragrance that smells green and watery on a friend can smell, on us, entirely different - warmer, sweeter, faintly medicinal. The signature is a fragrance on our skin, not on someone else's. We cannot know, until we have worn it for some weeks, whether it is going to behave. This is part of the work. We are not choosing from a menu. We are choosing a relationship.

## The commitment problem

The honest reason most of us do not have a signature is that commitment, in any form, has gone out of fashion. We change jobs more often. We change cities more often. We change partners, hairstyles, wardrobes, religions. We are, by both training and circumstance, a generation that has been taught to keep options open. A signature is the opposite of an open option. A signature is a closing.

This is also, perhaps, why signatures are interesting again. After a decade of perpetual rotation, of having every conceivable thing available at the touch of a screen, of never quite committing to anything because there might be something better, the signature has begun to feel almost radical. It is a small refusal of the endless comparison. It is a declaration that we know what we like and we are going to wear it, for as long as we like it, without checking what has launched this week.

This is not, on reflection, a small thing. The signature is, in the end, an argument against the optimization of self. It says: I am not interested in being a different person on every day of the week. I am interested in being myself, recognizable, on each of them.

## The boring middle of a signature

There is a stretch in the life of every signature, sometime between the second and the fourth year of wearing it, when it stops being exciting. We have noticed all the things we are going to notice about it. We have been complimented on it, or not, and we have made our peace with whichever has happened. We have, in some cases, been tempted by a new launch and have not switched. This is the boring middle. It is also the part that matters.

The boring middle is when the signature stops being a decision and becomes a habit. We reach for the bottle without thinking. We apply it without thinking. We stop noticing whether it is there. Other people, however, do still notice. Other people are, in fact, still smelling us. The signature, which has become invisible to us, has not become invisible to anyone else. This is when it does its real work. This is when, walking past someone we haven't seen in ten, the scent in the elevator is what tells them we were there, before they have even turned around.

The signature is, in other words, a small piece of architecture we are building around our future absence. We are deciding, today, what the air will smell like when we have left the room. It is a strange thing to think about. It is also, perhaps, the kind of strange thing that gives the practice its weight.

## Choosing, deliberately

If we are going to wear a scent every day, we should wear one we have chosen, not one we have inherited. The bottle that was our mother's, the one a lover left behind, the one we bought because the ad was good - these are not signatures. They are accidents of proximity. A signature is something we have walked toward deliberately, smelled on our skin for weeks, worn in public enough to know how it behaves, and chosen, with our eyes open, to keep.

There is no rule about what kind of scent should be a signature. Some people wear the heaviest, most animal of the orientals. Some wear something green and sharp that disappears within an hour. Some wear nothing at all, which is its own kind of signature. The point is not what we wear but that we wear it. The point is that the choice was made.

There is also no rule about how long a signature should last. Some signatures we wear for a season and then put aside. Some we wear for decades. Some we wear for life. The decision to retire a signature is, like the decision to adopt one, an act of small autobiography. We are saying, this was the person I was then. I am a different person now. I want to be remembered differently.

This is the larger point. A signature is not a logo. It is a relationship. It is the most private piece of personal branding we will ever commit to. It does not have to be commercial. It does not have to be famous. It does not have to be the same one our whole life. It has to be, for as long as we wear it, ours.
