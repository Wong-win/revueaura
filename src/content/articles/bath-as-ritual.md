The bath is the only room in the house designed to hold a single person doing nothing at all. The kitchen exists to produce food. The bedroom exists to enable sleep. The living room, for all its efforts to the contrary, exists to be seen in — either by guests or, more often, by ourselves. Even the reading chair, that small altar we set up in the corner of the room, requires us to be reading. The bath, uniquely, requires nothing of us. It only asks us to be in it.

This is, when you stop to think about it, a strange architectural decision. We have built, into a single room of almost every house, a piece of plumbing dedicated entirely to the experience of doing nothing. We heat gallons of water specifically so that a person can sit in it. We surround the tub with tile, and towel hooks, and a window (sometimes), and a stool (sometimes), and we ask of that room only that it hold a person who is, for the duration of the bath, relieved of every other task. The room has no other job. It is a small monument to the principle that occasionally, in a life structured around production, we are allowed to stop.

Most of us have lost the knack of the bath. We have been taught, by long habit and shorter showers, that bathing is about getting clean — a task to complete before the next thing, a hygiene step in a day full of hygiene steps. We shower quickly. We wash quickly. We emerge from the bathroom quickly. We do not treat the bath as a room. We treat it as a station on the way to the rest of the house. We have, in effect, removed the ritual from the ritual room.

This is a loss. Not a sentimental loss, the kind we lament but do not act on, but a practical one. The bath — taken slowly, taken without a phone nearby, taken as the central event of an evening rather than the preamble to it — does something for the nervous system that almost no other domestic practice does. It returns us, briefly, to a state we have otherwise forgotten how to reach: a state of being in a body without being asked to do anything with it.

## A Brief History of Doing Nothing in Water

We have been bathing ritually, in some parts of the world, for at least five thousand years. The bath has been a place of ritual longer than it has been a place of hygiene. The Hammam in the Islamic world, the onsen in Japan, the Roman thermae, the banya in Russia, the Korean jimjilbang — these are not, primarily, places to wash. They are places to sit, in hot water, for an hour or several, in the company of oneself or others, and to allow the body to soften. Cleanliness is a byproduct. Restoration is the point.

The bath, in these traditions, is understood to act on the person, not just the skin. The heat opens the capillaries. The buoyancy takes the spine out of gravity. The steam makes the breath slower. The muffled acoustics make the mind quieter. The combination produces a state — call it softening, call it dissolution, call it the slow returning of a body to itself — that cannot really be achieved by other means. We are soaked back into the present moment. The afternoon's emails stop mattering. The morning's argument loses its edge. The body, briefly, is enough.

Modern Western bathrooms have inherited none of this architecture of restoration. We have the tub, but we have lost most of the choreography that used to surround it. We bathe alone, behind a locked door, often in the morning rather than at the day's softest hour. We bring, into the bath, the device that most reliably prevents rest. We shower rather than soak, because showering is faster and we have things to do. When we do take a bath, we treat it as a weekend indulgence rather than a regular practice. The bath has been reduced from a ritual to a luxury. That is a category error.

## What the Bath Asks of a Room

A bath that is treated as a ritual asks something of the room it lives in. It asks, first, for warmth — not just the warmth of the water, but the warmth of the air around the water. Stepping out of a hot bath into a cold bathroom is one of the small shocks the body can do without. The floor should be warm. The air should be warm. There should be no draft. The room should feel like the inside of a held breath.

It asks for low light. The overhead fixture, if there is one, should be off. The room should be lit by a candle, or a single dim sconce, or the light that comes in through a frosted window in the late afternoon. The bath is a private, slow practice. It does not need to be seen.

It asks for a towel that is warmed, if possible. There is no small indulgence more reliable than stepping out of a bath into a towel that has been sitting on the radiator since you started running the water. The towel has been waiting for you. It is its own form of welcome.

It asks for the absence of a phone. This is the hardest of all the conditions, in the present era. The phone is the most reliable way to ruin the bath, because the phone does not know how to be in a room without being used. The phone does not understand doing nothing. It is a small loom of demand, endlessly weaving requests for our attention. Bring it into the bath and you will look at it, even if you swore you would not. You will check it "just for a second," and the second will turn into ten minutes, and the bath will have become, once again, a place where we waited for something else.

It asks, finally, for time. Not a lot of time — twenty minutes is plenty, in most cases — but uninterrupted time. The bath does not need to be a long thing. It only needs to be a true thing.

## The Substances of a Slow Bath

I do not want to make this sound prescriptive, but the substances we add to the water do change the experience, and it is worth choosing them carefully. A bath is a single sensory event. We are asking the body to be inside a medium, on purpose, for twenty minutes. The character of the medium matters.

Plain water is, of course, fine. A bath with nothing added is a bath. But a bath with a small amount of Epsom salt is a different bath — denser, more supportive, slightly more mineral against the skin. A bath with a few drops of an essential oil is a different bath again. The bathroom becomes scented. The steam carries the scent. The breath carries it. The bath has, suddenly, a third dimension.

The mistake to avoid is the bath that smells like a department store. Heavy florals, aggressive synthetics, anything that announces itself loudly in the first ten seconds — these are bath products that have not understood the difference between perfume and atmosphere. A bath product should join the room. It should be there in a way that, after ten minutes, you cannot quite detect, only sense. It should improve the quality of the air without taking it over.

I have, over the years, settled on a small number of ingredients that I trust. A handful of coarse sea salt. A few drops of something woody and quiet — vetiver, perhaps, or cedar, or a frankincense that does not try to be mystical. A slice of ginger in the water, if it is winter. A small bunch of rosemary, if it is summer. The bath becomes seasonal. It becomes connected to the month. The body learns that, in October, the bath smells like this.

## Returning to the Rest of the House

When the bath is done well, the return to the rest of the house is a different kind of return. The skin is slightly flushed. The muscles have released the tension they have been holding all day, often without telling us. The breath is slower. The mind is quieter, in the particular way that only warm water and twenty minutes of enforced stillness can produce. We are not yet asleep, but we are pre-sleep, in a good way — the kind of pre-sleep that lets the next hour move calmly toward bed.

We move, after the bath, with more attention. We notice the towel that we are using. We notice the floor we are walking on. We notice the closed door of the bedroom, and the lamp we will turn on through the gap. We are, in a way we have not been all day, fully inhabiting the room we are in. The bath has, temporarily, restored us to our own body.

This restoration is, I think, what the bath is really for. It is not for clean skin, although clean skin is a welcome side effect. It is not for the candle or the scent or the warm towel, although these are the small gifts that make the practice sustainable. It is for the restoration of attention to the present moment — a moment we lose, on most days, somewhere between the morning's first cup of coffee and the evening's last screen. The bath gives us a small, twenty-minute island on which to remember what it feels like to be a body in a room, with nothing to do.

The bath is the only room in the house designed for this remembering. We should not let the speed of our lives forget what it was built for.
