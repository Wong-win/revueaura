The bottle sits on the second shelf of the bathroom cabinet, behind the body wash and beside the spare razor. It is the largest bottle in the cabinet, larger than any of the facial products, larger than the hand soap, larger than the small tube of toothpaste that has been kept there for years. It is, in this sense, the most visible object in the room - and the one we look past most consistently. We do not, in most mornings, give the body lotion a thought. We pick it up only after the shower, only when the body is still damp, only for the briefest of gestures, only to be put back, almost apologetically, in the same place. The body lotion is, in this sense, the most neglected member of the bathroom shelf. It is also, if we are honest about it, the one that does the most quiet work.

There is a particular relationship a person has with her body lotion. It is not, in the way the other products are, a relationship of pleasure. The body lotion is not, in most cases, beautifully packaged. It is not, in most cases, the first thing we reach for in the morning, or the last thing we reach for at night. It is not, in most cases, the product we have spent the most money on. The body lotion is, instead, the product we have spent the least amount of attention on - which is, perhaps, why it is also the product whose absence we notice most quickly.

## The part of the routine we forgot

The body lotion is, almost always, the last part of the shower routine. The face has been washed. The hair has been conditioned. The body wash has done its work. The towel is around the shoulders, and the bathroom is, briefly, the steamiest room in the house. We are, at this moment, halfway out of the shower and halfway back into the day. The body lotion is the small bridge between the two.

Most of us, in this moment, are not paying attention. We have, by the time the body lotion is reached for, already begun thinking about the day. We have already begun thinking about the meeting, or the school run, or the email that needs to be answered by nine. We have, in other words, already begun leaving the bathroom. The body lotion is, in this sense, the thing we do while we are already, in our minds, somewhere else.

This is, perhaps, why most of us do it wrong. We do not, in most cases, use enough. We do not, in most cases, take the time to apply it properly. We do not, in most cases, give it the two minutes it actually needs to do the work it is supposed to do. We smear it on, half-heartedly, in the places we can reach without thinking - the arms, the shoulders, the front of the legs - and we put the bottle back on the shelf, and we move on with the day. The body lotion has, in this sense, become the part of the routine that happens in the margins.

## The two minutes that change the skin

The body lotion, done properly, takes two minutes. This is not a long time. It is, in fact, the shortest part of any beauty routine that actually does something visible. The two minutes begin with the body still damp from the shower, and they end with the body, all of it, lightly coated in something heavier than the body itself produces.

The two minutes are not, despite what certain bottles would have us believe, about the lotion. The lotion is, in most cases, doing relatively simple work. It is, mostly, replacing the moisture that the hot water has just stripped from the skin. It is, mostly, sealing, for a few hours, the kind of softness that the skin is, briefly, capable of. It is not, in most cases, doing anything more dramatic than that.

What the two minutes are actually about, if we are honest about it, is the attention. The two minutes are the only two minutes of the day, in most lives, during which we are required to touch every part of the body. The arms, the shoulders, the chest, the back of the neck. The stomach, the hips, the small of the back. The legs, from the ankles to the thighs. The feet, including the heels, which are, in most lives, the part of the body that gets the least amount of any kind of attention. The two minutes are, in this sense, the only two minutes of the day when we are required to take a full inventory of the body that has been carrying us around since we woke up.

This is also why the two minutes matter. The skin does not, in most cases, need the lotion as much as it needs the attention. The skin does not, in most cases, change in any visible way because of what is in the bottle. The skin changes, when it changes, because of the fact that we have, for two minutes, been touching it. The lotion is, in this sense, the small permission we give ourselves to take the inventory.

## The bottle that lasts the winter

There is, in most bathrooms, a particular bottle that lasts the winter. It is, almost always, larger than the bottles of summer. It is, almost always, heavier in the hand. It is, almost always, the bottle that smells, in some reliable way, like the inside of a coat - something with a base that lingers, something that promises, on the first cold morning of the year, that the skin will, despite the weather, be soft.

This bottle is rarely the most expensive. It is rarely the most beautifully packaged. It is, more often, the bottle that was bought, in some unglamorous drugstore, in the first week of October, by the person who has decided, again, that this will be the year she takes care of the body as well as the face. The bottle is, in this sense, the small private declaration of the new season. It is the bottle that says, in the language of the bathroom shelf, that we are about to enter the months when the skin needs more help than it usually does.

The bottle that lasts the winter is, almost always, used up faster than expected. This is because the winter is, in most climates, the season when the body lotion actually matters. The summer air is humid enough that the skin, in most cases, takes care of itself. The winter air is not. The winter air is, in most climates, the kind of dry that strips the skin of the moisture it has been quietly holding all summer. The bottle that lasts the winter is, in this sense, the bottle that does the most actual work of any bottle in the bathroom.

## What the skin remembers

There is, in the way the skin behaves across a year, a small piece of evidence for the body lotion. The skin that has been moisturized, daily, for the length of a winter, behaves differently in the spring. It is softer. It is less reactive. It is, in certain lights, almost the skin we had before the cold weather began. The skin that has not been moisturized, daily, for the length of a winter, behaves differently too. It is drier. It is more easily irritated. It is, by March, the skin that has been, all winter, working harder than it needed to.

This is also why the body lotion is, in many lives, the first thing to slip when the routine gets busy. We do not, in most cases, slip the serum. We do not, in most cases, slip the cleanser. We slip the body lotion, because the body lotion is the part of the routine that happens in the margins, and the margins are the part of any routine that gets sacrificed first when the day begins to compress.

But the body lotion is also, if we are honest about it, the first part of the routine to come back. The skin, by the second week of the slip, has begun to remind us. The arms feel tighter in the long-sleeved shirt. The legs feel less comfortable under the dress. The heels, by the third week, are the heels we had in February. The body lotion, in this sense, has a way of being missed before it is, formally, missed. The skin remembers. The skin asks for it back. The bottle is, eventually, taken off the shelf.

## The morning the bottle is opened

There is, in every long winter, a particular morning when the body lotion is, finally, opened. It is, almost always, the first cold morning of the year. It is the morning when the bathroom is, briefly, colder than the bedroom, and the body, after the shower, is more aware of itself than it usually is. The bottle is on the shelf, where it has been waiting since October. The cap is, finally, twisted off. The lotion is, finally, in the hand.

This is, in many lives, a small and slightly ridiculous ceremony. The lotion is not, in most cases, expensive. The lotion is not, in most cases, the most beautiful thing we have bought this year. The lotion is, more often, the unglamorous bottle we have been postponing for weeks, in the same way we postpone the first hot drink of autumn and the first heavy coat of winter. The bottle is, in this sense, the small piece of infrastructure that the cold has been asking for, and that we have, finally, agreed to provide.

The morning the bottle is opened is also, in many lives, the morning the routine gets, briefly, reorganized. The body lotion moves from the margins of the shower to the center. The two minutes that used to be the half-minute we gave it become, in the cold, the two minutes it always should have been. The body, on the first cold morning of the year, is given the attention it should have been given all along. The bottle, finally, is doing what it was bought to do. The winter, in this small and quiet way, has been, briefly, welcomed.
