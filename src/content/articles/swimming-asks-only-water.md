There is a particular moment, somewhere around the third length of an empty lane, when the mind, which had been carrying the entire day with it, finally puts the day down. You came into the water with the to-do list folded neatly into your chest, the small knot of a meeting still in the shoulders, the half-remembered email still pulsing at the back of the head. You pushed off the wall, took two strokes, took three, took ten. And somewhere between the tenth stroke and the twentieth, the list dissolved. The meeting receded to the deep end. The email went somewhere you could no longer reach. The shoulder knot, the one that had been there all morning, softened by half a centimetre. By the time you touched the wall at the far end, you were not the person who had walked into the building. By the time you turned, you were someone much older and much lighter — the person who existed before the meeting, before the email, before any of the days you have been carrying around. This is what swimming does. This is, in our view, the entire reason for the sport.

We want to argue, gently, for swimming. Not in the competitive sense. Not in the Olympic sense. We want to argue for the practice of putting the body into water, on a regular basis, and staying there for forty minutes, and seeing what is left when the body is no longer in the air. We want to argue for it because swimming is, in our experience, the most underpriced form of rest available to a body that has spent the week in a chair. We want to argue for it because swimming asks almost nothing of the participant. The sport does not require lycra. The sport does not require a partner. The sport does not require, in any meaningful sense, a particular age, or a particular kind of body, or a particular kind of weather. The sport requires, in its purest form, exactly what its name suggests: a body, and water.

This is, in an era of expensive wellness, a quietly radical thing.

## the body in the water, and how it changes

The body, when it enters the water, becomes a different body. This is a fact we tend to forget, on land, where the body is heavy at the bottom, aching in the middle, complaining about gravity in the way that all bodies on land eventually complain. In the water, the gravity complaint is briefly suspended. The body, in the water, weighs almost nothing — held up by the very thing it is trying to learn to move through. The water is, in other words, the only trainer we know of that lifts you up precisely so you can move through it.

We forget, on land, what it feels like to be a body in space without weight. We forget because we have been carrying the weight for so long that the carrying has become invisible. The shoulder knot has been there for three years. The lower back has been complaining since the chair the office bought in 2019. The water, gently, undoes all of this — not in any dramatic therapeutic way, but in the slow, cumulative way that a held thing is undone by simply being allowed, for forty minutes, to not be held.

The body, after forty minutes in the water, when it climbs out and stands on the wet tiles, is a slightly different body. The body is slightly taller, slightly lighter, slightly more its own. The shoulders are down. The breathing is slow. The face, in the mirror on the way to the changing room, has a particular openness to it — the openness of a person who has been reminded that they are, in fact, a person, and not a checklist.

## the breathing, and why it teaches you everything

The thing about swimming, the thing that no other sport does quite this well, is that the breathing is the practice. In running, the breathing is something you do while you do something else. In cycling, the breathing is automated to the point of invisibility. In yoga, the breathing is the practice, but only in the sense that you are, mostly, doing nothing else. In swimming, the breathing is the practice, and you are also moving, and the relationship between the two is the entire sport.

What we mean is this. The breath, in swimming, has to be timed. The breath has to be coordinated with the stroke. The breath has to be taken, in most strokes, through the mouth, in the small window of time when the face is out of the water. The breath has to be efficient, because inefficient breathing means you are dragging your face through the air for too long, which means you are not moving through the water, which means you are swimming badly. The breath, in other words, becomes a discipline. The breath becomes, within five minutes, the only thing you are doing.

And this is, weirdly, the lesson. The lesson of swimming, more than any other sport we have tried, is the lesson of breathing. The lesson is that breathing is something you can practise. The lesson is that breathing, when it is practised, becomes slower, deeper, less anxious. The lesson is that the body, when it is given a rhythm to breathe to, will, of its own accord, find the rhythm, and will keep finding it, if you let it, for the rest of the morning.

We are not going to argue, in this column, that swimming is meditation. Swimming is not meditation. Swimming is, in fact, a lot of work — the most aerobic of the easy sports, the one in which you can quietly raise your heart rate to something serious without ever leaving the lane. But swimming has, on top of the work, this small quiet meditative layer that we have not found in any other sport. The breath, in swimming, becomes, by the third length, the only thought. The stroke follows. The water follows the stroke. The morning, by the fifteenth length, has become the entire morning, and the morning has nothing in it except the next breath, and the next breath, and the next.

## the lane, and how it edits the day

The lane is one of the great inventions of the modern city. The lane is a rectangle of water, painted on the bottom of a pool, that exists to keep one swimmer out of the way of another — a piece of infrastructure designed for competition that turns out to be useful for almost everything else.

The lane, in use, becomes a small contained room. The lane has a wall at each end. The lane has, on either side, the gentle disturbance of the lane rope — the small floating plastic sausages that keep you from drifting into the next lane, and that create, without intending to, the soft boundary of a small private world. There is no phone in the lane. There is no email in the lane. There is no colleague, no doorbell, no dog. There is, in the lane, only the next wall, and the next wall, and the next wall.

This is what the lane does, gently and without fuss. The lane edits the day. The lane edits out the part of the day that was going to be the small unpleasant undercurrent of the modern, and replaces that part, simply, with the next stroke. The lane is, in this sense, the smallest possible holiday you can take — a forty-minute trip to a place where the requirements are very few and the rewards are, in our experience, surprisingly large.

## the water, and what it asks

The water is the entire sport. The water is, in fact, more than the sport — the water is the older partner, the thing that was here long before we were, and the thing that will be here long after we are not. The water asks very little. The water asks, on most days, to be entered. The water asks, when you enter it, that you be reasonably careful with the people sharing the lane. The water asks, beyond that, almost nothing.

We mention this because it has become fashionable, in the modern wellness landscape, to add things to the water. There are aqua aerobics classes. There are aqua jogging belts. There are waterproof bluetooth speakers marketed toward the kind of person who would like, on a Tuesday morning at seven, to do their laps to a soundtrack. We are not going to argue against any of these. We are, however, gently arguing for the simpler version — just you, in the water, with no soundtrack, with no coach, with no class, with no plan other than to put your face in, and to take it out again at the other end.

The simpler version is, in fact, the older version. People have been swimming for pleasure for at least several thousand years, in most cases without a soundtrack and without a clip on the chest. They have been doing it with whatever they brought to the water that morning — the day they were carrying, the ache they were carrying, the small knot of a thing that was worrying them. The water has been taking these things, gently, for several thousand years. The water is still taking them, on Tuesday mornings, in the slow lane of the local pool.

## the walk back to the changing room

The walk back to the changing room, after a swim, is a particular walk. The walk is slower than the walk in. The walk is slower because the legs, after forty minutes in the water, are slightly heavier than they were. The walk is slower because the body is, by this point, in a small quiet agreement with itself, and the agreement does not want to be hurried. The walk is slower because there is, on a swim morning, nothing in particular to be hurried toward. The day has already been edited, by the water, in the small way that the water edits days. The day has been put down. The day will, when you get to the changing room, be picked up again — but the day, when it is picked up, will be a slightly different day, the day of a person who has been in the water this morning, and whose body, when it sits down at the desk later, will be slightly less inclined to argue with itself about the chair.

You dry off. You put the wet costume in the small bag you have brought for it. You put the goggles in their case. You push open the door to the morning, which is, depending on the time of year, either still cold or slightly less cold. The cold, on swim mornings, is part of the practice. The cold, on the doorstep of the pool, is a small punctuation mark at the end of a sentence. The cold tells the body, briefly, that it is back on land. The cold tells the body, very briefly, that the water is finished, for today.

But the water, of course, is not finished. The water will be there tomorrow morning, and the morning after that, and the morning after that. The water is, in this small way, the most reliable thing in the week. The water does not cancel. The water does not reschedule. The water does not, on most days, change its lane allocations. The water is, like the morning itself, simply there, every Tuesday at seven, every Thursday at noon, every Sunday at nine, asking only that you show up, and put your face in, and take it out at the other end, and do this for forty minutes, and then walk home, slightly lighter than you walked in.

This is the small, soft argument we wanted to make. Not for laps. Not for a particular pool, of which there are several in most cities, and which we will not name. Not for any particular stroke. The argument is for the water, and for the breath, and for the slow Tuesday morning that the water makes possible, in a body that has been carrying the rest of the week for too long. The water is the sport. The water is, in our view, the rest. The water has been doing both of these jobs, quietly, for several thousand years. It will do them again on Tuesday. It asks nothing of you except that you show up.
