There is a small stretch of floor in every house that gets more scrutiny per square foot than anywhere else in the building. It is not the kitchen floor, where the cook stands for an hour at a stretch. It is not the bedside floor, where the cat waits to be let out. It is the first six feet past the front door — the strip between the threshold and the rest of the apartment — that every visitor walks across within seconds of arriving, and forms a permanent impression of, and remembers for years afterwards. This is the entryway. It is, by any honest accounting, the most over-determined six feet in the whole house.

Most of us treat it as the place where coats hang. We put up a row of hooks along one wall and consider the room designed. We set a small table beside the door for the mail — the mail that, in any reasonable accounting, could have been walked to the kitchen and left on the kitchen counter instead. We put a bench against the opposite wall, because benches are what entryways are supposed to contain, and we sit on the bench to take off our shoes, and the shoes go on the rack beneath the bench, and the rack fills up, and the bench becomes the place where shoes accumulate rather than a place where anyone sits. We call this done. We move on.

But the entryway is doing more work than we have been asking of it. It is the only room in the house whose primary job is to greet. Every other room has been chosen for what we do in it once we are alone — reading, sleeping, cooking, working. The entryway is the only room whose job is to welcome someone who is not yet at home. It is the first conversation the house has with the person arriving. And like any first conversation, it sets a tone that is very difficult to revise later in the evening.

## The Threshold Is a Sentence, Not a Line

The threshold — the actual line where the outside stops and the inside begins — is a sentence the house has been saying about itself since the day it was built. A house that has been generous with its threshold, that has placed a bench and a hook and a soft mat and a small lamp within arm's reach of the front door, is saying one sentence. A house that has nothing at the threshold — a strip of cold tile, a bare bulb, a single metal hook screwed into the plaster — is saying another. The visitor reads the sentence in the first second, and rereads it every time they come back.

I have come to think that the sentence should be short. Not ornate. Not curated. Not loaded with the kind of decorative objects you see in a magazine spread designed by someone who has never actually arrived home carrying a suitcase and a coat and a child and a damp umbrella. The sentence should communicate, as quickly as possible, three things: that this house has heard you coming, that you are expected, and that you will be asked to do almost nothing for the next hour except maybe take off your shoes.

A bowl for the keys, with the faint dent from the night you dropped them on the floor at one in the morning, does some of this work. A wooden bench that has been lived on enough to have one corner worn pale by the back of a coat does more. A single piece of mail on the table, already opened, suggests that someone has been home for a while. A flower in a small vase — even a tired one from the Saturday market — suggests that someone has been thinking about this room.

None of this needs to be expensive. Most of it should not be. The entryway is not the place for the inherited grandmother's lamp or the sculpture bought in a moment of optimism in a gallery in another city. The entryway is the place for the modest, the familiar, the in-use. A good entryway is one that does not look like it has been set up. It looks like it has been happening.

## The Bowl, the Hook, and the Lowering of the Shoulders

There is a moment, when arriving home, when you set down your bag. There is a moment when you hang up your coat. There is a moment when you take off your shoes. And there is a moment when you set your keys down, with the small unthinking clatter of metal against ceramic or wood. These are four small transitions, performed in sequence. They are the only rituals most of us still practice. And they almost all happen in the entryway.

If the entryway is set up well, these four transitions happen almost without your noticing them. The bag finds a chair arm. The coat finds a hook at the right height — not too high, not so low it drags the hem on the floor. The shoes find a rack, or a corner, or the floor under the bench. The keys find the bowl. You walk into the living room as the person you actually are at home, not as the person in the coat and the shoes and the work bag. The transition has been made. The day has been put down.

If the entryway is not set up well, one of these transitions fails. The coat is draped over the back of a chair because no hook was reachable. The bag has been set on the kitchen counter, where it will stay for three days. The shoes stay on, because taking them off would have meant putting them somewhere even worse than the hallway floor. The keys migrate to a windowsill, and then to a different windowsill, and then to the pocket of a coat that is no longer being worn. The transition has not been made. The day has not been put down. You will feel, all evening, slightly weighted.

This is the small miracle of a well-designed entryway. It takes the four small rituals we all perform, and gives each one a place, so that the rituals happen as rituals — quietly, without thought, without the friction of deciding where each thing goes. The house absorbs the day. The shoulders lower. The person left standing in the living room is the person you actually live with, and not the person the office keeps for you.

## What Does Not Belong in the Entryway

It is, perhaps, easier to start with what does not belong. The entryway does not want a rug that is too soft to be stepped on. It does not want a lamp bright enough to read by. It does not want a chair upholstered in a fabric that does not forgive a wet coat. It does not want a mirror so large that the visitor feels they have arrived in a fitting room. It does not want a console table deeper than the entryway is wide. It does not want a doormat so stiff that the toe catches on it.

These small miscalculations, multiplied by every arrival of every visitor over a year, add up to a room that works against the people in it. The mirror at the wrong height becomes the mirror you walk past without using. The lamp at the wrong brightness becomes the lamp no one turns on. The chair that does not forgive the wet coat becomes the chair with the wet coat on it, and then the next chair with the next wet coat, and then the chair no one ever sits in because it is always wearing someone else's coat.

What the entryway does want is modest and obvious. It wants a hook at a height that fits a coat without crumpling it. It wants a surface at waist height, not higher and not lower. It wants a place for the bag, not a place for every bag that has ever been carried. It wants a mat, made of a forgiving material, that can be shaken out once a week. It wants a small thing that is just for the eye — a branch in a vase, a framed drawing bought at a market, a piece of stone picked up on a walk — that no one has to pay attention to but that makes the room feel like it is paying attention.

## The Threshold, When You Are the One Leaving

We tend to design entryways as rooms for the person arriving. We forget that they are also the rooms for the person leaving. Every morning, the same six feet are walked in the opposite direction. The coat comes off the hook. The bag comes off the chair. The shoes come off the rack. The keys leave the bowl. And we walk out the door into the day.

The way a morning goes is, in some quiet way, set by the way the leaving feels. If the coat is on a hook that requires taking it off a hanger and rehanging it on a smaller hook — the kind of hook that you cannot find at speed in the dark — the morning begins with a small frustration. If the keys are in a bowl that gets knocked over by the dog in the night, the morning begins with a search. If the shoes are on a rack that has slipped forward on the floor, the morning begins with a small, embarrassing wrestle in front of the door.

The good entryway is set up for the leaving as carefully as it is set up for the arriving. The same hook holds the coat in the evening that holds it in the morning. The same bowl receives the keys in the morning that released them the night before. The same floor is walked twice a day, in the same pattern, by the same feet, and the room accepts these repetitions as the actual job of an entryway. It is not a foyer. It is a switchbox — the place the house goes when the people in it transition between inside and outside, between one version of the day and the next.

A house with a good entryway is, almost without exception, a house where the mornings go a little more easily than they otherwise would, and a house where the evenings feel a little more like arrivals than like returns. That is the work of the threshold. It is small work, but it is real, and it is the most repeated work in the whole house, and it deserves more attention than it usually gets.
