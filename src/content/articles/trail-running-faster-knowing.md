There is a particular path, near where you live, that you have walked for years. The path is a loop through the small wood at the edge of the village. The path climbs a low ridge, descends into a shallow valley, crosses a stream on a footbridge that has been there since before you were born, and returns to the car park by way of a field whose grass is, in October, the exact colour of a ripe apple. You know this path. You know this path the way you know the kitchen of a house you have lived in for a long time — by feel, by the slight tug of muscle memory as the body moves through the familiar space. You know, for instance, that the path turns sharply to the left at the old oak tree, and that it climbs, after the oak, for what feels like longer than it actually is, and that the stream, at the bottom, is louder than it has any right to be. You know all of this. You have known it for years. And then, one Saturday morning in September, you decide, for no particular reason, to do the loop at a run. And the path turns out to be a completely different path.

The path turns out to have two hundred and seventy-one roots. The path turns out to have a view, just past the oak tree, that you have never seen, because it is a view you only notice when you are moving at a speed where the mind is not occupied with walking. The path turns out to have, about a kilometre in, a slight bend in the stream where the water, in early morning light, hits a particular kind of stone and makes a particular kind of glint that you would have walked past in any other week. The path, in short, turns out to be a path you did not know. The path turns out to be a path that was always there, beneath your walking, waiting for you to come back at a different speed.

This, we think, is what trail running actually is. Trail running is not the gym-and-pavement sport the magazines tend to sell it as. Trail running is, properly understood, the faster way of knowing a place. Trail running is hiking with a heart-rate attached. Trail running is the practice of taking a path you thought you knew and, by moving through it at a different speed, finding that the path has been keeping, all along, a number of small secrets from you. Trail running is, in our view, one of the more useful ways of paying attention to a place that is available to a person with two legs and a Saturday morning.

This is the practice we want to argue for, gently.

## what the run does to a path you thought you knew

The first thing the run does to the path, as we have been describing, is reveal it. The first thing the run does is turn the path from a familiar walk into a sequence of small decisions. At a walk, the path is a path. At a run, the path is a small problem every ten seconds — the root to step over, the puddle to skirt, the steep bit where the quads suddenly care about the line you have chosen, the bench where the older couple wave and you have to nod while you are still trying to breathe. The path, at a run, becomes an event. The path, at a run, is no longer something your body does without thinking. The path, at a run, is something the body has to keep doing the small work of reading, every few strides.

This is, in our view, one of the underestimated pleasures of trail running. The pleasure is not, mostly, the speed. The pleasure is not the calorie, the heart rate, the slight post-run glow. The pleasure is the act of reading a landscape at a rate at which the reading is suddenly interesting. The pleasure is the small moment of arriving, slightly out of breath, at a corner of the path you thought you knew, and finding that the corner is not, in fact, a single corner — it is two corners, the second one slightly to the right of where you remembered, and you have, in the last half-second, decided to take the second one at speed, and the decision has worked.

You will not, on the first run, know which line to take. You will, on the first run, take a number of bad lines. The bad lines are useful. The bad lines are the way the body learns where the path actually goes, as opposed to where the path looked like it went from a walking pace. The bad lines are the small bruises on the shin, the slight stumble on the loose stone, the small moment of thinking, briefly, about whether the path ahead is going to keep going up or whether the path ahead is, in fact, about to go down. The bad lines are, in time, replaced by good lines. The good lines, by the third or fourth run, become the lines you take without thinking. The good lines, by the end of the season, become the path.

## what the path does to a body that runs it

The body, when you start trail running, will complain. The body will complain in ways the body did not complain when you were walking the same path. The calves, especially, will complain — the calves are the muscle group that has to do the work of stabilising the foot on every uneven surface, and the calves, after a few runs of trail running, will be doing work they have not done for years, possibly decades. The ankles, in particular, will begin to feel things. The ankles will feel the small twist on a root you did not see. The ankles will feel the small landing on a stone you cannot quite remember deciding to land on. The ankles, by the end of the first month of trail running, will have become, in the small quiet way of ankles, a slightly more capable piece of equipment than they were before you started.

We mention this not to put you off, but to be honest about the early weeks. The early weeks of trail running are, by most accounts, slightly uncomfortable. The early weeks involve, in our experience, a number of small adjustments — to the shoes, to the routes, to the willingness to walk the uphills when the legs say so. The early weeks are the part of the practice that the magazines skip, because the early weeks do not photograph well. The early weeks are also, we should say, the part of the practice that is doing the work. The early weeks are the weeks during which the body, which has been on a path, gradually re-learns what a path actually requires of it.

The body, by the second month, will have stopped complaining quite this much. The body, by the second month, will be doing the small stabilising work without thinking. The body, by the second month, will be the body that, on the Saturday morning, no longer has to make a decision about whether the path is possible. The body, by the second month, will be the body that assumes the path is possible, and gets on with the small specific work of finding the line. The body, by the end of the season, will be a slightly different body — a body that knows, in the small quiet way of bodies that know things, the difference between a path you are walking and a path you are running.

## the seasons, and how the path is different in each

A path, run in summer, is a particular path. A path, run in autumn, is a different path. A path, run in winter, is a different path again, and a path, run in spring, is the same path you thought you knew, except that everything in it has come back into leaf, and the stream is louder, and there is, in the small pool at the bottom of the valley, a frog you would not have seen in any other season.

This is one of the deeper pleasures of running the same path across a year. The path is not the same path. The path, in any given month, is a small study in what the month is doing to the landscape. The path, in November, is a study in mud. The path, in November, has small mud sections that, six months earlier, were small grass sections, and the difference matters for the line you take. The path, in January, is a study in ice. The path, in January, has a particular kind of half-frozen puddle at the bottom of the valley that is, on the third step after it, suddenly not as solid as you thought it was, and that will be the small story of the run. The path, in March, is a study in mud again. The path, in March, has birds in it. The path, in March, has the first returning chiffchaff at the oak tree, which is the same oak tree that, in August, had a small flock of long-tailed tits moving through it, and the same oak tree that, in October, has a redwing under it eating the fallen apples.

The path, run across a season, is, in other words, not one path. The path, run across a season, is approximately ten paths. The path, run across a season, is a small gallery of the months, opened up by the fact of doing the same loop, in different weeks, at a slightly elevated heart-rate. The path, by the end of the season, has become, in your head, a more richly populated place than the path was, even six months earlier. The path, by the end of the season, is the path you actually know.

## the walk back to the car

The walk back to the car, after a run, is a particular walk. The walk is slower than the run, partly because the legs, after forty minutes of uneven ground, are slightly heavier than they were, and partly because the breath would like to keep moving at the speed of recovery. The walk back is a walk through the same wood — but a slightly different wood, where the small birds that flew out of the way an hour earlier have come back to the same branches. The walk back is, in our experience, the part of the morning in which the body and the wood meet, briefly, at a speed the wood can keep up with.

You get back to the car. You sit in the driver's seat for a few minutes before starting the engine, in the slightly stupid way that people who have just been running tend to sit in their car for a few minutes before starting the engine. You look, through the windscreen, at the path that you have just run.

The path, of course, will be there tomorrow. The path will be different tomorrow, in the small way of a slightly different weather and a slightly different light. The path, by the end of the season, will be a path you know by heart — a path that knows you back, in the small way that all regularly visited places know the person who visits them.

This is the small, soft argument we wanted to make. Not for speed. Not for any particular pair of shoes. The argument is for the same loop, run across a season, until the loop becomes a small interior landscape — the Saturdays, the bad lines, the better lines. The argument is for the practice of getting to know a path by heart, in the way that only the body, at the slightly elevated pace, can. The path is there. The path has always been there. Run it, on a Saturday morning, for forty minutes, and see, by the end of the season, what the path was keeping from you.
