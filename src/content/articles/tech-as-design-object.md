Walk into any well-designed apartment of the last decade and you will notice, before the sofa, before the art, a small altar on the desk. A laptop, closed, with its maker's logo catching the light like a tiny chapel window. A phone, face down, in a leather cradle. Perhaps a pair of headphones coiled with the care of a rosary. The objects are not being used at this moment, and yet they are clearly on display. They are not appliances. They are part of the room's vocabulary of taste.

We have, without quite noticing, begun treating our electronics the way earlier generations treated their watches, their pens, their pipes — as small monuments to a kind of life one is trying to live. The mid-century desk of a writer or a designer was a stage set for a certain kind of cultivated selfhood; the same is now true of the writer who keeps only a clear desk, a single lamp, and one beautiful machine at the center of it. The technology has, in a curious reversal, become the still life.

## Material Culture in Aluminum

There was a time when a computer was a beige box behind a desk, judged almost entirely by its function, the way a refrigerator is judged. That era, surprisingly recent, is over. The iMac G4, the original MacBook Air, the first Kindle — each of these was a moment when the consumer technology industry discovered that the body of the device could be an argument. The case could be a color. The seam could be a thesis. The weight in the hand could be a verdict on the era that made it.

Design historians call this *material culture*, which is a polite way of saying that objects are never just objects. A room furnished with mid-century modern is making a statement; a room furnished with brass and velvet is making a different one; a room furnished only with a single beautiful screen, a single beautiful lamp, and a single beautiful chair is making a third, and it is this third that has come to feel native to our period. The clean desk, the soft white desk lamp, the discreet speaker — these compose what one might call a monastic aesthetic of technology. It is no accident that so many product photographs now resemble the inside of a chapel.

The reverence is genuine, and not entirely irrational. When the software on these devices changes every week, when the news cycle around them never rests, when the underlying business model of the industry is occasionally something to be uneasy about, the physical object offers a kind of constancy. The aluminum does not pivot. The weight in the hand remains. The hinge still opens with the same soft resistance. In an age of accelerated obsolescence, the design object is one of the last things one can reliably *keep*.

This is, we suspect, why the aesthetics of our devices have moved in the direction they have — toward the matte, the seamless, the nearly seamless, the quietly architectural. The design language of the high-end smartphone, with its rounded corners and its symmetrical cameras and its glass back, is the design language of a small smooth stone. It asks to be held. It declines to advertise its contents. It is, in some hard-to-articulate way, *closed* — a thing with surfaces rather than a thing with features. The user can take a kind of contemplative pleasure in the object precisely because the object refuses, politely, to display its own interiority. It is, like a good poem, an object in which what is hidden is at least as important as what is shown.

## The Quiet Theology of Industrial Design

Look, for a moment, at the design of a high-end pair of headphones, or a flagship camera body, or a brushed-steel desk lamp. There is something almost devotional in the engineering. The tolerances are tighter than they need to be. The surfaces are finished with a care that has nothing to do with function. The packaging itself is designed as if the unboxing were a small liturgy. We are, whether we admit it or not, in the presence of an industrial theology — a faith that good form can dignify the user, that the right object in the right hand can elevate an ordinary afternoon into something composed.

The temptation to overstate this is real, and we should resist it. The aluminum and the glass are, finally, just aluminum and glass. The product photographer has used the same five lighting tricks everyone else has used. The object will, within a year or two, be replaced by another object of approximately the same appearance. The aesthetic religion of the design object is, in the end, a religion of modest miracles: the slightly softer click of the volume button, the slightly more pleasing curve of the lid, the slightly more satisfying weight in the hand. These are real pleasures. They are also small ones. The danger is in confusing the small pleasures with a spiritual practice.

The danger of this reverence is that it can shade into a kind of cosplay. One begins to acquire the objects not for what they do but for what they say about the kind of person who owns them. The taste becomes a costume, and the costume begins to demand upkeep. The desk must be kept clean for the laptop to look right. The cables must be hidden for the lamp to read as art. The room is no longer lived in; it is curated.

There is also, beneath all this, a slightly uncomfortable inheritance. Most of our beautifully designed objects are made, in whole or in part, in places where the workers who made them would not themselves be able to afford to furnish a room with the result. The aluminum is gorgeous; the mine it came from is not. The glass is flawless; the supply chain behind it is something we would rather not sketch on a whiteboard. The aesthetic of the clean desk is, often enough, a triumph of design that quietly brackets the question of who paid for it.

We do not raise this to scold. The contradiction is genuinely structural — it is a feature, not a bug, of a globalized design economy in which the wealthy consume the labor of the less wealthy and the consumption is laundered, in part, through the language of taste. But it is worth holding both things in mind at once. The object on the desk is beautiful. The world that produced the object is not entirely so. The good reader of design, like the good reader of poetry, is the one who can hold the two together without collapsing either into the other.

## What the Object Wants

A good design object, we are told, disappears into the use. This is the old Bauhaus idea — the tool that becomes invisible in the hand. But our technology has a peculiar habit of refusing to disappear. It wants to be looked at, charged, updated, dusted. It wants to be on the desk when guests arrive. It wants its corner of the room. In this sense, our devices are not quite tools and not quite furniture but something in between: objects that participate in our lives as companions do, with their own quiet demands.

This is why, we suspect, the relationship many of us have with our favorite devices has begun to shade, slowly, into something closer to affection. We name them, sometimes — *the old MacBook*, *the work laptop*, *the camera* — in the way one names a small boat or a piano. We feel slightly guilty when we replace them, even when the replacement is materially better. We keep, in a drawer somewhere, the previous generation, the way one keeps old letters. None of this is rational. All of it is, we think, deeply human.

We are, perhaps, in a transitional moment. The next generation of devices — smaller, more ambient, more embroidered into the walls and the wrists and the cars — will not lend themselves to altars in quite the same way. The phone, as a single hand-held object one places on a charging pad at night, may be the high-water mark of technology as a discrete still-life subject. Already, the screens are disappearing into the walls; soon the cameras will be too.

What will remain, we suspect, is not the device but the *impulse* — the human desire to surround oneself with a small number of beautifully made things and to live, however imperfectly, inside the gesture of having chosen them. That impulse is older than any of this technology, and it is one of the better things we do. It is what makes the difference between a room that is merely occupied and a room that is inhabited. And it is, in the end, a thing that no algorithm, however elegant, can do for us.
