The thing about a mountain is that it does not care about your inbox. This is the first thing you discover, somewhere around the second hour of the climb, when your phone has lost signal and your shoulders have lost the particular tension they have been carrying since Tuesday. The mountain is its own event. It was here before you, and it will be here long after you, and the trail you are following was probably walked by shepherds and smugglers and lovers long before anyone thought to put a signpost on it. You are, briefly, a footnote in a much longer sentence. It is a great relief.

There is a moment, on most hikes, when the trail emerges from the trees and the view opens up, and you feel the altitude in your chest before you feel it in your legs. It is a small physiological event — the air is thinner, the barometric pressure has dropped, your heart is working a little harder — but it registers, almost immediately, as a kind of cognitive shift. The things that were pressing on you at the trailhead feel different up here. Not unimportant, exactly. Just less central. The way a distant mountain looks less central than the tree in front of you, even though it is much larger. Perspective, it turns out, is mostly a matter of where you are standing.

## What Altitude Does to the Mind

There is a long literary tradition of going up mountains to think clearly. The desert fathers went up. The Romantics went up. Nietzsche went up. Mary Oliver wrote her best poems on the trail and at the cabin, and it shows. The reason is not mystical, although it feels mystical at the time. It is biomechanical. Above a certain elevation, the brain begins to receive slightly less oxygen, and slightly more carbon dioxide, and this produces a state that researchers have started to call, without irony, soft euphoria. The body is mildly stressed. The mind interprets mild stress as importance. The mundane falls away because the body is otherwise occupied.

But I think there is something more than chemistry. There is the question of attention. On a trail, attention is given over entirely to the body and to the landscape. You cannot read. You cannot reply to messages. You cannot even, really, plan. The trail demands its own attention, in real time, in three dimensions. Where to put the foot. Whether to cross the stream here or twenty yards upstream. Whether the rock is stable. Whether the cloud on the horizon is the friendly kind or the concerning kind. This is the original human interface with the world, and it is very good at quieting everything else.

I have found that the decisions I make on a mountain tend to be better than the decisions I make at my desk. Not because I am suddenly wiser. Because I am paying attention to fewer things. The cognitive load of a desk is enormous — the half-heard conversations, the open tabs, the small social negotiations, the ambient anxieties about what might be happening elsewhere. On the trail, the load is honest. Eat. Drink. Walk. Stay warm. Stay found. Get down. Everything else is commentary.

## The Trail As a Teacher of Patience

Hiking is, among other things, a long lesson in the difference between speed and progress. A trail has its own speed, which is not yours. The mountain does not care whether you reach the summit by noon. The trail does not flatter you when you climb fast and does not punish you when you climb slow. It simply asks that you keep going, and rewards you, when you do, with the next view, which is always slightly better than the last.

This is a strange thing to learn in a culture that has built its entire economy on the conflation of speed and progress. We have apps that promise to make us faster. We have gadgets that promise to make us faster. We have meetings about how to make us faster. And yet, on the trail, the people I know who actually finish long hikes are the ones who learn to stop trying to be fast. They are the ones who learn to walk at the pace the trail wants, and to stop when their body asks them to stop, and to look at the small things along the way. The ones who try to hustle up the mountain end up tired, sore, and often not at the summit at all.

I think this generalises. I think most of what we are trying to do in life is more like a long hike than we admit. There is a route. The route is mostly clear, with a few ambiguous sections. There are other people on the trail. There are times when the trail forks and you have to pick a direction without full information. There is weather. There is the body's honest feedback. And there is, eventually, the summit, which is usually less dramatic than you imagined, and which is mostly the occasion to sit down and eat something and look at the view.

## What We Carry Up and What We Carry Down

There is a small tradition, among hikers, of carrying a stone in the pack for the climb and leaving it at the summit. I used to find this sentimental. I no longer do. I think the stone is a stand-in for whatever it is you brought up that you no longer need to carry. A worry. A resentment. A version of yourself that is no longer accurate. You climb with it. You set it down. You come back down lighter.

This is not a metaphor I would have used ten years ago. But I have noticed, on my own hikes, that there is almost always some thought I brought up that did not survive the descent. A friendship that had been quietly souring for a year. A project that had stopped being the right project. A relationship that had stopped being the right relationship. These things do not, of course, change because of the altitude. But the altitude gives me a vantage from which I can see them more clearly, and the small physiological gift of soft euphoria gives me a small grace period in which to consider what I want to do about them. By the time I get back to the trailhead, the decision has usually already been made, quietly, somewhere on the third switchback.

I want to be careful here. Hiking is not therapy. It is not a cure. It is not, despite the language of wellness influencers, a way to "reset" yourself between meetings. Hiking is its own thing, and it has its own rewards, and those rewards have nothing to do with optimising your mood or your week. The clarity you find on a mountain is the clarity that comes from any long, honest piece of work that takes you out of your ordinary frame. It is the clarity of the painter at the end of a long day in the studio. The clarity of the cook in the last hour of a long dinner. The clarity of anyone who has done something with their body for long enough that the mind has had to take a back seat.

## Returning to the Sea Level

The strange thing about coming down from a hike is that the world you re-enter is, mostly, the same world you left. Your inbox is still there. Your deadlines are still there. The conversation you were avoiding is still waiting. But you are different, in some small way, and the small way is enough. The next decision you make at your desk will be a little cleaner. The next conversation will be a little more patient. The next small crisis will be a little less central. Not because the mountain has done anything to your circumstances. Because the mountain has reminded you, for a few hours, that your circumstances are smaller than you had been pretending.

This is, I think, the actual gift of hiking. Not the views, although the views are real. Not the exercise, although the exercise is real. The gift is the reminder that there is a pace at which life makes sense, and that pace is not the pace of the city. It is the pace of the body on the trail. It is the pace at which our species actually evolved. We are, all of us, slightly mismatched with the modern world, and the mountain is one of the few places where we can feel the correct fit, even briefly, even for a weekend, even just for the long climb back to the trailhead.

There is also, I want to add, the quiet social grace of being on the trail. You greet other hikers. You step aside on a narrow path. You share water at a spring, or shade at a switchback. You compare notes about the weather, the trail, the river crossing. None of this is profound. All of it is human. The trail seems to make people polite in a way that the city, these days, often does not. The eye contact returns. The small courtesies return. The willingness to acknowledge a stranger as a person, briefly, before continuing in opposite directions — this returns. It is not the main reason to hike. But it is one of the reasons I keep going back, and one of the reasons the trailhead always feels, to me, like a small homecoming.
