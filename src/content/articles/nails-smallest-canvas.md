The first thing you noticed, when you came back from the manicurist, was not the color. It was the way the rest of you looked different. The hands you had been carrying around for years, the hands that washed the dishes and held the heavy bags and signed the long forms, were suddenly the hands of a slightly more deliberate version of yourself. The cuticles looked pushed back. The shape was tidier. The polish was doing something small and definite in the world. You walked out of the salon and held your coffee cup in a slightly different way, and the woman at the next table glanced at your hands in passing, and you felt, for the rest of the afternoon, like someone whose hands were being read.

The nail is the smallest canvas most of us carry around in public. Ten small rectangles, one on each finger, always visible, always out. The face we can cover with a mask. The hair we can tie up or let down. The body we can, on most occasions, hide behind a long-sleeved shirt. The nail is the part of the beauty project that cannot be hidden. It is, in this sense, the most honest surface we own - because every photograph we have ever taken, every handshake we have ever offered, every cup of coffee we have ever lifted to our mouth, has shown our nails to someone.

## Why the smallest transformation is the most complete

There is a particular kind of makeover that the beauty industry does not talk about often enough, because it is too small to photograph and too inexpensive to advertise. It is the polish change. It is the half-hour, on a Saturday afternoon, when you sit at the kitchen table with the small bottles and the cotton pads and you change the color of your nails. It is not, by any standard measure, a dramatic act. It does not require a salon. It does not require an appointment. It does not, in most cases, cost more than the price of a sandwich. And yet, when you are done, you are, in some reliable sense, a different person.

The reason is, perhaps, that the nail is more visible than we usually notice. We think of the face as the thing people see. We think of the clothes as the thing people remember. The nail, in this hierarchy, is small. But the nail is also the one part of us that almost every gesture displays. You hand the change to a cashier. You hold the menu open. You sign the receipt. You lift the glass to your mouth. Every one of these small, ordinary actions shows the nails. The polish is, in this sense, the only beauty product that is, quite literally, always on display.

This is also why a polish change feels, in a way that is hard to explain, more complete than many larger beauty gestures. The haircut changes the silhouette, but the silhouette is not always visible. The new lipstick changes the face, but only in close conversation. The nail is visible in every handshake, every doorway, every gesture of lifting something. A new color is, in this sense, a small public statement that something about you has changed. The change may not be dramatic. It is, by the end of the afternoon, complete.

## The half-hour at the kitchen table

There is a ritual, passed down from mothers and older sisters and from the practice of certain magazines, of doing one's nails at home. It is, in many households, a Saturday-afternoon thing. It begins with the removal of the old polish, usually with the small bottle of remover and a wad of cotton. It continues with the trimming and the filing and the pushing back of the cuticle, which is, in the hands of an ordinary amateur, more or less the limit of what can be done at home. And then, finally, the painting.

The painting is the part everyone remembers. The first stroke is the one that determines everything. The brush is held at a slight angle. The polish goes on in three careful passes - one down the center, then one on each side - and then you wait. You do not, under any circumstances, do anything with that hand for the next fifteen minutes. You hold it awkwardly at your side. You wave it, gently, to dry the surface. You do not pick up a book. You do not pick up a child. You do not put your hand in a pocket, because the pocket will leave a mark in the wet polish that will follow you around for a week.

This is the strange and slightly ridiculous cost of a polish change. The half-hour of painting is followed by an hour of doing almost nothing, during which you are, in effect, a person without the use of one of her hands. You cannot type. You cannot wash up. You cannot tie your hair back. You are, for an hour, a person with a small painted object at the end of her arm that must, at all costs, not be smudged. The trade is not, in retrospect, unreasonable. The trade is the small price of the smallest complete transformation a person can make in an afternoon.

## What color does

It is worth pausing, for a moment, on the question of what the color itself is doing. The polish is not, in any literal sense, decoration. The nail is too small for the color to be a statement. The color is not seen at a distance. It is not the first thing anyone notices about you, in most rooms. The color is, instead, something closer to a piece of private information. It is the small, deliberate thing you have done for yourself, that other people will glimpse, in fragments, in passing.

The vocabulary of color, for the nails, is enormous and also strangely personal. There is the burgundy that is, in the right light, almost black - the burgundy that says you have decided, today, to be a slightly more serious version of yourself. There is the orange that says the opposite - that today is a day for linen and iced coffee and a book in a café window. There is the pale neutral that is, in many offices, the safest possible color, and that is, for that reason, the most private. There is the green, the gold, the specific blue that only one of your friends would recognize. Each of these is, in its way, a sentence. Each of them carries its own small weather.

The choice of color is, in this sense, less about aesthetics and more about mood. The nails are the part of the body where we are, briefly, allowed to be more deliberate than we are in the rest of the day. We have, by the time we are sitting at the table with the bottle, decided something about the week. The color is the small public evidence of that decision. We may not, by Monday, remember why we chose it. But we will look down at our hands, in the middle of a meeting, and the color will still be there, doing its quiet work.

## The chip, and what it does

Every painted nail, eventually, chips. This is not a failure. This is the natural life of polish, which is, despite the claims of certain bottles, a relatively temporary object. The chip happens at the corner, usually, where the nail meets the world. It happens because the nail is, at that point, in contact with everything - the steering wheel, the doorframe, the keyboard, the edge of a ceramic mug. The chip is, in this sense, not a flaw. It is a record. It is the small visible evidence that the hand has been doing what hands do.

There is, in the way we respond to a chip, a small piece of information about how we relate to our own beauty projects. Some of us, the morning after the chip, file the polish off entirely and begin again. Some of us wear the chipped polish for a week, and then a second, because the chip is, at this point, too small to be worth the half-hour of removal. Some of us, the most honest of all, look at the chip and feel, briefly, a small and slightly ridiculous grief - the grief of a small deliberate thing that did not last as long as we wanted it to.

None of this is, in the larger scheme of things, important. The chip does not change anything fundamental about the day. It does not, in most cases, change the way anyone else looks at the hand. It changes, mainly, the small private relationship between the wearer and the wearing. The chip reminds us, in the most minor of ways, that all beauty projects are temporary, that they are not the body, that the body underneath is the part that is doing the actual living. The chip is, in this sense, a small reminder that the hand is also a hand - that it is, in addition to being a small painted canvas, the thing that picks up the baby and opens the jar and signs the letter.

## The nail and the rest of the person

What is, perhaps, most interesting about a nail is how disproportionate its effect is. The nail is the smallest part of the body that we deliberately decorate. It is, by any measure, less significant than the face, less expressive than the hair, less structurally important than the silhouette. And yet the nail is, in many lives, the part of the beauty project that gets the most attention. The most carefully chosen color. The most regular appointments. The most whispered conversations about which shade, which brand, which finish.

This is not because the nail is beautiful, in any abstract sense. It is because the nail is, in a way that the face and the hair and the clothes are not, entirely ours. The face we share with our expressions. The hair we share with the weather. The clothes we share with the dry cleaner. The nail is the part of the beauty project that does not depend on anything except our own half-hour, with the bottle and the brush, at the kitchen table. It is the only part of being a person that is, in this sense, entirely a private decision.

This is, perhaps, why we keep doing it. Not because the paint is beautiful. Not because the manicure is impressive. Because the half-hour at the kitchen table, with the small bottle and the careful brush and the awkward hour of not using the hand, is one of the few purely private beauty rituals we have left. The face, the hair, the body - these are mostly done for others, or in the company of others, or in spaces we share. The nail is, often, done alone. The nail is the part of beauty that is, in some quiet way, only for the person doing it. We paint the nails. We are, for an hour, the only one who knows what we have chosen. The color is the small secret we carry at the end of our hands, until the chip finally tells the world.
