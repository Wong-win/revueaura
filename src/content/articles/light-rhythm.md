The first thing a room tells you is not its color or its size. It is the hour. Walk into a room in the early morning, and the room is one thing. Walk into it at three in the afternoon, and it is another. Walk into it again at dusk, and a third. The walls do not change. The furniture does not move. What has changed is the angle of the planet relative to the sun, and the room's response to that slow, indifferent rotation. Light is the first and last thing a room knows, and most of what we call the mood of a room is just light, mooded differently.

I think about this often when I travel. Hotel rooms are interesting precisely because they are so forgettable — generic walls, generic lamps, generic curtains — and yet each one feels completely different from every other, because the light that passes through each window belongs only to that window, that street, that orientation, that climate. A room facing east in Lisbon in March is a profoundly different animal from a room facing west in Reykjavík in the same month. The room itself is interchangeable. The light is not.

This is the trouble with designing a room from a photograph. Photographs are stolen moments. They show a room at one instant, in one slant, with one color temperature. They do not show you the room at seven in the morning in February, when the sun does not rise until nearly eight and then arrives at a low, exhausted angle that makes everything look like a watercolor. They do not show you the room at noon in July, when the light is so direct and so white that you retreat into the interior of the apartment and live there like a cave-dweller. They do not show you the room at four in November, when the lamps come on and the room becomes its own small country, governed by its own small sun.

## The Composer of Days

If a room is a kind of weather system, light is its composer. It decides when the day begins. It decides when the day ends. It decides where, in the room, you can sit comfortably without squinting, and where you must stand up and close a curtain. It decides whether the room is a morning room or an evening room, a winter room or a summer room, a working room or a resting room. Most of these decisions are made before you ever open a paint can or hang a picture.

This is why the question of where to put the sofa is, in the long run, less important than the question of where to put the window. Windows are the original light fixtures, and they were installed long before electricity was invented. Everything we do indoors is, in some sense, an answer to the question of what to do when the sun goes away. We invented lamps so that we could extend the day. We invented curtains so that we could shorten it. We invented mirrors so that we could borrow light from a room that was not our own.

But natural light — the kind that arrives unbidden, that you can neither turn on nor turn off, that varies by season and by latitude and by the particular cloud cover of an individual Tuesday — natural light is the composer of the day. Everything else is accompaniment.

I have come to believe that one of the most generous things you can do for yourself, when you are choosing a home, is to choose one that has at least one window you do not need to look through. Not a window for the view — though those are wonderful. A window for the light. A window whose only job is to let the afternoon in. A window you walk past at a particular hour and find yourself stopping. A window that turns a wall into a calendar.

## Seasonal Light, Seasonal Self

There is a kind of grief that arrives in northern cities around the third week of January, when the light has been thin and gray for so long that you have begun to forget what direct sunlight feels like. There is a corresponding joy that arrives in late March, when the days lengthen so suddenly that you find yourself standing at a window at six p.m., astonished to discover that it is still light, that the day has been extended without your noticing. These are not poetic observations. They are physiological ones. The body responds to light the way a plant does. We are slower to admit it, but we are not different in kind.

This is why the same apartment feels like a refuge in January and a prison in July. It is why the same window that seems dreamily generous in October feels oppressively bright in May. It is why the north-facing rooms of one hemisphere are the south-facing rooms of another, and why the design advice that travels well across the internet is often completely wrong about which walls should be painted which colors. Light, in any given room, is local. It is tied to latitude and orientation and the particular obstructions — trees, neighboring buildings, hills — that surround the window. There is no universal rule about light. There is only the light that arrives at your window on the day you happen to be standing there.

I have a friend who moved from Sydney to Edinburgh and was astonished to find that her south-facing windows, which had been unthinkably bright in the Australian summer, were now merely pleasant in the Scottish version. She had to learn, all over again, what her apartment was for. The light had changed, and with it the entire emotional geography of her home. She had not moved so much as her city had moved around her, dragging her rooms with it.

## Living With the Day

What I have come to love, more than any particular lamp or fixture, is the practice of letting the day into the room and following it as it moves. There is something almost devotional about it. You wake, and the room is dark, and then there is a moment — different every morning — when the first gray light arrives at the window, and you know that the day has begun even if you have not yet opened your eyes. You get up, and the light has moved an inch. You make tea, and the light is in the kitchen now, falling on the counter where you are working. You sit down with your book, and the light has crossed the room and is warming the wall opposite the window. By afternoon, it is on the floor. By evening, it has gone.

If you pay attention to this, you begin to feel that the room is alive in a way that has nothing to do with you. The room is going about its business. You are merely a tenant inside it, allowed to observe. And the more you observe, the more you begin to time your own activities to the movement of the light — not because anyone has told you to, but because the light makes certain activities feel right and others feel wrong. You do not want to read at noon, when the glare is on the page. You do not want to eat dinner at three in the afternoon, when the light is high and thin and the room has no patience for candles. You want the right hour for the right thing, and the light tells you what the right hour is.

This is a small, ancient pleasure. It is the pleasure of being a creature in a place that has seasons. It does not require any particular design choices. It does not require expensive windows or curated views. It requires only that you let the room do what it is already doing, and that you pay attention to it.

The best rooms I have ever lived in were not the ones with the most beautiful light. They were the ones where I had learned what the light was doing, and where I had arranged my days so as not to fight it. The kitchen was placed near the morning window, because morning is when you want to be in the kitchen. The reading chair was placed near the late-afternoon window, because late afternoon is when you want to read. The dining table was placed where the light would fall on the plates at dinner, because dinner deserves that.

None of this is a rule. None of it appears in a magazine. None of it will impress a visitor. But it is, I think, the truest version of what it means to live with light — to let the light set the rhythm, and to follow.
