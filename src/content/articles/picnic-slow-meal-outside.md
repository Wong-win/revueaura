There is a particular meal, somewhere in the long middle of the year, when the weather has finally agreed to be weather, that asks nothing of you except that you sit on the ground and eat. The meal is a picnic. The meal is, in fact, the only meal we know of where the plate is the grass, the tablecloth is whatever cloth you remembered to bring, the lighting is the sun, the music is the birds arguing about territory, and nobody, least of all you, is allowed to check the time. The picnic is the slowest lunch of the year. The picnic is the meal that has, by its very nature, no agenda. And this, we think, is the entire point.

We mention the picnic here, at some length, because we think it has been miscategorised. The picnic has been filed, in the modern imagination, under the heading of leisure, and bundled, by the lifestyle industry, with the inflatable kayak, the polaroid camera, and the checked blanket that appears every summer in three hundred Instagram posts and one magazine spread. The picnic has been sold as an aesthetic, which is a great pity, because a picnic, properly understood, is not an aesthetic at all. A picnic is a practice. A picnic is one of the oldest and most useful practices we have, for slowing down a day, for making a meal into an event, and for reminding the body, which has spent the morning indoors, that the body is, in fact, still allowed to sit on the ground.

What we want to argue, gently, is that the picnic is worth doing at least once a month between May and October, and that it does not require anything the magazines would suggest. It does not require a basket, although a basket is nice. It does not require a checked blanket. It requires, in its simplest form, three things: a piece of ground, a piece of bread, and a piece of time. None of these need be fancy. All three are, in their quiet way, free.

## the ground, and how to choose it

The first thing about a picnic, and the thing the magazines will not tell you because it is not photogenic, is that the ground matters more than the food. You can have a perfectly good picnic with bad bread. You cannot have a good picnic with bad ground. The ground is the table. The ground is the dining room. The ground is, for the duration of the meal, the architecture, and the architecture has to be right.

What we mean by right is several things, and none of them have to do with views. The ground should be flat, or close to flat, because you do not want the bottle of cold drink to spend the meal rolling toward your neighbour. The ground should be soft enough to sit on for an hour without your back complaining, which is to say grass, or sand, or a meadow, rather than a paved terrace. The ground should have, ideally, a single tree nearby, for shade, and the tree should be the kind whose roots have not created an obstacle course for the person who has to sit on the ground for an hour. The picnic, in our experience, is best held in the part of the park that no other adult seems to have remembered exists.

We are not, in this column, going to argue for the magazine picnic ground — the clifftop, the wildflower meadow in the south of France, the lakeside with a single sailboat in the middle distance. We are going to argue for the small, ordinary corner of your local park, where the grass is slightly longer than elsewhere, and the only sound is the wind in the oak tree. This is the dining room. This is where, on a Wednesday in June, or a Sunday in September, you are going to put down a piece of cloth, and unpack a loaf of bread, and eat slowly, for an hour, in the company of one or two people.

## the bread, and how to pack it

The second thing about a picnic, and this is the part the lifestyle industry has gotten mostly correct, is that the food should be simple. A picnic is not the meal on which to bring out the lobster. A picnic is not the meal on which to demonstrate, to your guests or to yourself, the kind of culinary ambition that requires, in advance, a list, a plan, a trip to the fishmonger, and a small crisis of confidence in the kitchen. A picnic is the meal that rewards, instead, the simple things. The loaf of bread, the cheese, the apple, the bunch of grapes, the small pot of olives, the packet of crackers. The picnic is, in its essence, an antipasto set on the grass, and the antipasto has been doing its work, on the grass, since long before anyone thought to photograph it.

The bread is the centre of the picnic. The bread should be a real bread — not a sliced loaf in a plastic bag, but a loaf from a baker you trust, baked that morning, with a crust that crackles when you cut it. The bread should be cut, if there is a knife, into slices thick enough to hold a piece of cheese without breaking. The bread should be eaten, ideally, in pieces torn off by hand, in the slightly uncivilised way of all meals eaten on the ground.

What goes with the bread is up to you. We would, gently, suggest that you resist the temptation to bring too much. A picnic is not a buffet. A picnic is a meal that has been deliberately cut down to its essentials, edited, in advance, by the understanding that you will, after an hour on the ground, want to carry everything home again in whatever bag you brought. Bring the bread. Bring the cheese. Bring one apple per person, or one bunch of grapes. Bring a small bottle of something to drink. Bring, if you have remembered, a small knife. The picnic, like the bag, is more generous when it has been edited down.

## the slowness, and how to allow it

The third thing about a picnic, and this is the part the magazines do not sell you, because there is nothing in it for them, is that a picnic takes time. A picnic takes, in our experience, an absolute minimum of ninety minutes, and ideally closer to two hours, and any attempt to do a picnic in less than an hour is, in our experience, not a picnic but a slightly embarrassing sandwich eaten on a bench.

The reason a picnic takes time is that the picnic is not really about the food. The picnic is about the slowness. The picnic is the meal during which, by design, nobody has anywhere to be. You are on the ground. The ground is not going anywhere. The bread is not going anywhere. The cheese, ideally, is not going anywhere, although in warm weather it is advisable to eat the cheese before the cheese has done what cheese does in warm weather. The two hours, in any case, are not a constraint. The two hours are the gift. The two hours are the time during which you are allowed, by the simple fact of sitting on the ground in a public park, to do almost nothing. You are allowed to talk to the person beside you. You are allowed to lie back and look at the clouds. You are allowed to read, slowly, the back of a paperback. You are allowed to fall asleep, briefly, on the cloth, in the sun, in the way that only people who are very young or very tired or very wise ever actually do.

This slowness, we should say, is not the same as boredom. The slowness is what boredom used to be, before we replaced it with the phone. The slowness is the small, mild, slightly uncomfortable feeling of having nothing to do, and then, after about ten minutes, the realisation that nothing, in this case, is plenty. Nothing, on the picnic cloth, is the entire menu. Nothing is the appetiser, the main course, the dessert, and the digestif. Nothing is the thing you came out to have, although you would not have said so, in advance, if anyone had asked.

## the company, and how to keep it small

We have a strong opinion, which we will share gently, about the company at a picnic. The picnic is, in our experience, best enjoyed by no more than four people, and ideally by two. Picnics, with larger groups, become a logistical exercise, and the logistical exercise is the enemy of the slowness, and the slowness is the point. A picnic with eight people is, in our experience, mostly a picnic about organising eight people, which mostly undoes the picnic.

Two people, by contrast, is a picnic. Two people can decide, without a vote, what to bring. Two people can decide, without a vote, where to put the cloth. Two people can be quiet together, in the way that two people who have known each other for long enough know how to be quiet together. The bread is for two. The cheese is for two. The afternoon, which is the entire afternoon, is for two.

There is, of course, a version of the picnic that is for one. The picnic for one is the original picnic, in some sense, before the picnic became a social occasion. The picnic for one is a small, slightly eccentric ritual that we would gently encourage. You, alone, on a cloth, with a book, with a piece of bread, on a Wednesday in May, in a corner of the park that no one else seems to have remembered. The picnic for one does not have to perform for anyone. The picnic for one is just a person, sitting on the ground, eating bread, looking at the sky — the meal at which, finally, you are allowed to be the only person in your life, which, in a busy week, can feel like a small luxury.

## the walk home

The walk home, after a picnic, is a particular walk. The walk home is slower than the walk there. The walk home is slower because you are slightly heavier — the bread, the cheese, the apple cores, the empty bottle, all of which have to be carried back, because you are, in this small way, a responsible citizen of the park. The walk home is slower because the body has spent ninety minutes on the ground, and the body, when you stand up, is briefly unsure of its relationship to gravity. The walk home is slower because you are thinking about the picnic, and the picnic, somehow, takes the rest of the day with it.

The walk home, also, smells faintly of grass. The walk home has, on the knee of the trousers, a small green stripe, where the cloth was folded. The walk home has, in the hair, a small piece of something that might be a twig, or might be a strand of the cloth, or might be nothing, but that you will discover, when you wash your hair that evening, and that will remind you, briefly, of the picnic. The walk home, in short, carries the picnic, in the body, the way the picnic carried you, on the cloth, for an hour and a half. The picnic has, in this small way, entered you.

This is, in the end, what the picnic is for. Not for the photograph. Not for the aesthetic. The picnic is for the small, slow, slightly out-of-time experience of eating bread outside, on a Wednesday, with no plan. The picnic is for the rare meal at which, finally, you are allowed to do almost nothing, and the almost nothing, after ninety minutes, turns out to be everything. The picnic is the slowest lunch of the year, and the most generous. It costs almost nothing. It returns almost everything.

Bring a cloth. Bring a loaf. Find a corner of the park that no one else has remembered. Sit down. Eat slowly. Stay longer than you meant to. The picnic will do the rest. It has been doing it, very patiently, for several hundred years. It will do it again this summer, and the summer after that, provided someone keeps putting down the cloth.
