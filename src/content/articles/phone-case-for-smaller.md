We remember, with the kind of clarity reserved for small obsessions, the first morning we slipped a small phone into the pocket of a coat and walked out into the city without thinking about it. The phone was not, by any modern measurement, a particularly good phone. The camera was modest. The battery ran out by mid-afternoon if we used it for directions. The screen was the kind of screen one could read in a cafe without the person across the aisle glancing over to see what we were reading. None of these limitations, we discovered over the following months, were the problem we had been warned they would be. None of them were, in fact, problems at all. What the small phone gave us, more than any feature, was the experience of not carrying a phone — of having, on our person, a small rectangle of metal and glass that did not, in any obvious way, announce itself.

The phone we had before the small phone was not, technically, much larger. It was six-tenths of an inch larger, on the diagonal, and a third of an ounce heavier, and yet those few millimeters made, over the months we carried it, an enormous difference to the texture of the day. The larger phone did not fit comfortably into the small inside pocket of the coat we liked to wear in autumn. It did not sit, in the palm of the hand, without the faint sense that it was about to fall out. It could not be operated, with any reliability, while holding a coffee cup in the other hand. We are, we want to be clear about this, not engineers. We are not, in any meaningful sense, technology critics. We are simply two people who, over the course of a couple of years, had become aware that the phone we carried had begun to dictate a small but persistent set of movements — the two-handed pickup from the table, the careful slide into the back pocket of a pair of trousers that had not been designed for this, the small tilt of the head to see the top of the screen without scrolling. These movements, individually, were nothing. Aggregated across a day, they were a kind of low-level tax.

## the case for the pocket

The case for a smaller phone, in the discourse of the last decade, has mostly been made on aesthetic grounds. The small phone is, in some obvious visual sense, prettier. It looks more like a small object one might find in a leather shop than like a slab of glass that one might find in an electronics store. There is, in the small phone, a certain return to the proportions of older objects — the pocket watch, the cigarette case, the small notebook — that had, for the better part of a century, lived in the small inside pockets of coats and jackets without anyone particularly complaining about them. The small phone, in this sense, fits into a tradition of small carried objects, and the larger phone, by contrast, has broken with that tradition in a way that some people — including us — have found, on quiet reflection, a little melancholy.

But the case we have come to think is more interesting is not the aesthetic case. It is the case made on the grounds of the rest of the hand. The rest of the hand is, in our experience, the most underrated piece of personal technology of the last century. The rest of the hand is what holds the coffee cup. The rest of the hand is what opens the door. The rest of the hand is what reaches, automatically, into the pocket of the coat to retrieve the keys, the wallet, the small paper receipt that one will, for reasons one cannot quite explain, not throw away. The rest of the hand is, in the modern phone era, the most underutilized piece of personal technology we have. We have, over the years, slowly degraded it. We have, over the years, given over more and more of its function to the small rectangle we now hold in it.

The smaller phone returns some of that. With the smaller phone in the hand, we can hold the coffee cup in the same hand, on the subway, in a way that we have not been able to do for years. We can hold the leash of the dog, the strap of the bag, the handle of the child's bicycle seat, and still take a photograph if we need to. We can hold the small umbrella, in the rain, and the small phone, in the same hand, and the rest of the body — the legs, the lungs, the part of the brain that is paying attention to the puddle — can do its work. This is, on the surface, a small thing. It is, in the experience of a typical day, a significant thing. The hand that does not have to choose between the phone and the coffee cup is the hand that is, quietly, more available to the rest of the body.

## the myth of the small compromise

We have lost count of the number of times, in conversations about phones, that someone has gently suggested that the smaller phone is a compromise. The smaller phone, in this version, is the phone one chooses when one cannot afford the better one. The smaller phone is the phone one settles for when one has not yet been able to get the larger one. The smaller phone is the phone of the budget, the modest, the not-yet-arrived. This is, we have come to think, mostly a fiction — and a strange one, given that the smallest phones in our shared memory were, in fact, not the cheapest. The original iPhone, in 2007, was not small because it was cheap. The small Android phones of the early 2010s were not small because they were second-rate. They were small because, in the judgment of their designers, small was, in 2010, the right size for a phone.

What changed, of course, was the screen. The screen, over the course of a decade, became the most expensive component of the phone, and the marketing of the phone became, in turn, a marketing of the screen. The bigger screen was sold to us as the better screen. The bigger screen was the screen on which movies looked better, on which games looked better, on which the photographs looked more like the photographs one wanted to take. The bigger screen was the screen one ought to want. The bigger screen, over time, stopped being an option and became the default, and the small phone, slowly, became the strange one.

This is, we want to be careful to say, not a conspiracy. The people who designed the bigger phones were, by and large, designing what they thought their customers wanted. The people who bought the bigger phones were, by and large, buying what they had been told to want. The feedback loop, as is often the case in the technology business, was not a conspiracy but a kind of slow mutual agreement that, in retrospect, looks a little grim. We all wanted the bigger screen. We all bought the bigger screen. The small phone, in the meantime, became rarer — and the rarer it became, the more it cost to make one in a market that had, over the years, been optimized for the large.

## a small object in a large world

What we have come to think, in the months since we switched back to the small phone, is that the small phone is not, in fact, a compromise at all. It is, in its own quiet way, a position. It is the position that the phone, in the life one is trying to live, is a small object. It is the position that the screen is not the center of the day. It is the position that the rest of the hand is entitled to do its work. It is the position that the coffee cup, the door handle, the leash, the umbrella, the small paper receipt one cannot quite bring oneself to throw away — that all of these are, in the small economy of the body, also worth holding. None of these positions are, in any obvious sense, revolutionary. None of them are going to make the news. They are, however, the kinds of positions that one can hold, quietly, for a long time, without anyone particularly noticing — which is, we have come to think, the best kind of position to hold.

We are aware, as we say this, that the small phone market is, in 2026, not what it was. The number of manufacturers making truly small phones has shrunk considerably. The number of small phones available on the carriers has, in some markets, gone down to one or two. The cost of the small phone, in many cases, has gone up, partly because the small phone is now a specialty item, partly because the small phone, in some markets, no longer has the volume discounts it once had. We are aware, also, that the small phone is not, in fact, for everyone. Some of our friends have larger hands than we do. Some of our friends have eyes that genuinely need the larger screen. Some of our friends have jobs that, in fact, require the larger phone for reasons that are real and not just snobbery. We are not, in this article, going to argue that everyone should give up the larger phone. We are, at most, going to argue that the small phone is a reasonable position — that the small phone is, in fact, an honorable answer to the question of what one wants a phone to be.

## the phone as a small piece of luggage

What the small phone has, in our experience, recovered is something we had not realized we had lost — which is the sense of the phone as a small piece of luggage. The phone is, in the older phrase, something we carry. The phone, in this sense, is closer to a wallet than it is to a television. The wallet we carry every day without giving it much thought. The wallet, on a given morning, is just there. The wallet is not a piece of equipment one actively engages with, twenty or thirty times an hour, in the anxious way one engages with the larger phone. The wallet is, in the smaller sense, part of the body's load — a thing one carries, in the pocket, and which one, occasionally, when one needs to, takes out.

The smaller phone returns this. The smaller phone, in the pocket of the coat, on a given morning, is just there. The smaller phone is not, on most mornings, the foreground of the day. The smaller phone is, in fact, more or less the same kind of object as the keys, the wallet, the small piece of paper one cannot throw away. The smaller phone is one of the things one carries, in the pocket, in the body, in the morning. The smaller phone is, in this older sense, an object that has, in some small way, gone back to being a thing one carries rather than a thing one is, in some larger way, carried by.

We had not, before the smaller phone, realized how much of our day had been quietly reorganized around the larger phone. We had not realized, until the smaller phone took its place in the pocket, how many of our movements — the careful two-handed pickup from the table, the slow slide back into the pocket, the small tilt of the head — had been, over time, edited out of the rest of the day. The smaller phone has, in a way that we had not expected, restored those movements. It has restored the rest of the hand. It has restored the part of the day that the larger phone, slowly and politely, had taken over. The day is, on the whole, the better for it. The hand is, on the whole, the better for it. The coffee cup, which we can now hold, on the subway, in the same hand as the phone, has not yet learned to appreciate the change. We have.
