If you have lived in a city for more than a year, there is probably a green rectangle somewhere near your home that you have walked past two hundred times without quite noticing. The entrance might be a wrought-iron gate between two brick piers, or simply a break in the fence where the pavement gives way to gravel. The name, if there is one, is something civic and forgettable — Memorial Park, Maple Common, the Horticultural Grounds, the Reseda. Whatever the name, this is one of the most generous pieces of infrastructure in your life, and we want to suggest, gently, that you might consider making it yours.

A public garden is, in essence, a free subscription you forgot you signed up for. Someone, decades ago, decided that this particular parcel of land ought not to be sold to a developer. Someone else drew up a planting plan. A gardener, whose name you will never know, has been turning up each morning to tend it. The beds are weeded, the paths are raked, the roses are pruned at the right moment of the year. You are receiving the dividend of all this labour without doing anything more strenuous than crossing the threshold. The price of admission is the willingness to be there.

This is what we mean by sustainability. Not the word the magazines have run into the ground, with its photographs of glass jars and folded linen. We mean the quieter thing, the thing a garden actually does for you across a whole year. It sustains a relationship with the seasons. It sustains a posture, which is the slow posture of looking. It sustains a counter-rhythm to the phone, the inbox, the screen, the apartment that is the same temperature every hour of every day. And it sustains this relationship regardless of your income, your politics, your marital status, or how many unread emails you have. The gate is unlocked. The gate is always unlocked.

## a garden you walk through every month

Here is the practice we want to propose. Once a month, on a weekend morning or a slow afternoon, walk through your local garden for thirty minutes. That is the entire commitment. Bring nothing. You might bring a thermos; you might bring a notebook; you might bring a small child in wellington boots. None of these are necessary. What is necessary is the cadence — once a month, twelve times a year, in every sort of weather — that turns a piece of municipal land into something you privately know.

By the third visit, you will notice that the magnolia near the rose garden is out. By the fifth, you will know which bed gets the worst drainage and therefore blooms latest. By the seventh, you will have begun to recognise the man who walks his beagle there at the same hour, the way you would recognise a commuter on your train. By the ninth, you will be able to say with some confidence, in February, that the crocuses will be open by the twelfth of March. By the twelfth visit, the garden will have stopped being a place and started being a season. You will, without noticing, have become the kind of person who knows things.

This is a slow accumulation. None of these individual visits is going to feel like a revelation. You will not have an insight you can post about. You will simply walk among the plantings, and then you will leave, and then a month will pass, and then you will come back, and the garden will have done the gentle, persistent work of keeping the year moving. The frosts come, the frosts go. The roses blacken, the roses return. You were there for each of these, briefly, and the witness of being there is what we mean by an interior life.

We should say a word about the weather, because we are writing this in the part of the world where weather is a factor. You do not, of course, have to walk through your garden in a storm. But we would gently encourage you not to wait for a perfect afternoon in late May. The garden in November, when the last leaves are coming down in slow streamers, is just as much the garden as the garden in June. The benches are slightly damp. The paths are a little slippery. The roses are skeletal but their hips are bright and heavy. To know only the green months is to know half the garden, and to rob yourself of the season in which the gardener, who is the true protagonist of the place, becomes most visible.

## the gardener as invisible companion

There is a person — sometimes a team — who is responsible for the fact that your garden is the way it is. They are usually up before you. They are usually working in clothing that the magazines would call practical. They have opinions about mulch. They have favourites among the beds. They will almost certainly never introduce themselves to you, and you will almost certainly never introduce yourself to them, because gardens are one of the last places where strangers can work alongside each other without speaking, and that quiet social contract is part of what makes the place restorative.

Once you begin to notice the gardener, you begin to notice what the garden actually is, which is a kind of long essay in choices. Someone decided that this border should be a tapestry of perennials rather than a formal lawn. Someone decided that the cherry tree should be planted where it is. Someone decided, this spring, that the lavenders had finished and should be cut back hard, and the salvias should be let go another fortnight. None of these decisions is visible in a single visit. They become visible across months, the way brushstrokes become visible only when you stand back from the canvas. This is part of what makes the monthly walk a richer instrument than the single heroic visit.

We think of it, sometimes, as a kind of slow correspondence. You walk through. The gardener has done something to the place since last time. Next time, you walk through again, and they have done something else. You do not write to each other. You do not need to. The garden itself is the letter, and you are reading it.

## what you take with you

The most surprising thing, when we have done this for two or three years, is how much of the garden comes away with us. Not in any literal sense. We cannot take cuttings from a public planting without permission, and even if we could, that is not the point. What we take is a kind of internal calendar. We know now, without thinking about it, what week the irises bloom, what week the apples fall, what week the first frost usually arrives in our particular patch of the city. We know how a magnolia opens from tight grey bud to fat white flower over the course of a fortnight. We know that there is a particular bench near the pond where, around five in the afternoon in late August, the sun comes through the plane trees at a particular angle and turns the grass the colour of pale honey.

None of this is on the sign at the gate. None of this is in the brochures. It is the unpriced dividend of doing the same walk forty times, in different weathers, with different attentions, at different ages of your life. The garden does not advertise it. The garden simply waits for you to come back.

This is, we think, what the older European cities understood when they laid out their public planting. A city is not only its shops and offices and apartments. A city is also the green thing in the middle of it that you can go to for free, and that asks nothing of you but the willingness to come back. It is an inheritance from the people who lived before us, and a small gift to the people who will live after. The only thing it requires is that someone, somewhere, does the slow work of walking through it once a month, and notices.

You do not have to make your garden your whole life. You do not have to learn its Latin names or photograph its borders. You do not even have to sit on the bench, though the bench is there for a reason. You only have to keep showing up. The garden will do the rest. It has been doing it, very patiently, for a hundred years. It will do it again this spring, and the spring after that, and the spring after that, provided someone keeps the gate unlatched.
