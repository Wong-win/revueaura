We have, in our small collection of objects, a device that we have come to think of as the most underestimated piece of technology of the last twenty years. It is not particularly fast. It does not, in any meaningful sense, multitask. It does not, despite years of patient lobbying by the marketing departments of various companies, do anything except the one thing it was designed to do. It holds books. It shows them, in a font of one's choosing, on a screen that is, in decent light, almost indistinguishable from a piece of paper. It does not buzz. It does not, between paragraphs, suggest one might prefer a different book. It does not, in any case, know what one might prefer. It is, in short, an e-reader, and we have come to think of it as one of the great quiet achievements of the century.

The case for the e-reader, in the discourse of the last two decades, has mostly been made on practical grounds. It is light. It holds, in something the size of a paperback, a small personal library. It costs less, over the long run, than the equivalent in print. These are real arguments, and we have made use of all of them. But the case we have come to think is more interesting — and, in the end, more persuasive — is the one that is rarely made. The e-reader, we have come to think, is the device that has quietly returned to us the lost experience of deep reading.

The phrase *deep reading*, like most phrases of its kind, has been somewhat overused. There is a small academic literature on it, descended from the work of the psychologist Maryanne Wolf, that argues that the human brain, when it reads on screens, processes text differently than it does on paper — more shallowly, more distractedly, more in the mode of skimming than of absorbing. Whether or not one accepts the strong form of this argument, we have all experienced the milder version. One reads a long article on a phone and, an hour later, remembers almost nothing of it. One reads the same article on paper and, an hour later, can quote a paragraph. Something, somewhere, is different.

## a screen that does not interrupt

What we have come to think, after several years of using an e-reader for most of our non-work reading, is that the case for the device is not primarily about the screen technology. The e-ink screen is, in 2026, very good. It is, in many lighting conditions, indistinguishable from paper. It does not glow in the dark in the slightly aggressive way that a phone does. It does not, between chapters, suggest that one might prefer to read a review of the book instead of the book itself. These are real virtues, and they are not nothing. But the deeper virtue, we have come to think, is the absence of everything else.

The phone, in 2026, is an extraordinary piece of technology. It is also, by deliberate design, an interruption machine. Every notification, every badge, every small red dot is, in some sense, a small bid for one's attention. The phone is engineered to be looked at. The phone is engineered to be picked up. The phone is, in the literal sense, a thing one picks up, on average, eighty times a day. This is, on the whole, not what one wants when one is trying to read a novel. It is, in fact, almost exactly what one does not want when one is trying to read a novel.

The e-reader, by contrast, does almost nothing else. It does not, in the version we use, have a web browser in any meaningful sense. It does not, except for the briefest of moments when one transfers a new book onto it, know what the internet is. It does not, between pages, show one a notification. It does not, between chapters, suggest a different book. It does not, after a particularly moving passage, ask one to rate the passage on a five-star scale. It does not, after a less moving passage, suggest one might like to skip ahead. It is, in this sense, an almost aggressively single-purpose device. It is a device that does one thing, and it is, on the whole, a great pleasure to use precisely because it does one thing.

## the disappearance of the device

What is interesting, in our experience, is how quickly the e-reader disappears once one starts using it. We have, over the years, used many devices that have done this in their way — the paperback book, the good notebook, the simple alarm clock — and they all share a certain quality. They become, after a few weeks of regular use, almost invisible. They are not, in any meaningful sense, the focus of one's attention. They are the medium through which one is attending to something else. The e-reader, in our experience, has this quality to a degree that we had not expected.

The book one is reading, on an e-reader, is more present than the e-reader itself. The e-reader, in this sense, is a kind of good transparent medium. It does its work and gets out of the way. The phone, by contrast, is the opposite of a transparent medium. The phone is, almost by design, the foreground. The book one is reading on the phone is, in many cases, almost an afterthought — a small piece of content that one is consuming between other uses of the phone. The phone wants one's attention in a way that the e-reader does not. The phone, in this sense, is the bad reader's medium. The e-reader is the good one's.

There is, we have come to think, a related pleasure in the e-reader that is harder to articulate. It is the pleasure of using an object that does not, in any obvious sense, belong to the broader attention economy. The phone is, in 2026, a piece of infrastructure in the attention economy. Every tap, every scroll, every second of looking at the screen is being measured, weighed, monetized. The e-reader is, on the whole, not. The e-reader does not, in any meaningful sense, know who one is. It does not, in any obvious sense, care what one reads. It is, in this older sense, almost a piece of analog equipment that happens to use electricity. We have found, in our own use, that this is, by 2026, almost a revolutionary feature. The object that does not know one is, in a world saturated with objects that know one, an extraordinary small luxury.

## reading as a slow domestic act

What the e-reader has, in our own case, restored is not so much deep reading as the conditions for deep reading. We read, in our twenties, in the way that everyone reads in their twenties — in transit, in cafes, in the fifteen minutes before bed. We read on the subway with the noise of the subway in our ears. We read in cafes with the noise of the cafe in our ears. We read in bed with the phone in our hand and the half-conscious sense that one was, at any moment, going to be interrupted by something. The reading was, in many cases, surprisingly deep — but it was also, in many cases, surprisingly exhausting. One was reading against the grain of one's environment. One was, in a small way, performing a feat of attention.

What the e-reader has restored, in our thirties and forties, is the older, more domestic, more leisurely sense of reading as something one does at home. We read, now, on the couch, in the armchair, in bed, in the bath, on a quiet morning at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee. The e-reader is light enough to hold with one hand. It is small enough to leave, when one is done, on the side table for three days at a time without losing one's place. It does not, when one picks it up, take thirty seconds to remember what one was reading. It is, in this sense, the perfect domestic reading device. It is the device for the kind of reading one does in the kind of rooms one has, when one is in their thirties and forties and not, in any reasonable sense, going anywhere.

We have come to think, in fact, that the e-reader has, in some quiet way, restored the older relationship between reading and the rooms in which one reads. The book on the bedside table is, in our memory, more vivid than the book on the phone. The book in the armchair is more vivid than the book on the subway. The book one has been reading for a month is more vivid than the book one has been reading for a week. The e-reader, by making it easier to keep a book on the bedside table for a month, has made it possible, again, to have a book one is reading. There is, we have come to think, a small but real difference between reading a book and reading books. The e-reader has, in our case, restored the former.

We are not, in the end, going to argue that the e-reader is the best device ever made. It is not. It is a small, slightly slow, deliberately single-purpose piece of technology that does one thing well. It is, in 2026, the closest thing we have to the older, more analog experience of reading a book — that is, the experience of being absorbed, for a long time, in a piece of text, in a particular room, without the world outside the room demanding anything of one. There are, of course, no apps that do this. There are no notifications that do this. There are no algorithms that do this. There is only the e-reader, and the long quiet afternoon, and the book one has been reading for a month. We have found, in our own case, that this is, by 2026, almost a radical idea. We have also found, more to the point, that it is a good one.
