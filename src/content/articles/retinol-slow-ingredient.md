The bottle is the smallest on the bathroom shelf. It is smaller than the cleanser, smaller than the serum, smaller than the day cream. It is the kind of bottle that you have to pick up to read the label, because the label is printed in the kind of small type that assumes you have already decided what the bottle is, and that only the rest of us are still squinting at. The dropper is also small. The amount you are supposed to use is, by any measure, almost nothing - the size of a single pearl, applied at night, in a thin line across the forehead and down the cheeks, in the half-dark of the bathroom, after the rest of the routine has been put away. The retinol is, in this sense, the smallest gesture of the night. It is also the gesture that asks the most patience of the person making it.

There is a particular relationship a person has with her retinol. It is not, in the way the other products are, a relationship of immediate reward. The cleanser produces, within seconds, the small feeling of having begun. The serum produces, by morning, a small visible improvement. The day cream produces, within minutes, the small feeling of being held together. The retinol produces, in most cases, nothing. The retinol produces, in some cases, for the first three months, slightly less than nothing. The skin, briefly, looks worse. The skin, briefly, peels. The skin, briefly, behaves in ways the rest of the routine has not prepared it for. The retinol is, in this sense, the only ingredient in the bathroom that asks the user to trust it through a period in which the trust is, visibly, not being repaid.

## The first three months

There is, in most retinol stories, a first three months. The three months begin, almost always, on a Sunday night in autumn, after a long conversation with a friend whose skin has, in some reliable way, been getting better for a year. The three months begin with the small bottle being opened, and the small pearl being dispensed, and the small pearl being applied, with a certain amount of ceremony, to a face that has not yet decided whether to be grateful for the attention.

The three months are not, in most cases, pleasant. The retinol is, by design, a slightly uncomfortable ingredient. It is, in most formulations, the kind of ingredient that the skin has to learn to accommodate. The first weeks are, almost always, the easiest - the skin is, briefly, too busy being flattered by the attention to complain. The second month is harder. The skin begins, in some small way, to push back. The third month is hardest of all. The skin, briefly, behaves in ways it has not behaved since adolescence. The face, by the middle of month three, is the face that has been doing the work.

This is also the moment when most people give up. The bottle is, in most lives, abandoned somewhere around week ten. The bottle is put back on the shelf, in the back, behind the other bottles, where the light does not reach. The retinol has, in this sense, lost its small private war against the patience of the person who bought it. The skin returns, eventually, to the version of itself that existed before the three months began. The face, in this sense, has been, briefly, asked to change, and has, briefly, declined.

## The quiet year

There is, in every retinol story, a quiet year. The quiet year is, almost always, the year after the first three months. It is the year when the bottle is, finally, on the shelf, opened, and used, every other night, without the small private drama of the first three months. The bottle is, by the quiet year, just a bottle. The dropper is, by the quiet year, just a dropper. The pearl is, by the quiet year, just a pearl. The skin, by the quiet year, has stopped protesting. The skin has, by the quiet year, accepted the small nightly visitor.

The quiet year is also, in most retinol stories, the year when the changes begin. The skin is, by the middle of the quiet year, slightly different. The pores are, in some small way, less visible. The texture is, in some small way, more even. The lines around the eyes, which were, in the first three months, more visible than they had been, are now, in the quiet year, less visible than they were before the bottle was opened. The retinol is, in this sense, doing the work it was always going to do. The work is, by the quiet year, simply happening.

This is also why the quiet year is the year most people miss. The changes are not, in the quiet year, dramatic. The changes are, in the quiet year, the kind of changes that are visible only in photographs taken at the beginning and the end of the year, side by side. The skin, in the quiet year, is becoming the skin it is going to be. The becoming is, by the quiet year, almost invisible. The becoming is, by the quiet year, the most useful thing happening in the bathroom.

## The decade it changes

There is, in every retinol story, a longer story than the quiet year. The longer story is, almost always, a decade. The decade begins, in most lives, in the late twenties or early thirties. The decade is the decade during which the bottle is, with varying degrees of attention, applied. The decade is, also, the decade during which the skin is, with varying degrees of subtlety, changed.

The retinol is, in this sense, the only ingredient in the bathroom that asks for a decade. The other products ask, in most cases, for a season. The cleanser asks for the morning. The day cream asks for the day. The retinol asks for the decade. The retinol is, in this sense, less an ingredient than a long piece of time. The bottle is, in this sense, less a product than a small private contract with the future.

The decade is, in most lives, the decade during which the face becomes, slowly, the face it is going to be. The retinol is, in most lives, not the only thing that does this. The retinol is, in most lives, one of several quiet collaborators - the sunscreen, the sleep, the water, the years of being, mostly, kind to the body. The retinol is, however, the one that asks for the most explicit patience. The retinol is, in this sense, the ingredient that reminds the user, every other night, that the skin is a long project, and that the long projects are the ones that change the face.

## What the bottle asks for

The retinol bottle asks, of the person holding it, for a particular kind of attention. It asks, first, for the willingness to begin without the guarantee of the result. It asks, second, for the willingness to continue through the three months in which the skin is, visibly, protesting. It asks, third, for the willingness to apply, every other night, a small pearl of an ingredient that does not, in any immediate sense, feel like it is doing anything.

The bottle asks, in this sense, for the kind of attention that most of us are not, in the modern world, very practiced at giving. We are, in most lives, practiced at the short attention. We are practiced at the morning routine and the evening routine and the weekly treatment. We are not, in most lives, practiced at the decadal attention. The decadal attention is, in most lives, the kind of attention reserved for the long friendships and the long marriages and the long careers. The retinol bottle is, in this sense, asking to be treated as one of these.

This is also, perhaps, why the bottle is so often abandoned. The bottle is asking for something that most of us have not, in our wider lives, gotten very good at giving. The bottle is asking for the small private discipline of a long, slow, invisible project. The bottle is asking for the same thing that the daily walk asks for, and the daily meditation asks for, and the long quiet marriage asks for. The bottle is, in this sense, less about the skin and more about the person.

## The patience, eventually, paid

There is, in every retinol story, a moment when the patience is paid. The moment is, almost always, quiet. It is not, in most cases, a moment anyone else notices. It is, in most cases, a moment when the person who has been applying the bottle, every other night, for three or four or five years, looks in the mirror on a Tuesday morning and realizes, in some small way, that the face looking back is, somehow, less tired than it ought to be.

The face is, in this sense, paying back the attention. The face is, in this sense, doing the thing it was always going to do, given enough time. The face has, in this sense, kept up its end of the small private contract. The bottle has, in this sense, done what the bottle was always going to do. The patience has, in this sense, been the most useful ingredient of all.

The bottle is, by this point, the third or fourth bottle of the same ingredient. The dropper has become, in some reliable way, an extension of the hand. The pearl is, by this point, applied without thinking. The routine is, by this point, the routine of someone who has decided, in a small quiet way, that the long projects are the ones worth keeping. The retinol is, in this sense, less a bottle and more a quiet piece of evidence that the long projects do, eventually, change the face.
