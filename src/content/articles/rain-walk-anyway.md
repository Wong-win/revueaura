There is a voice in most of us — older than we are, calmer than we are, slightly more practical than we would like it to be — that looks out of the window on a wet morning and says, with perfectly reasonable conviction, that we should stay inside. The voice is not wrong. The pavement will be slick. The jacket will be damp. The hair, if the hair has been particularly thought about, will not survive. There are a thousand reasons not to go out, and the rain supplies every one of them. And yet, we want to argue, with some feeling, that this is the morning to go out anyway.

What we want to suggest is not heroism. There is no need, here, for gumboots or a waxed-cotton cape or any of the costume that the outdoor magazines will eventually try to sell you, if you let them. The practice is much smaller than that. The practice is simply: open the door, step into the rain, and walk for twenty minutes. Whatever you are wearing will be slightly damp on your return. That is the entire cost.

What you receive in exchange for the damp is a different walk. Not a worse one. We want to be clear about this — not a worse one. A different walk. The city under rain is a city that has changed registers. The traffic is quieter; the horns are angrier but more distant; the air is doing that particular weightlifting it does in October and April, when the barometric pressure has made a decision and is pushing down on your forehead. The shop awnings are out. The dog walkers have put on their clear ponchos. Someone, somewhere on your street, has just opened a window to listen to whether the rain is going to stop.

## the city under a sheet

If you walk for long enough in rain, you notice something that you would never have noticed in clear weather, and which is, in some deep biological sense, the entire reason the rain is interesting. The city is louder and gentler at the same time. The cars are louder — because the drivers, like you, have been forced to slow down, and are compensating with the brake pedal rather than the good sense that a slower speed requires. But the building sounds are gentler, because the rain has laid a soft sheet of white noise over the pavement, the awnings, the chained-down café tables, the small iron staircases that descend into the basements of the old buildings. Everything gets dulled at the edges. You can hear, suddenly, the water itself, doing its patient work along the gutters.

Colours are different in rain. We should warn you, this is one of the things that take people by surprise. A city is, underneath its advertising, mostly grey and beige. Rain makes the grey deeper and the beige richer. The pavement turns the colour of a wet pebble in a river. The shopfronts — the awnings, the lit windows, the small mosaic floors visible through a glass door — pick up intensity. The umbrellas, of course, are the visual exclamation points, and there is a moment, particularly at a busy corner, when thirty of them tip and bob in coordination, and the city looks for one instant like a single breathing organism. You did not see this on a clear afternoon. You can only see it in the rain.

What is also true, and what we think the older cities understand better than we do, is that rain forces a kind of intimacy. You walk closer to people. You share the small overhang of a chemist's awning with a stranger for forty-five seconds. You hold a door for someone who is shaking out a dripping jacket in the doorway of the bakery. These are tiny, unbilled courtesies, and they are the texture of a civic life. In the sun, every pedestrian is a private kingdom. In the rain, we are briefly, slightly, in it together.

## the body's small argument

There is also, we think, something going on below the shoulders. The body has opinions about rain. The body feels the cold drop on the crown of the head and decides, within a few seconds, that it is fine. It is not, in fact, cold. It is wet, which is different. The lungs take in air that has been washed. The face is met with a kind of cool generosity. After about ten minutes, you will probably notice something that surprises you — a small steadying, a small quieting, the kind of slow feeling-down that we associate with the first few minutes of a swim.

This is, we suspect, what the cold-water people know, and what the sauna people know, and what the long-runner-in-bare-snow people know, although none of us are quite at those altitudes yet. Rain at walking pace is the entry-level version of the same practice. You can layer it back into your life without any of the apparatus. No ice. No thermometer. No particular clothing, beyond whatever is reasonably waterproof. You just step into it.

The mind, of course, has an opinion about all of this, and the mind's opinion is usually that the rain walk is a form of punishment. The mind says, why would you do this. The mind says, you could be at home. The mind is correct, in a narrow sense. You could be at home. You could also be at home every other day, and the home, after a few years of this, would become slightly smaller than it is at the moment. The rain walk is one of the ways we keep the home honest. It is the small daily reminder that the world is in fact still out there, at the same temperature as we are, and has not been arranged around our comfort. That reminder is worth a damp cuff.

## a different relationship with the weather

We have, on the whole, a strange relationship with weather in the contemporary city. We have, over the last thirty or forty years, made ourselves almost independent of it. We have heated apartments, refrigerated apartments, dehumidified apartments, air-conditioned subway cars, and a general cultural agreement that the weather is something to be passed through on the way from the lobby to the taxi. We have done this for good reasons, and we are not, in this column, proposing to abandon it. But we are proposing that a small portion of our day should be given back to the weather — to feeling it, to noticing it, to letting it do whatever it is going to do with us for twenty minutes at a time.

The rain walk is the easiest version of this. It requires no special fitness. It requires no particular neighbourhood. It requires nothing more than a willingness to step out the door, which is the only thing that has ever been required, by any contemplative practice, in any century. The weather will meet you where you are. It will not be impressed by your preparations. It will give you exactly what is happening right now, in this precise block, under this cloud, in this air. That is its gift to you.

There is also, we should say, a small calendar in it. After a few months of rain walks, you will find that you have begun to recognise the particular quality of the rain in this city. The October rain is different from the April rain, which is different from the January rain, which is different from the August rain, which is, in many places, hardly rain at all and is more properly called an event. You will know, without thinking about it, that the drizzle that begins around four o'clock in late November tends to continue until the small hours, and that the sudden heavy shower of an August afternoon is usually over by the time you have decided whether to turn around. None of this knowledge makes you a meteorologist. It makes you, more simply, a person who knows the place where they live.

You will come home slightly damp. You will, perhaps, put the kettle on. The towel will be where the towel is. The day will continue. The only thing that will have changed is that you will have walked through a small piece of weather with your own two legs, and the city, after a few years of this, will have stopped being a backdrop and started being a thing you are, briefly, in.

And that, we think, is the practice. Not getting better at walking. Not getting better at being cold. Simply getting better at going out. The door opens. The door closes. The rain is still there. You are still there. The walk is twenty minutes long, and it does not require anything of you except that you do it again next week, and the week after, until one day you realise that the weather, like the city, like the year, like the small green rectangle in the middle of it, has been keeping a rhythm you never quite noticed, and you have been part of it all along.
